# The Week-One Collision
### A two-page brief for higher education on K-12 AI policy, and on whether your institution is ready for the students it trained

## The scene

Two first-year students sit in the same section of the same course. One came through Oklahoma. Her high school taught a five-level AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale adapted from a peer-reviewed academic source. Every assignment carried a posted level. At Level 0 she signed an honesty pledge that no AI touched the work. Above Level 0 she submitted links to her AI chat transcripts with the final product. Her state guidance told her AI detectors produce false positives and should not decide integrity cases. The other student came through Texas. His state issued no classroom AI guidance at all. Whatever rules he met were his district's invention.

The syllabus in front of them says nothing about AI. Their other four syllabi say four different things, or nothing. The Oklahoma student reads the silence as a step down in rigor from her high school. The Texas student guesses. Both are one misreading away from an integrity case.

*Provenance: a composite built from the project's per-state analyses (Oklahoma, Texas) and the cross-state comparison, Implications #2. It is illustrative. State guidance is mostly voluntary and adoption varies by district, so not every Oklahoma graduate had this training. Some did. You cannot tell which by looking.*

## Is higher education ready? No, in three provable senses.

1. **No shared course-level vocabulary.** At least seven K-12 jurisdictions published leveled, per-assignment AI permission scales. Four of them print attribution to the same peer-reviewed source. Most institutions have nothing equivalent, and course policy is set instructor by instructor.
2. **No published positions at the documented collision points.** K-12 wrote down its detector stances, disclosure norms, and appeal paths. Fourteen jurisdictions' guidance cautioned against or discouraged AI-detection software. Most institutions have published none of the three, which is exactly where the first disputes land.
3. **No diagnostic capacity.** Nobody measures what entering students actually know about AI. K-12 did not either. Only Idaho even directs the creation of such an assessment, and it is still pending. An institution that cannot see its incoming variance cannot manage it.

**The exception proves the point.** Tennessee law required every public university to adopt an AI policy covering students, faculty, and staff by July 1, 2025, with a legislative hearing for noncompliant boards. Compliance is unrecorded. A Tennessee public institution without an adopted policy is not merely unready. It is past due.

**The window.** Every K-12 scale instrument dates from 2024-2025. Students entering in fall 2026 spent at most about two and a half years under the earliest of them, and only in adopting districts. Scale-trained cohorts arrive in force roughly 2027 through 2029. The honest framing is not that they are already here. It is that the preparation window is two to four years and closing.

## The thesis, stated precisely

Two independent claims. Neither borrows strength from the other.

- **The merits claim.** K-12 built instruments higher education should adopt because they are good: a peer-reviewed permission scale, disclosure-based integrity, and a defensible rationale for keeping some work AI-free. This is true no matter how many students arrive trained.
- **The tail claim.** A growing minority of entrants carry taught priors: per-assignment permission expectations, distrust of detectors, disclosure habits down to chat links. Those priors will collide with improvised course policies. Because no one can tell which students carry them, the only rational response is entry diagnosis plus one published institutional floor.

## What K-12 built that a faculty member can lift

- **A citable scale.** New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, and North Carolina print attribution to Perkins, M., Furze, L., Roe, J., & MacVaugh, J. (2024), "The AI Assessment Scale (AIAS): A framework for ethical integration of generative AI into educational assessment," *Journal of University Teaching & Learning Practice* 21(6). *[Confirm volume/issue/DOI against the published record before release.]* Washington runs a compatible five-level scale that Puerto Rico translated verbatim. Adopting the AIAS joins an existing chain. It does not invent a local convention.
- **An integrity model that does not need detectors.** Citation of AI use in standard formats, disclosure statements, chat-link transparency, conversation-first handling of suspicion, and an appeal path. Fourteen jurisdictions' guidance cautioned against or discouraged detection software. Louisiana alone recommends it.
- **A reason to keep some work AI-free.** Vermont's two-question test: what skill should students develop here, and will AI help them practice it or perform it for them? Arizona's sharper frame: the question is not whether students offload thinking but which thinking they offload and why. Minnesota's rationale: unbounded AI use bypasses the repetition that builds foundational skill.

## Know, reinforce, teach

| Students likely already know | Reinforce it | Teach it new |
|---|---|---|
| AI use in coursework is governed and misuse is an integrity matter | Disclosure and citation in a named format | Your institution's own permission vocabulary |
| AI should support, not replace, their thinking | Verification before using any AI output | Discipline-specific AI norms |
| AI output can be wrong or biased | The productive-struggle rationale for AI-free work | Honest self-assessment of AI literacy, via an entry diagnostic |
| | Never placing personal data into consumer AI | The appeal path and due process |

## Five moves for the first term

1. **Adopt one institutional permission scale with a genuine AI-free floor** and require every syllabus to use it. Owner: provost.
2. **Publish three positions now**: the detector stance, the data rule, and the appeal path. Owner: provost and general counsel.
3. **Answer six questions on every syllabus**: which scale, what default level, how to disclose, which tools are provided to all, what happens on suspicion, how to appeal.
4. **Run an entry diagnostic** instead of assuming a baseline, and screen both tails: CTE-trained students with real technical skill, and no-baseline students who need the full floor. Owner: first-year experience.
5. **Audit your own syllabi** for one week. Count how many state any AI rule at all. That number is your local readiness evidence. Owner: teaching center.

## Annex: the scale, level by level

As printed in New Mexico's guidance (primary-source verified), adapted from Perkins et al. (2024):

1. **NO AI** — "The assessment is completed entirely without AI assistance... You must not use AI at any point."
2. **AI PLANNING** — AI for brainstorming, outlining, and initial research. The final submission shows how the student developed the ideas.
3. **AI COLLABORATION** — AI may help complete the task, including drafting and feedback. The student critically evaluates and modifies AI output.
4. **FULL AI** — AI may complete elements of the task with the student directing it and demonstrating critical thinking.
5. **AI EXPLORATION** — AI used creatively, with assessments co-designed by student and instructor.

Oklahoma's variant runs 0-4 (NO AI Use; AI-Assisted Idea Generation and Structuring; AI-Assisted Editing; AI for Specified Task Completion; Full AI Use with Human Oversight), each level pairing permission with a disclosure requirement, including links to AI chats. Washington's runs 1-5 (No AI Assistance; AI-Assisted Brainstorming; AI-Supported Drafting; AI-Collaborative Creation; AI as Co-Creator). Pick one numbering and publish it.
