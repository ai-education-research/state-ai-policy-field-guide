# The Gap Between K-12 AI Policy and the College Classroom

*A two-page brief for faculty and academic leaders. Every K-12 claim traces to a 52-jurisdiction comparison of primary state documents. The two companion documents, the full comparison and the readiness guidelines, serve as this brief's appendices. Claims about higher education's own condition are the author's professional observation, stated as such, and testable at your institution in an afternoon.*

## Start with two students in the same first-year section

The first came through an Oklahoma district that adopted the state's AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale. Every assignment in her high school carried a posted level from 0 to 4. She disclosed her AI use, attached links to her chat transcripts, and was taught that AI detectors produce false positives. The second came from Texas, where no state classroom guidance exists. Whatever rules he had were set by his district, if it set any.

Their college syllabus says nothing about AI. She reads the silence as a step down in rigor from her high school. He reads it as permission. The same silence fails both of them, in opposite directions. Multiply this by five concurrent courses with five different unstated policies, and that is the first-year experience this brief exists to name.

## What K-12 built while higher education debated

- **A shared, citable permission vocabulary.** Four states print attribution to the peer-reviewed AI Assessment Scale (Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh, 2024). Washington runs a compatible five-level scale. Puerto Rico teaches a verbatim Spanish translation of it. A university adopting the AIAS joins an existing chain rather than inventing a convention.
- **An integrity model built on disclosure, not detection.** Fourteen jurisdictions caution against relying on AI detectors. Six say do not use them. Eight say never as sole evidence. Eight cite research or bias against multilingual writers. Louisiana alone recommends them. Some of your students can quote their own state's guidance against a detector verdict.
- **A defensible rationale for AI-free work.** Vermont teaches the calculator analogy and a two-question task test. Arizona asks which thinking students offload and why. Minnesota explains that offloading bypasses the repetition that builds foundational skill. This is ready-made language for a syllabus.
- **Law that already reaches universities.** Tennessee required every public university to adopt an AI policy by July 1, 2025, with a legislative hearing for noncompliance. The clock has already run.

## The gap, stated plainly

In most universities, AI policy lives at the level of the individual instructor. It ranges within one institution from a blanket ban to silent permission. It is rarely stated per assignment. This is the author's observation from inside higher education, not a corpus finding. Test it yourself. Collect ten current syllabi at your institution and count the distinct AI policies. Then count the ones that say nothing.

## Is higher education ready

No, in three provable senses. There is no shared course-level vocabulary, while seven K-12 jurisdictions taught one. There are no published institutional positions on the exact collision points K-12 wrote down, detector stance, disclosure format, appeal path. And there is no diagnostic capacity anywhere. Nobody measures what entering students actually know about AI. K-12 does not either, so the profile of any incoming class is guesswork until an institution tests it.

One caution keeps the argument honest. The scale instruments all date from 2024 and 2025, so students socialized under them arrive in force around 2027 to 2029. The urgent claim is not that they are already here. It is that institutions have a two-to-four-year preparation window, and that dual-enrollment students are living the collision this semester.

## What students know, what to reinforce, what to teach

| | |
|---|---|
| **Students likely already know** | AI use in coursework is governed and misuse is an integrity matter. AI should support, not replace, their thinking. AI output can be wrong or biased. |
| **Higher education should reinforce** | Disclosure and citation of AI use in a named format. Verifying AI output before using it. Keeping personal information out of AI tools. Per-assignment permission logic. The productive-struggle rationale for AI-free work. Process transparency instead of detector reliance. |
| **Higher education must newly teach** | This institution's own permission vocabulary. Discipline-specific AI norms. Working in an open multi-tool environment. Honest self-assessment of AI literacy. The institution's appeal process. |

## Five moves for this academic year

1. Adopt one institutional permission scale with a genuine AI-free floor, and require every syllabus to use it. The AIAS gives continuity with what four states already teach.
2. Publish the institution's detector position. Fourteen states already told your students the tools are unreliable.
3. Run an entry diagnostic. It is the only cure for incoming variance no one can see.
4. Give every syllabus six answers. Which scale, what disclosure, which tools, why some work is AI-free, what happens on suspicion, how to appeal.
5. Fund faculty capacity to design AI-integrated assessment. Massachusetts named this the Design Divide, and it is the gap access policies never touch.
