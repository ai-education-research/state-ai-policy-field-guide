# Cross-State K–12 AI Policy Comparison

*Prepared for higher-education faculty and administrators. Evidence base: the 52 completed jurisdiction analysis files in `analysis/`. No outside facts are introduced.*

---

## Scope and Method

**Corpus.** 52 jurisdictions: 50 states + District of Columbia + Puerto Rico. Inventory verified: every jurisdiction appears exactly once, and every jurisdiction has a completed analysis file (`analysis/<Jurisdiction>.md`).

**Evidence rule.** Every claim below traces to one or more jurisdiction files, cited as `(Tennessee.md)`. Those files in turn cite primary documents by filename and page or section. This comparison introduces no facts not present in the state files.

**Labeling conventions used throughout:**
- **[Evidence]** — directly supported by a cited state file.
- **[Synthesis]** — my aggregation of multiple state files into a pattern.
- **[Inference]** — my forward-looking reading about students or institutions, not stated by any state document.

**Two constraints observed strictly, per instruction:**
1. **Uniqueness discipline.** Nothing is called "unique" unless it appears in exactly one state file. Several features that read as distinctive actually recur, and §"Unique State Approaches" separates true single-state features from near-unique ones. Notable corrections made during this pass: the *student right to contest AI-generated grades* appears in **two** files (Alabama.md, Colorado.md); the *EVERY* framework appears in **two** (Alaska.md, North Carolina.md); the *three digital divides* framing appears in **two** (Massachusetts.md, Oregon.md); *environmental cost of AI* appears in **three** (Montana.md, Vermont.md, North Carolina.md); the *Michigan maturity rubric* appears in **two** (Michigan.md, Arkansas.md).
2. **Silence ≠ rejection.** Where a state file records "Not found in the provided documents," that is treated as an evidentiary gap, never as evidence the state opposes the practice. This matters most for the detector dimension, where many states are simply silent.

**Known corpus limitations.** Four jurisdictions have state guidance whose substance was not captured (Delaware.md, Florida.md, Hawaii.md, and partially Maine.md). Several rest on ledger records rather than read primary text (New York.md, Texas.md). These are flagged in-line and in §"Evidence Gaps and Limitations."

## Executive Summary

**Start with the collision, because it is what a faculty reader will actually experience.** In at least seven jurisdictions, students were taught that AI permission is a property of the *assignment* — posted, leveled, and disclosed, sometimes down to submitting links to their AI chat transcripts (North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Washington.md, Wyoming.md). When one of those students reads a college syllabus that says nothing about AI, or bans it globally, the syllabus reads as *less rigorous and less clear than their high school* (§Implications #2). And if that student is disciplined on AI-detector output, they may be able to cite their own state's K-12 guidance against the tool (§Implications #3; §Major Differences #2). That is the week-one scenario this comparison exists to prepare institutions for — while noting honestly that the modal entrant probably does *not* carry the leveled vocabulary (§Expected Graduate AI Readiness), so the collision is a growing-tail problem, not a universal one. **[Synthesis + Inference]**

**Behind that collision sits the central finding.** State education agencies have already built a working governance vocabulary for classroom AI — leveled permission scales, tool-vetting rubrics, disclosure conventions, prohibition lists, and developmental gating. The instruments are concrete, adoptable, and in several cases traceable to a peer-reviewed source a faculty member can cite directly. *(The companion claim that higher education spent the same period without equivalent instruments is [Author framing]: this corpus is K-12-only and contains no survey of university practice — indeed its only direct higher-ed evidence, Tennessee's July 1, 2025 university mandate, cuts the other way.)* **[Synthesis]**

**Six findings matter most to a university reader:**

1. **The scales are not independent inventions.** Four jurisdictions carry printed attribution to Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024), the AI Assessment Scale: New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md, North Carolina.md. Washington.md has a structurally similar five-level scale that is OSPI-original, and Puerto Rico.md is a verbatim Spanish translation of Washington's. Oregon.md recommends the AIAS without authoring one. **[Evidence]**

2. **A near-consensus against *relying on* AI detectors, with one clear outlier.** **Fourteen** jurisdictions caution against or discourage detection software, at two distinct strengths, and a further two are tolerant-with-caveats. Full taxonomy with stated membership criteria is in §Major Differences #2; that taxonomy governs every downstream mention of the detector finding. **Louisiana.md is the sole outlier, affirmatively recommending detection tools.** Roughly half the corpus is silent, which per method is a gap, not opposition. **[Evidence]**

3. **Cognitive offloading is a named, taught concept in K-12 — and one state's treatment is more sophisticated than most university policy.** Arizona.md argues "not all cognitive offloading is harmful" and reframes the question as "not whether students offload thinking, but which thinking they offload and why," proposing a microtask/productive-friction model. Vermont.md gives offloading a dedicated section. Pennsylvania.md makes it an assessed teacher competency. **[Evidence]**

4. **Higher education is already named in state policy — four different ways.** By statute binding universities (Tennessee.md); by assigned governance role (Virginia.md, Montana.md, West Virginia.md); by teacher-preparation instrument (Pennsylvania.md, Georgia.md); and by authorship of the guidance itself (Arizona.md, Florida.md, Kansas.md, Oregon.md, Missouri.md, Nevada.md). Texas.md runs the opposite direction, carving higher education *out* of state AI-governance duties. **[Evidence]**

5. **Mandates govern adoption, not content.** Even the strongest-statute states require that *a policy exist*, not what it says (Tennessee.md, Ohio.md). Consequently within-cohort variance remains high everywhere. **[Synthesis]**

6. **Timing undercuts several apparent baselines.** Alabama's CS/AI graduation requirement begins with the class of 2032 (Alabama.md); Iowa's proposed requirement with 2030-31 (Iowa.md); California's Model Policy posted June 2026 (California.md); Idaho's operative content is still pending agency development (Idaho.md). Near-term entering cohorts predate these. **[Evidence + Inference]**

**Do not publish a single count of "states with AI guidance."** The number depends entirely on whether curated resource pages, CTE course standards, third-party model policies, and uncaptured web guidance count. Publish a sourced range and let the per-state files carry specifics. **[Synthesis]**

## Shared Themes

**1. Human primacy is universal.** Every jurisdiction with substantive captured guidance asserts that AI must not replace teacher judgment or student thinking. The phrasing recurs almost verbatim across unrelated states: "augment human capabilities... never replacing human judgment" (Alaska.md), "AI should never fully replace teachers" (Virginia.md), "AI cannot and should not ever replace human judgement" (Virginia.md), "supplement human thought and decision-making rather than replace it" (Missouri.md), "AI supports human teaching and learning but cannot replace educators" (Wisconsin.md), "cannot replace the value of a student's relationship with a caring educator" (California.md), "AI augments, never replaces, human instruction" (Oklahoma.md). **[Synthesis]**

**2. The anti-ban / anti-unrestricted middle path.** A striking number of states explicitly reject *both* prohibition and free rein, often in near-identical construction: "an outright ban impedes skill and career development, while unrestricted access falls short of needed protections" (Alaska.md); "Providing unrestricted access falls short of providing needed protection. Conversely, an outright ban... impedes the development of essential future workforce skills" (Utah.md); banning is "impractical" and "the biggest risk is doing nothing" (Mississippi.md); "outright bans can shut down opportunities for learning and growth" (Minnesota.md); "Risk of Doing Nothing" as a section heading (Vermont.md). **[Synthesis]** The recurrence of this exact rhetorical shape across states suggests shared source material rather than independent derivation. **[Inference]**

**3. Local control as the default delivery mechanism.** The overwhelming majority of guidance is voluntary and expressly delegates operative rules to districts. Even statutory states mandate policy *adoption* rather than content (Ohio.md, Tennessee.md, Idaho.md). Montana.md grounds this in state law; Missouri.md cites RSMo 171.011 explicitly. **[Evidence]**

**4. Federal-law compliance as the shared floor.** FERPA and COPPA appear in nearly every substantive framework, frequently joined by CIPA, IDEA, PPRA, Section 504, and the ADA (Missouri.md, Nevada.md, Utah.md, Oregon.md, North Dakota.md, Wisconsin.md, Montana.md, Ohio.md). Several states layer their own statutes on top: Montana's Pupil Online Personal Information Protection Act (Montana.md), "Utah FERPA" (Utah.md), Oregon's OSIPA (Oregon.md), Louisiana's R.S. 17:3914 (Louisiana.md). **[Evidence]**

**5. PII must not enter consumer AI systems.** A near-universal, concretely stated rule (Alabama.md, Utah.md, Missouri.md, Montana.md, Nevada.md, Tennessee.md, Vermont.md, West Virginia.md, Wisconsin.md, Oklahoma.md). Vermont.md goes furthest, arguing de-identification is insufficient because student intellectual property still trains the model. **[Evidence]**

**6. Disclosure and citation of AI use as the primary integrity mechanism.** Rather than surveillance, states converge on transparency: cite AI in APA/MLA/Chicago format (Utah.md, West Virginia.md, Ohio.md, North Carolina.md), submit a disclosure statement (Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md), model disclosure as educators (North Carolina.md, West Virginia.md). **[Synthesis]**

**7. Assessment redesign over enforcement.** The recurring instructional answer to AI is to change the task, not to police the tool: require "personal context, original arguments, or original data collection" (Utah.md, West Virginia.md); emphasize "reasoning, analysis, reflection, and process over recall" (Maryland.md); collect process evidence such as drafts and chat links (North Carolina.md); use performance-based and in-person assessment (Rhode Island.md); "assessment of process versus product" (Oregon.md); real-world projects and collaborative co-construction (Mississippi.md). **[Synthesis]** This is the single most transferable body of practice for higher education. **[Inference]**

**8. Equity of access as a stated condition of permission.** Multiple states independently arrive at the same rule: if an assignment permits AI, the tool must be available to all students (Utah.md, West Virginia.md, North Dakota.md, Ohio.md). Free-versus-paid tier disparity is named explicitly (North Dakota.md, Mississippi.md, Missouri.md). **[Evidence]**

**9. Professional development treated as prerequisite, not follow-up.** Universal across substantive frameworks (Wisconsin.md's five pillars, Montana.md's tiered training, Nevada.md's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow, Missouri.md's micro-credentials, Virginia.md's exploration-first strategy). Massachusetts.md names the underlying gap most precisely as the **Design Divide** — teacher capacity to *design* AI-integrated learning, distinct from access and use. **[Evidence]**

## Common but Inconsistently Implemented Approaches

**Leveled student-use scales.** Present and instrument-grade in seven jurisdictions (New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, North Carolina.md, Washington.md, Wyoming.md, Puerto Rico.md, Louisiana.md); recommended-but-not-authored in one (Oregon.md); referenced-but-not-reproduced in one (Alaska.md); explicitly absent in roughly two dozen. The single most consequential inconsistency in the corpus for university planning. **[Synthesis]**

**Developmental grade-band gating.** Strong and reasoned in four (Vermont.md, North Carolina.md, North Dakota.md, Rhode Island.md), which converge independently on an *Awareness → Literacy → Fluency* arc and on restricting open-ended chatbots for young children. Absent entirely from most other frameworks, including several otherwise detailed ones. **[Synthesis]**

**Tool-vetting and procurement rubrics.** Present in varied forms: seven-criterion evaluation rubric (Georgia.md), M.A.Z.E. mnemonic (New Mexico.md), Tool Vetting Framework (Vermont.md), procurement pillar (Alabama.md), AI Software Procurement checklist (Rhode Island.md), Business and IT checklist (North Dakota.md), procurement section (Ohio.md, Montana.md, Missouri.md), resource evaluation tool (Colorado.md). Inconsistent in rigor and in whether they bind. **[Synthesis]**

**Prompt-engineering instruction.** Taught as a named skill in several states with entirely different frameworks: "Five S" (Missouri.md), CRAFT 2.0 with Power-Ups (North Carolina.md), task/format/voice/context (Nevada.md), EVERY (Alaska.md, North Carolina.md). No convergence on a single model. **[Synthesis]**

**Student due-process protections around AI.** Rare but real. Alabama.md requires a process for students to contest AI-generated grades; Colorado.md recommends an appeal process for students to contest both AI grading *and* accusations of AI use. Oklahoma.md achieves a related end by statute, barring AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions. Maryland.md and Illinois.md draw support-versus-high-stakes lines. **[Synthesis]** Two states with an explicit contest/appeal mechanism means this is rare, **not unique**.

**Model-policy templates.** Alabama.md (ALSDE template), Ohio.md (fill-in-the-blank board policy), California.md (2026 Model Policy), Tennessee.md (TSBA 4.214), South Dakota.md (ASBSD, uncaptured), Montana.md (MTSBA, uncaptured). Widely used mechanism, inconsistently retrievable. **[Synthesis]**

**Deepfake / non-consensual imagery prohibitions.** Present in Ohio.md (tied to Braden's Law), Oklahoma.md, Utah.md, West Virginia.md, Montana.md (manipulation/harmful content), North Carolina.md (deepfakes and cyberbullying). Common in the newer documents, absent from earlier ones. **[Synthesis]**

**AI-based student safety monitoring.** Described approvingly in Utah.md (flagging distress in written communications). Litigated adversarially in Kansas.md (the Lawrence USD 497 Gaggle lawsuit raising First and Fourth Amendment claims). The corpus contains no reconciliation of these two postures. **[Synthesis]**

## Major Differences

**1. Legal force ranges from binding statute to complete agency silence.** At one pole, Tennessee.md's Public Chapter 550 binds public universities, school boards, and charter schools with a legislative-hearing backstop for noncompliance. At the other, Nebraska.md and South Dakota.md document state agencies with no AI content at all. **[Evidence]**

**2. Detector posture is the sharpest values split. Taxonomy with stated criteria (governs all downstream mentions):**

| Tier | Criterion | Jurisdictions | Count |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Discourage / refrain** | Guidance tells educators not to use detection software, or to eliminate reliance on it | Washington.md ("highly recommended that educators do not utilize"), Massachusetts.md ("discourage"/"eliminate reliance"), West Virginia.md ("refrain from using"), Vermont.md ("do not rely"), Colorado.md ("already failing to be helpful"), Maryland.md ("not endorsed"; "unreliable and inequitable") | 6 |
| **B. Not-sole-evidence caution** | Detectors may be used but must not be the sole or decisive basis for an integrity finding | Illinois.md ("one data point, not proof"), Indiana.md ("reconsider as sole source"), California.md ("not sole evidence"), North Carolina.md ("never the only factor"), Oklahoma.md ("not always reliable; false positives"), Arizona.md ("never sole source"), New Mexico.md ("low reliability"), Mississippi.md ("accuracy + EL false positives") | 8 |
| **C. Tolerant with caveats** | Use permitted or suggested, with a reliability warning attached | Oregon.md ("consider using... with an understanding"), Utah.md ("use caution... one of several tools") | 2 |
| **D. Recommends** | Detection tools affirmatively recommended | **Louisiana.md** (Turnitin, Grammarly, GoGuardian) | 1 |
| **E. Silent / not captured** | No position in the captured documents — a gap, not opposition | remainder of the corpus | ~35 |

**Tiers A + B = 14 jurisdictions cautioning against reliance.** This corrects an earlier count of 13, which omitted Mississippi.md despite its file recording an English-learner false-positive warning that satisfies the Tier-B criterion. **A named bias or research rationale appears in eight files only** — Washington.md (cites Vanderbilt and Stanford), Arizona.md, New Mexico.md, North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Maryland.md, Massachusetts.md, Mississippi.md — and must not be attributed to Tier-B states whose files contain no such rationale (Illinois.md, Indiana.md, California.md). **[Evidence]**

**3. Whether higher education is bound, partnered, or excluded.** Tennessee.md binds universities by law. Virginia.md assigns roles to SCHEV, VCCS, and every public college. Maryland.md statutorily tasks a four-year institution with certifying K-12 AI tools. New Jersey.md would require institutions to offer AI degree programs (pending). **Texas.md carves higher education out of its AI-governance duties entirely.** These are opposite policy choices about the same sector. **[Evidence]**

**4. Instrument-rich versus principle-only.** Georgia.md supplies three liftable artifacts (redlight system, high-stakes table, evaluation rubric). Michigan.md supplies a full maturity matrix. Washington.md supplies a scale plus an educator matrix keyed to real assignment types. Against these, Connecticut.md, Minnesota.md, Kentucky.md, and Virginia.md are deliberately principle-only — Montana.md is explicit that external frameworks "should be treated as adaptable references, rather than mandates." **[Synthesis]**

**5. Prescriptiveness about tools.** Puerto Rico.md mandates a single platform (MS Copilot) for students 13+ and prohibits unsupervised use — the most prescriptive posture in the corpus, a function of operating as one unified territory system. Most states name no tools at all; Georgia.md and Michigan.md explicitly decline to endorse products. **[Evidence]**

**6. Whether AI is a special case or an ordinary technology.** Kentucky.md deliberately normalizes AI, arguing that "treating it differently than other emerging technologies can hinder its effective integration and create unnecessary anxieties." Indiana.md recommends folding AI into existing policies rather than writing a standalone one, as does Ohio.md structurally. Against this, Idaho.md creates an entire new statutory chapter for it. **[Evidence]**

**7. Effective dates and who is actually covered.** Alabama.md's requirement starts with the class of 2032; Iowa.md's proposed requirement with 2030-31; Oklahoma.md's district policies before 2027-28; Ohio.md's adoption deadline July 1, 2026; Maryland.md's AI literacy in standards by June 1, 2027. Several "mandate states" therefore have no covered graduates yet. **[Evidence + Inference]**

**8. Scope boundaries differ materially.** North Carolina.md covers **PK-13** and names educators in its top band. Wisconsin.md extends to **public libraries and adult community learners**. Virginia.md covers **all education including higher education**. Kansas.md's KU framework is **Pre-K-20**. Most other states stop at grade 12. **[Evidence]**

## Policy Archetypes

Grouped by observable characteristics in the state files. **Criteria are stated for each.** States frequently satisfy more than one archetype; overlaps and misfits are named. **[Synthesis]**

### A. Instrument Builders
**Criterion:** publishes a leveled, assignment-level student AI-use scale as an adoptable artifact.
New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, North Carolina.md, Washington.md, Wyoming.md, Puerto Rico.md, Louisiana.md.
*Sub-split by lineage:* AIAS-attributed (New Mexico, Oklahoma, North Carolina, Wyoming); OSPI-original and its translation (Washington, Puerto Rico); SAMR-aligned (Louisiana).
*Misfit:* Louisiana belongs here on instrument design but is the corpus's lone detector advocate, placing it outside the group's shared integrity philosophy.

### B. Principles Charters
**Criterion:** organizes around named guiding principles with no leveled scale.
Alaska.md (7 principles), Montana.md (5), Massachusetts.md (5), Minnesota.md (5), Nevada.md (7 STELLAR), West Virginia.md (7), Utah.md (8), Virginia.md (6), Illinois.md (4 tenets), Kentucky.md (7 + 3 paradigms), Indiana.md, Colorado.md (3 domains).
*Note:* Utah and Virginia also carry statutory elements, so they double-count with Archetype D.

### C. Governance and Compliance Instruments
**Criterion:** primary orientation is administrative — procurement, risk management, board policy, data governance — rather than classroom pedagogy.
Alabama.md (8 pillars on NIST AI RMF), Ohio.md (board-policy template), Maryland.md (coordinator + tool certification), Missouri.md (LEA policy workbook), North Dakota.md (five role-based checklists), Michigan.md (maturity rubric), Oregon.md (2025 policy-development workbook).
*Misfit:* Michigan is a self-assessment rubric authored by a nonprofit and MDE-endorsed, so its authority differs from the rest of the group.

### D. Statutory Mandate States
**Criterion:** enacted law imposing an AI-related requirement on schools, universities, or curriculum.
Tennessee.md (universities + K-12 policy adoption), Oklahoma.md (use guardrails + policy adoption), Ohio.md (policy adoption by 7/1/2026), Idaho.md (framework, standards, assessment, PD), Utah.md (AI literacy in required gr. 7-8 course), Maryland.md (policy + coordinator + tool certification), Alabama.md (CS/AI graduation credit, class of 2032).
*Pending, not enacted:* New Jersey.md, Iowa.md, South Carolina.md, New York.md, West Virginia.md. *Enacted but text unverified:* Virginia.md.

## Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

This is the dimension where K-12 policy most directly anticipates the higher-education debate, and where the corpus is most uneven.

### Tier 1 — Explicit, research-grounded treatment

**Arizona.md** is the most sophisticated in the corpus and is more nuanced than much university policy. Its "Cognitive Offloading" subsection argues **"not all cognitive offloading is harmful"** and reframes the governing question as **"not whether students offload thinking, but which thinking they offload and why."** It cites research on both sides — retention loss where students "complete entire tasks from beginning to end" (Kosmyna et al., 2025) versus benefit from "purposefully-designed AI tutors" — and proposes a **microtask / productive-friction model** in which teachers decide "which cognitive muscles to focus on and which ones rest," designing tasks where students explain their reasoning. **[Evidence]**

**Vermont.md** gives offloading a dedicated section, citing Ethan Mollick's "Against Brain Damage," using a calculator-and-multiplication analogy, and supplying a two-question teacher test: *what skill should students develop, and will AI help them practice it or perform it for them?* It states that **"Teachers should feel empowered to say when AI usage is inappropriate."** **[Evidence]**

**Minnesota.md** states the mechanism plainly: students may "rely on AI to complete tasks they should be practicing themselves, bypassing... the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity," and directs guidelines to "distinguish between AI as a learning aid versus a replacement for thinking." **[Evidence]**

**Pennsylvania.md** converts the concern into an **assessed teacher competency**: endorsement candidates must analyze "how cognitive offloading to AI systems influences attention, memory, and problem-solving," alongside effects on socialization and belonging. **[Evidence]**

**Utah.md** names agentic systems specifically, requiring that teachers and students understand how "any AI system, that offloads decision making and/or cognitive effort in the classroom" operates, and lists over-reliance as a **prohibited use**. **[Evidence]**

### Tier 2 — Structural protection via instrument design

Every leveled scale in the corpus reserves an AI-free floor whose stated purpose is preserving unaided cognition. The wording varies by state but the design is identical: "rely solely on their own knowledge, understanding, and skills" (Oklahoma.md, verbatim; Wyoming.md reads "rely entirely," North Carolina.md "own thinking, knowledge, and skills," Washington.md "knowledge and skills," New Mexico.md "entirely without AI assistance," Puerto Rico.md "depend solely"), and AI-Prohibited for tasks "designed to evaluate students' core knowledge and skills" (Louisiana.md). Georgia.md's RED level performs the same function without a full scale. **[Synthesis]** This is the corpus's most widely replicated offloading control, and it is directly portable to a syllabus. **[Inference]**

### Tier 3 — Developmental protection

The gating states protect cognition by delaying access: premature AI use "may bypass the essential struggle needed to develop these core competencies" (Vermont.md); over-reliance "can short-circuit students' development of problem-solving skills" (North Dakota.md); avoid over-reliance "as this will impede students' development of problem-solving skills" (Rhode Island.md); "young children are not developmentally ready to critically evaluate AI outputs" (North Carolina.md). **[Synthesis]**

### Tier 4 — Principle-level protection

Human-in-the-loop and agency principles function as general offloading guards without naming the mechanism: Alaska.md (all AI use begins with human inquiry, ends with human reflection), Washington.md (same bookending), Kentucky.md (three paradigms tracking how much cognitive agency stays with the learner; HITL tenet), Maryland.md (AI-resilient assessment plus a caution against educator over-trust of automated recommendations, citing Goddard et al., 2012), Massachusetts.md ("reinforce learning, not short-circuit it"; "move beyond detection and toward deeper engagement with thinking"), Illinois.md ("metacognitive challenge while preserving originality and student voice"), Indiana.md (AI as scaffold that "open[s] up time for higher level thinking"), Montana.md and Rhode Island.md (productive struggle named), Wisconsin.md (human irreplaceability plus media-literacy critique), Mississippi.md (scaffolding-fade: "gradually reducing students' reliance on these tools"). **[Synthesis]**

### Tier 5 — Not addressed in captured documents
Arkansas.md, Delaware.md, Florida.md, Hawaii.md, Kansas.md, Maine.md, Nebraska.md, New Hampshire.md, South Carolina.md, South Dakota.md, Texas.md, New York.md, Iowa.md. Per method, **silence is a gap, not a rejection.** **[Evidence]**

**Synthesis for a faculty audience.** K-12 has already produced the two things university AI policy most often lacks: a defensible reason why some assessments must remain unaided (Arizona's productive-friction model, Vermont's calculator analogy, Minnesota's repetition argument), and a mechanism for operationalizing it (the AI-free floor on a per-assignment scale). **[Inference]**

## Expected Graduate AI Readiness

Derived from the state files, with the universal caveat that most guidance is voluntary and district-implemented, so these are **ceilings of possibility, not guarantees.** **[Inference throughout this section]**

### Likely high-variance dimensions

**AI-permission vocabulary.** Students from New Mexico, Oklahoma, North Carolina, Washington, Wyoming, Puerto Rico, and Louisiana may recognize a leveled, per-assignment permission structure and the expectation that a syllabus states which level applies (those state files). Students from the roughly two dozen explicit-no-scale states and the absence states will not. **A single classroom may contain both.** **[Inference]**

**Disclosure habits.** Oklahoma.md and Wyoming.md students may be accustomed to submitting **links to their AI chat transcripts** with work — a documentation practice most universities do not request. North Carolina.md students may expect to submit process evidence (outlines, drafts, chat links). Utah.md, West Virginia.md, and Ohio.md students may expect to cite AI in APA/MLA/Chicago format. **[Inference]**

**Detector expectations.** Students from the thirteen discouraging states may arrive actively distrusting detection software, in several cases having been taught the specific research basis and the equity argument (Washington.md, Arizona.md, Maryland.md, Massachusetts.md, New Mexico.md, North Carolina.md). **Louisiana.md students may arrive expecting detectors as normal practice.** Students from silent states have unknown priors. **[Inference]**

**Tool familiarity.** Puerto Rico.md students likely used a single institutional platform (MS Copilot) under supervision — an open, multi-tool campus environment will be a substantial shift. North Carolina.md and Wisconsin.md students may have used hands-on model-building tools (Teachable Machine, Machine Learning for Kids). South Carolina.md CTE completers may have real technical AI/ML skills (Python, model building) that their peers entirely lack. Nebraska.md, South Dakota.md, and Texas.md students have no state-shaped tool baseline. **[Inference]**

**Independent-learning and metacognitive skill.** Strongest expected signal from the gating and offloading states, where students were taught to critique AI output from middle school (North Dakota.md, North Carolina.md, Rhode Island.md, Vermont.md) and to reflect on their own AI use (Mississippi.md, Puerto Rico.md's metacognition rubric, Illinois.md's metacognitive framing). **[Inference]**

### Structural facts that constrain any readiness assumption

- **Youngest cohorts had deliberately less AI exposure**, not more, in the gating states — formal fluency work concentrates in grades 9+ (Vermont.md, North Carolina.md). **[Evidence + Inference]**
- **Several statutory baselines have no graduates yet**: class of 2032 (Alabama.md), 2030-31 if enacted (Iowa.md), pending SDE content (Idaho.md), June 2026 model policy (California.md), 2027-28 district policies (Oklahoma.md). **[Evidence]**
- **Access-driven variance is structural in some states.** Montana.md flags rural connectivity, multi-role educators, and budget limits as barriers its own guidance may not overcome; Nevada.md, Iowa.md, and North Dakota.md raise comparable equity concerns. Absence of exposure in these cohorts should be expected, not treated as student deficiency. **[Evidence + Inference]**
- **Formal assessment of AI literacy is rare.** Idaho.md is the one jurisdiction whose statute directs the development of **assessment guidelines to evaluate student understanding of GenAI** — meaning future Idaho graduates might arrive having been formally evaluated on AI literacy. **[Evidence]**

### A realistic composite

The likeliest incoming profile is a student who: was governed by a district acceptable-use policy rather than a state scale; was told AI should support rather than replace their thinking; may or may not have been taught to cite AI; probably was not taught a leveled permission vocabulary; and, if from one of the fourteen detector-cautioning states, may bring skepticism of AI detectors. **Expect the range within any single cohort to exceed the range between states.** **[Inference]**

## Implications for Higher Education

**1. A shared K-16 vocabulary already exists and is peer-reviewed.** Because New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md, and North Carolina.md all attribute their scales to Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024), a university adopting the AIAS is not inventing a local convention — it is joining an existing chain and can cite the scholarship rather than a state PDF. Washington.md and Puerto Rico.md add structurally compatible five-level scales. **[Synthesis]**

**2. The per-assignment expectation is already set.** Students from scale states experienced AI permission as a property of the *task*, often posted and explicitly taught (North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Washington.md). Against that background, a blanket institutional ban or a silent syllabus reads as *less* rigorous and less clear than high school. **[Inference]**

**3. Detector reliance is a defensibility problem, not merely an accuracy problem.** Fourteen jurisdictions' guidance advised districts against relying on detection software (§Major Differences #2, Tiers A and B), and **eight of those files carry a named bias or research rationale** — Washington.md (Vanderbilt and Stanford), Arizona.md, New Mexico.md, North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Maryland.md, Massachusetts.md, Mississippi.md — several specifically citing false positives against multilingual writers. Note the limit: this is *district-facing guidance*, and the state files themselves hedge student exposure as "may arrive" and "from adopting districts," so it does not establish that students were uniformly taught it. Even so, an institution disciplining a student on detector output should expect that some students can cite their own state's K-12 guidance against the tool. **[Evidence for the guidance; Inference for the student-side collision]**

**4. Several institutions are already named in state policy — possibly without knowing it.** Tennessee.md requires public universities to have adopted AI policies by July 1, 2025, with a legislative-hearing consequence for noncompliance. Maryland.md tasks Morgan State or another four-year institution with certifying K-12 AI tools. Virginia.md assigns roles to SCHEV, VCCS, and "every public college and university." Montana.md names the Board of Regents a Constitutional Player. West Virginia.md seats the higher-education Chancellor ex officio on the board that issued the K-12 guidance. Ohio.md and Wyoming.md invite postsecondary representatives onto district AI workgroups. **Administrators should check their own state's file.** **[Evidence]**

**5. Teacher-preparation programs face the most concrete new obligations.** Pennsylvania.md's endorsement framework specifies six competency domains, a 12-credit structure, and a PK-12 capstone for university teacher-prep programs. Georgia.md involves educator preparation providers. Iowa.md's pending bills invite CS teacher-preparation institutions. Montana.md charges the University System with preparing AI-capable teachers. **[Evidence]**

**6. Instruments built for districts transfer to institutions with minimal editing.** Michigan.md's three-stage, eight-domain maturity rubric works as a college AI-readiness self-assessment by substituting "institution" for "district" — the state file says so explicitly. Georgia.md's high-stakes/non-high-stakes table models what should never be delegated to AI (subjective grading, evaluations, individualized decisions). Colorado.md's "If and How Checklist" and Kentucky.md's three paradigms map onto course design. **[Evidence + Inference]**

**7. Student due-process expectations may arrive with the students.** Alabama.md guarantees a process to contest AI-generated grades; Colorado.md recommends appeal rights covering both AI grading and accusations of AI use; Oklahoma.md bars AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions by statute. Students from these states may reasonably expect comparable protections at university. **[Inference]**

**8. AI surveillance is legally contested, and campuses use the same tool categories.** Kansas.md documents the Lawrence USD 497 Gaggle litigation raising First and Fourth Amendment and state student-publications claims over AI monitoring of student communications. Utah.md, by contrast, describes AI distress-monitoring approvingly. Institutions running proctoring, wellness-flagging, or communications monitoring are operating in this unresolved space. **[Evidence + Inference]**

**9. Faculty capacity is the named bottleneck.** Massachusetts.md's **Design Divide** — "gaps in teacher training on designing meaningful AI-based learning experiences" — identifies precisely the gap most institutions have not addressed, having focused on access and acceptable use. Oregon.md uses the same three-divide framing. **[Evidence]**

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

Grounded in what the state files actually recommend, so that institutional practice extends rather than contradicts what students were taught. **[Synthesis + Inference]**

### AI access and equity
- **Guarantee tool parity.** Adopt the rule four states independently reached: if an assignment permits AI, the institution supplies the tool to every student (Utah.md, West Virginia.md, North Dakota.md, Ohio.md). Audit free-versus-paid tier gaps (North Dakota.md, Mississippi.md).
- **Verify accessibility features** — text-to-speech, translation, adaptive interfaces — against stated standards (Montana.md cites WCAG and Section 508; New Mexico.md writes accessibility into procurement).
- **Do not penalize AI-supported accommodations.** Rhode Island.md directs exactly this for Differently-Abled Students; Washington.md empowers special-education students to use AI for access.

### Onboarding
- **Assume no common baseline; diagnose instead.** Use New Mexico.md's three-part AI-literacy definition (understand how AI works, evaluate risks, apply thoughtfully) or Nevada.md's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow as a ready entry rubric.
- **Screen for the two tails.** South Carolina.md CTE completers may hold real technical AI/ML skills; students from absence states (Nebraska.md, South Dakota.md, Texas.md) may have none. Place accordingly.
- **Bridge vocabulary explicitly** for Puerto Rico.md students, whose AI vocabulary (SENSE, the five niveles) is Spanish, and whose experience was single-platform and supervised.

### Faculty development
- **Treat the Design Divide as the priority** (Massachusetts.md, Oregon.md): fund faculty capacity to *design* AI-integrated assessment, not just to set acceptable-use rules.
- **Adopt exploration-first PD.** Virginia.md's strategy is hands-on structured experimentation before policy discussion; Montana.md and Nevada.md recommend low-stakes practice and cross-institution communities of practice.
- **Import ready pedagogy.** Arizona.md's microtask/productive-friction design, Vermont.md's two-question task test, New Mexico.md's teaching-with-versus-about distinction and interrogation prompts, Wisconsin.md's five training pillars.
- **Counter automation bias in faculty**, which Maryland.md names directly, citing the documented tendency to over-trust automated recommendations.

### Assessment
- **State AI permission per assignment, on a named scale.** Use the AIAS for continuity with New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md, and North Carolina.md students, and structural compatibility with Washington.md and Puerto Rico.md.
- **Preserve a genuine AI-free floor** where the learning goal is core-skill demonstration — the one control every scale state implemented.
- **Adopt AI-resilient design** rather than detection: reasoning, analysis, reflection and process over recall, with "visible evidence of student thinking" (Maryland.md); personal context, original argument, original data (Utah.md, West Virginia.md); performance-based and in-person demonstration (Rhode Island.md); process artifacts including drafts and chat links (North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md).
- **Keep AI out of high-stakes judgment.** Follow Georgia.md's high-stakes table, Oklahoma.md's statutory bar, Illinois.md's and Maryland.md's support-versus-high-stakes lines, and Utah.md's rule that "AI will not be solely responsible for grading."

### Governance
- **Check your statutory position first.** Tennessee.md (universities bound), Maryland.md (tool-certification role), Virginia.md (assigned roles), Montana.md and West Virginia.md (board-level presence), Texas.md (explicitly exempt), New Jersey.md (pending program mandate).
- **Take the seats offered.** Ohio.md and Wyoming.md invite postsecondary representatives onto district AI workgroups — a direct channel for aligning entry expectations with feeder districts.
- **Name an accountable owner.** Maryland.md requires a central-office AI coordinator; Missouri.md and Ohio.md recommend an AI coordinator role.
- **Build a review cycle, not a document.** Montana.md, Missouri.md, Ohio.md, and North Carolina.md all operate cyclically; North Carolina.md maintains a public update log.
- **Adopt an institutional readiness self-assessment.** Michigan.md's rubric is explicitly transferable; Rhode Island.md and North Dakota.md supply getting-started and procurement checklists.

### Student support and due process
- **Create an appeal path** covering both AI-assisted grading and allegations of undisclosed AI use, following Alabama.md and Colorado.md.
- **Handle suspicion by conversation, not verdict.** Washington.md recommends a conversation anchored to the published matrix; North Carolina.md frames it as "a teachable moment... rather than a 'gotcha'"; Vermont.md treats integrity violations as potentially "a cry for help."
- **Publish your detector position explicitly.** Given that fourteen states' guidance cautioned against detector reliance and Louisiana.md recommended the tools, no shared prior can be assumed in either direction.

### Promoting deeper learning over offloading
- **Adopt the "which thinking, and why" frame** (Arizona.md) rather than a blanket permit/ban binary — it is the most defensible formulation in the corpus and it survives contact with legitimate accessibility uses.
- **Protect productive struggle by design**, naming it as Montana.md, Rhode Island.md, and Vermont.md do, and explaining the repetition rationale as Minnesota.md does.
- **Fade scaffolds deliberately.** Mississippi.md's principle — "gradually reducing students' reliance on these tools" — and North Carolina.md's "start the year with Level 0 or 1... progress to Levels 3-4" give a semester-scale model.
- **Require human verification as a standing obligation** (Alaska.md's and North Carolina.md's EVERY framework ending in "You are responsible"; Missouri.md's and Montana.md's oversight requirements).

## Evidence Gaps and Limitations

**1. Voluntariness limits every student-level claim.** Most guidance is non-binding and district-implemented. No jurisdiction file contains per-district adoption data. Every statement about what graduates experienced is a ceiling of possibility. **[Evidence]**

**2. Five jurisdictions have uncaptured or partially captured substance.** Delaware.md (web-only pages), Florida.md (university-hosted Google Docs), Hawaii.md (login-gated), Maine.md (interactive toolkit partially captured), New York.md (NYC district guidance JS-rendered). These cannot be placed in substantive archetypes, and their students' experience cannot be characterized.

**3. Two jurisdictions rest on ledger records rather than read primary text.** New York.md and Texas.md have no downloaded source files; all statutory characterizations derive from provenance-ledger summaries.

**4. Four third-party substitute documents are non-retrievable.** The New Hampshire coalition PDF (New Hampshire.md), the ASBSD model policy (South Dakota.md), the KU Pre-K-20 framework (Kansas.md), and MTSBA model policies (Montana.md). In each case the document that actually shaped district practice could not be read.

**5. Enacted-but-unverified statutory text.** Virginia.md's HB 1186 and SB 394 are enacted effective July 1, 2026, but Virginia LIS renders bill text client-side; substance rests on secondary sources.

**6. Absence claims are bounded by search, not certified.** Nebraska.md, South Dakota.md, Texas.md, and others document thorough-for-tooling null results. A newly posted or locally hosted document could exist outside what was fetched.

**7. Living documents are moving targets.** North Carolina.md (continuously updated Google Doc, captured 2/25/2026), Wisconsin.md ("regularly updated" Word file, June 2024 version), Montana.md (biannual review), West Virginia.md (versioned). Analyses reflect the captured version only.

**8. Detector-stance silence is systematically ambiguous.** Roughly half the corpus takes no position. Per method these are gaps; they must not be reported as neutrality or as opposition.

**9. Some documents disclose AI assistance in their own drafting.** Alaska.md, Illinois.md, and North Carolina.md each record this. It bears on how much independent authority to assign their prose. **[Evidence]**

**10. Two jurisdictions are not states.** District of Columbia.md and Puerto Rico.md operate under different governance structures — Puerto Rico as a single unified territory system, DC with a large uncovered charter sector — so their directive force is not comparable to state guidance.

Corrections identified during this pass** and recorded in the per-state files, pending fold-back into `VERIFICATION.md` and `data.js`: the Washington five-level scale reversal (Washington.md); Puerto Rico's actual level names (Puerto Rico.md); Louisiana's single SAMR model (Louisiana.md); New Jersey's stronger-than-recorded pending university mandate (New Jersey.md); Utah SB 322 failed and the framework is text-extractable (Utah.md); Ohio's orphaned AI Toolkit lead document (Ohio.md); Georgia's unverifiable "SB 179" (Georgia.md); Texas's higher-ed-specific rather than blanket carve-out (Texas.md); and four consumer-protection statutes mis-scoped as school law (Washington.md, Oregon.md, Tennessee.md, Vermont.md).

## Conclusion

Across 52 jurisdictions, K-12 policy has produced what higher education still largely lacks: **a shared, citable vocabulary for assignment-level AI permission; a defensible pedagogical rationale for protecting unaided cognition; and an integrity model that does not depend on unreliable detection.** The vocabulary traces to a single 2024 academic source adapted by at least four states, which means a university adopting it joins an existing chain rather than inventing a local convention (New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md, North Carolina.md). **[Synthesis]**

The corpus also shows the limits of that achievement honestly. Most guidance is voluntary, several statutory baselines have no graduates yet, five jurisdictions' substance could not be captured, and nine have no state framework at all. **Within-cohort variance will exceed between-state variance for the foreseeable future**, which argues for a common baseline established at entry rather than assumed competence. **[Synthesis + Inference]**

For an institution, three moves follow directly from the evidence. **First, check whether your state already names you** — seven jurisdictions assign higher education a role, obligation, or exemption, and Tennessee binds public universities by law. **Second, adopt a per-assignment permission scale with a genuine AI-free floor**, the one control every scale-building state implemented and the one that makes "no AI on this task" defensible rather than arbitrary. **Third, invest in the Design Divide** — the faculty capacity to design AI-integrated assessment — which Massachusetts.md and Oregon.md identify as the gap that access policies and acceptable-use rules leave untouched. **[Inference]**

The K-12 record does not tell universities what to do. It does remove the excuse that no one has worked out how to do it.
