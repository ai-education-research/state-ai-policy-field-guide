**Nature of the documents (read before everything below):** STELLAR is explicitly a guidance and support document, not a rulebook. It says NDE is "dedicated to assisting school districts and charter schools known as Local Education Agencies (LEAs) in navigating this evolving landscape" (p.6) and is "designed to help LEAs develop policies and provide practical guidance" (p.7). **Nothing in it is a statewide mandate on districts.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. The companion Ethics Statement is a one-page graphical poster and adds no extractable policy text beyond STELLAR itself.

## Executive Summary

Nevada's guidance is a recommendation-level framework organized around the mnemonic STELLAR, seven principle chapters: Security, Transparency, Empowerment, Learning, Leadership, Achievement, and Responsible Use (TOC, pp.9-36). It was produced by NDE with the Nevada AI Alliance, a stakeholder body that ran research sessions and town halls from January to May 2024, and the document is unusually multi-audience, closing with distinct Best Practices pages for Districts, Administrators, Teachers, Parents, and Students (pp.41-46).

It has no leveled student-use permission scale. Its closest structural device is a four-area learning cycle, Understand, Apply, Integrate, Grow (p.24), which is a professional-learning progression for all stakeholders rather than an assignment-level AI-permission ladder. Its prompt-craft guidance names four elements, task, format, voice, and context (p.25). It does not adapt Perkins et al. or any external scale as its own instrument. Nevada's distinctive local anchor is Personalized Competency-Based Learning (PCBL), the state's existing mastery-based model, into which AI is woven (p.24).

For a higher-education reader, three facts stand out. First, higher education helped author it. The Executive Steering Committee names faculty from the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, the College of Southern Nevada, and Western Nevada College (pp.51). Second, Nevada states its cognitive-offloading position bluntly in its literacy goals: "Focus on critical thinking and reasoning, emphasizing the process, not the result" and "Technology should serve learning, not define it... AI is a support for learning, not the foundation" (p.33). Third, the whole document is framed PreK-12 with a workforce and "future-ready" horizon.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI amplifies, it does not substitute for, human intelligence.** The overview headline reads: "Artificial intelligence is not a substitute for human intelligence; it is a tool to amplify human creativity and ingenuity" (p.5). In the classroom, "AI should be used to empower educators, not to substitute their expertise and human connection" (p.6). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Seven STELLAR principles anchor the guidance:** Security, Transparency, Empowerment, Learning, Leadership, Achievement, Responsible Use (pp.9-36). *[Recommendation]*
- **The overarching goal is equitable, accessible, thoughtfully integrated AI, PreK-12** (p.6). Equity recurs as a throughline: "AI should help close equity gaps, not widen them" (p.27). *[Recommendation]*
- **It grows from stakeholder consultation.** The Nevada AI Alliance's 2024 research sessions and town halls "shaped this guide" (p.7). *[Framing]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The document serves "teachers, students, parents, administrators, and educational leaders" (p.7) and provides tailored Best Practices for each group (pp.41-46). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is PreK-12, tied explicitly to Nevada's PCBL model and to "Portrait of a Nevada Learner" (pp.24, references). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Permitted use is delegated to LEAs, which the document urges to define "which AI tools are available, when and how they should be used, whether for assignments, assessments, or research, and setting clear expectations for ethical and appropriate use" (p.26). *[Recommendation / not a mandate]*
- The Learning chapter recommends teachers "use a rubric to ensure responsible and ethical integration in assignments" (p.42), but supplies no standardized rubric. *[Recommendation]*
- Age restrictions are a stated gate: "Always check and follow age restrictions for any AI tools or resources" (p.15). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Security is the first STELLAR principle and the most detailed.** It requires FERPA compliance, PII protection, data minimization ("Limiting data collection to only what is essential"), vendor scrutiny, and regular security assessments (pp.12-14). A federal-law box lists FERPA, COPPA, CIPA, and PPRA as tools that "meet these important requirements," and notes IDEA, ESSA, and civil-rights law implications (p.15). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level law]*
- **Transparency is its own principle:** "teachers, students, and parents need to understand how AI systems make decisions and influence learning" (p.6). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity and accessibility** are woven throughout, with assistive tools (text-to-speech, real-time translation) named as inclusivity supports (pp.27, 35). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity** appears in the Responsible Use chapter. "Students need to clearly understand what counts as plagiarism and what is considered responsible AI use," supported by "clear integrity policies and honor codes that define how AI can and should be used" (p.38). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** the document does not endorse or discuss AI-detection software. Its plagiarism plan is process-based, revise assignments with clear skill guidelines, "track AI usage, collect regular writing samples to identify student styles, and enforce academic integrity policies" (p.42). *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Professional learning is continuous and structured** by the "Grow" area, webinars, peer networks, conferences, and hands-on workshops (p.26). *[Recommendation]*
- **Curriculum integration runs through PCBL.** Embedding AI into Personalized Competency-Based Learning lets students "advance upon demonstrating mastery... regardless of time, place, or pace" while building "critical thinking, adaptability, leadership, and problem-solving" (p.24). *[Recommendation]*
- **The Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle** is the professional-learning spine: build an AI foundation, put AI into action, embed it into teaching and learning, and foster continuous growth (pp.24-27). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Achievement lists five AI-literacy goals for Nevada** (p.33): set expectations and awareness; focus on process over result; use generative AI to enhance practice; support diverse learners; and keep technology serving learning. *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation leans on external supports and partnerships**, with a Guidance and Resources section, "AI Websites and Supports," and reliance on the Nevada AI Alliance (pp.41-47). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Nevada states the over-reliance concern directly and ties it to process over product.

- **Its literacy goals name the offloading risk as policy.** Goal 2: "Focus on critical thinking and reasoning, emphasizing the process, not the result." Goal 5: "Technology should serve learning, not define it. AI is a support for learning, not the foundation" (p.33). *[Recommendation]*
- **The Responsible Use chapter is explicit:** "it is crucial to ensure students do not become overly dependent on it. Teaching them how to think critically, generate original ideas, and evaluate AI-generated information helps build their confidence as independent learners" (p.38). *[Recommendation]*
- **Administrators are told to guard against overdependence.** Best practice: "monitoring student interactions to prevent misuse or overdependence" (p.41). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy includes evaluating and refining AI output**, "mitigating bias, verifying accuracy, and ensuring appropriate use" (p.27). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Human oversight recurs as a boundary.** Teachers must "remain the primary decision-makers" (p.41), and AI "should complement, not replace, human interaction" (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- The Achievement chapter's framing line captures the intended balance: "With AI, we don't replace effort, we amplify success" (p.32). *[Framing]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Nevada graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and district-implemented, exposure will vary.

- **Familiarity with AI inside a mastery/PCBL model**, where students advance on demonstrated competency and may have used AI for adaptive, self-paced learning (p.24). *Basis: p.24, recommendation.*
- **A process-over-product orientation to AI**, if teachers followed the state's literacy goals (p.33). *Basis: p.33.*
- **Basic prompt-craft using task, format, voice, and context** (p.25). *Basis: p.25, instructional guidance.*
- **An integrity expectation grounded in honor codes and disclosure**, where districts adopted them (p.38). *Basis: p.38.*
- **Exposure to assistive AI** (text-to-speech, translation) as inclusivity tools (p.35). *Basis: p.35.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Because STELLAR is voluntary and district-implemented, faculty should expect wide within-cohort variance, and should note that Nevada framed AI around competency and process rather than a fixed permission vocabulary.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Higher education co-authored Nevada's guidance. Faculty from UNLV, the College of Southern Nevada, and Western Nevada College sat on the Executive Steering Committee (p.51), and the UNR Library's "Citing AI" guide is a named resource (p.47). Nevada institutions may already own pieces of the framework their incoming students learned under, and can align entry expectations to it.
- **Higher-education implication:** Nevada students may arrive framing AI through *process over product* and *competency-based mastery* (pp.24, 33). A university AI policy that rewards reasoning and demonstrated understanding, rather than finished output, will feel continuous with their K-12 experience.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught an integrity model built on *honor codes and disclosure* (p.38), and Nevada's guidance offers no AI-detector endorsement. Institutions relying on detection software should not assume Nevada students were trained to expect it; disclosure-based approaches map better.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because Nevada emphasized *AI literacy for all stakeholders* through the Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle (pp.24-27), incoming students may already have a shared vocabulary for evaluating and refining AI output that universities can extend rather than introduce.
- **Higher-education implication:** The workforce and "future-ready" framing (pp.6, 33) suggests students may value AI instruction tied to careers and disciplinary practice.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Governance and partnership:** Where the institution is in Nevada (notably UNLV, CSN, WNC, UNR), confirm its role in the Nevada AI Alliance or STELLAR authorship and align entry expectations with the state framework (p.51).
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment, since Nevada offered no standardized scale, and reward process and mastery, mirroring PCBL and the state's process-over-result goal (pp.24, 33).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor honor-code and disclosure-based integrity, which continues what Nevada taught (p.38). Do not assume detector familiarity.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; district-level variance will be high. Nevada's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle (pp.24-27) is a ready scaffold for a baseline.
- **Prompt-literacy scaffolding:** Build on the task/format/voice/context prompt elements Nevada taught (p.25), extending toward source verification and citation.

## Unique Aspects

- **The STELLAR mnemonic** as the organizing structure, seven named principle chapters (Security, Transparency, Empowerment, Learning, Leadership, Achievement, Responsible Use).
- **Integration with Personalized Competency-Based Learning (PCBL)**, Nevada's existing mastery-based model, rather than a bolt-on AI framework (p.24).
- **A dedicated, published multi-audience Best Practices set** for Districts, Administrators, Teachers, Parents, and Students (pp.41-46), including a parent-facing "Emotional Well-Being" practice that few states address.
- **Higher-education and industry co-authorship** through the Nevada AI Alliance and an Executive Steering Committee spanning UNLV, CSN, WNC, Google, and Code.org, plus student-voice contributors (p.51).
- **Blunt process-over-product literacy goals** that put the anti-offloading stance in writing (p.33).
- **A comprehensive federal-law box** (FERPA, COPPA, CIPA, PPRA, plus IDEA/ESSA/civil-rights implications) unusual for its completeness (p.15).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness limits every claim about students.** STELLAR mandates nothing and is district-implemented, so actual student exposure is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists.** The Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle is a stakeholder learning progression, not an assignment-level permission scale, and Nevada adapts no external scale. This is a genuine absence.
