# Cross-State Comparison: K-12 AI Policy for a Higher-Education Audience

*A synthesis across all 52 jurisdictions (50 states + District of Columbia + Puerto Rico), built from the per-state analysis files. This is the comparison layer the handoff deferred until the per-state pass was complete. It is written for university faculty and administrators who receive these K-12 graduates.*

**How to read this file.** Each claim traces to one or more per-state analysis files, which in turn cite primary documents. Where a pattern rests on the earlier-pass states (Alabama through New Mexico), it draws on those files and the cross-cutting notes recorded during the source-corpus pass. Provenance flags are respected throughout: pending bills are called pending, tracker-sourced claims are labeled, and absence is distinguished from silence.

---

## 1. The headline pattern

The project's thesis holds up against the full corpus. **State education agencies built a working governance vocabulary for classroom AI while most universities were still writing "ask your instructor" into syllabi.** By mid-2026, a large majority of jurisdictions had published K-12 AI guidance, several had enacted statutes, and multiple states had produced leveled student-use scales, tool-vetting rubrics, prohibition lists, and disclosure conventions. The instruments are concrete, adoptable, and in several cases lifted directly from a peer-reviewed source a faculty member can cite.

Do not put a single count in the masthead. The number of "states with guidance" depends on definitions (agency guidance vs. statute vs. curated resource page vs. district-only), and several jurisdictions changed status during this pass. Publish a sourced range and let the per-state files carry the specifics.

The more useful finding is the **shape** of what K-12 built, which the rest of this file maps.

---

## 2. The instruments spine: one 2024 paper, adapted many times

The single most important cross-state fact for a faculty audience is that the states are not independently inventing student-use scales. They are adapting one academic source: **Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024), the AI Assessment Scale (AIAS).**

**Primary-confirmed AIAS lineage (attribution printed in the state document):**
- **New Mexico** — five-level AI Assessment Scale, verbatim AIAS level names (No AI → AI Exploration).
- **Oklahoma** — five-level (0-4) AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale, "Adapted by Dr. Karen Leonard... from Furze, Perkins, Roe & Mcvaugh."
- **Wyoming** — four-level Student AI Usage Continuum (AI FREE → AI EMPOWERED), "As adapted from: Furze, Perkins, Roe & Mcvaugh."
- **North Carolina** — five-level (0-4) "0 to Infinity" AI Use Continuum, "based on the work of Furze, Perkins, Roe and McVaugh... adapted for the K12 environment."

**AIAS one step removed (translated or recommended, not directly attributed):**
- **Washington** — a five-level OSPI scale (No AI Assistance → AI as Co-Creator) that is structurally a cousin of the AIAS but is OSPI-original, not Perkins-attributed.
- **Puerto Rico** — a verbatim Spanish translation of Washington OSPI's five-level scale, so its lineage runs through Washington, not directly through Perkins.
- **Oregon** — recommends districts adopt the AIAS (or the Kapor Center tool) but authors no state scale of its own.

**The takeaway for faculty:** a university can adopt the peer-reviewed AIAS and expect partial prior familiarity from students in New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, North Carolina, and (via translation) Puerto Rico and Washington. Citing Perkins et al. is more defensible than reverse-engineering a state PDF, and it gives K-16 continuity.

## 3. Scale designs compared

Among the scale-bearing states, the designs cluster but differ in ways that matter:

| State | Levels | Numbering | Top level | Distinctive feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Mexico | 5 | 1-5 | AI Exploration | Verbatim AIAS names; national-lab K-16 pipeline |
| Oklahoma | 5 | 0-4 | Full AI Use w/ Human Oversight | Per-level disclosure incl. **links to AI chats** |
| North Carolina | 5 | 0-4 | AI Exploration | Teacher + student versions; PK-13 scope |
| Washington | 5 | 1-5 | AI as Co-Creator | Companion educator matrix (assignment types × levels) |
| Puerto Rico | 5 | 1-5 | IA como Cocreador | Spanish; single mandated platform (Copilot) |
| Wyoming | 4 | named (unnumbered) | AI EMPOWERED | Per-level disclosure incl. **links to AI chats** |
| Louisiana | 4 | SAMR-aligned | AI-Empowered/Redefinition | One SAMR model (not two), recommends detectors (outlier) |

Two design signatures recur and are worth teaching:
- **The AI-free floor.** Every scale reserves a bottom level (Level 0 or 1, "No AI") to preserve unaided skill demonstration. This is the shared answer to cognitive offloading.
- **Escalating disclosure.** Higher levels demand more transparency. Oklahoma and Wyoming go furthest, requiring students to submit **links to their AI chat transcripts**. A faculty member can adopt this directly.

## 4. The shared-source signal (a finding for the comparison)

Two independent clues show that some states drew from a **common intermediate adaptation**, not from the AIAS paper directly:
- **Oklahoma and Wyoming both misspell "MacVaugh" as "Mcvaugh"** in their attribution lines, and both require submitting links to AI chats.
- The **TeachAI toolkit** is the shared parent of the principle-based states (below).

This matters because it means the apparent convergence is partly a citation cascade. The states look like they independently landed on the same good idea, but several are copying the same intermediary. That is both a caution (errors propagate, as the misspelling shows) and a simplification (faculty can go to the original two sources, Perkins et al. and TeachAI, rather than 50 state PDFs).

## 5. Governance posture: a spectrum from statute to silence

Jurisdictions fall on a clear spectrum of legal force:

**Enacted statutes with real requirements (strongest):**
- **Tennessee** — Public Chapter 550 requires public *universities*, local school boards, and charter schools to adopt AI policies (universities by July 1, 2025), with a noncompliance hearing backstop. The dataset's only law binding higher education directly.
- **Oklahoma** — SB 1734 (Responsible Technology in Schools Act) mandates educator-directed use, human-in-the-loop review, a ban on AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions, parental disclosure and opt-out, and district policy adoption before 2027-28.
- **Ohio** — ORC 3301.24 (HB 96) requires every district, community school, and STEM school to adopt an AI-use policy by July 1, 2026 (adoption mandated, content local).
- **Utah** — HB 218 embeds AI literacy into the required grade 7-8 digital skills course.

**Guidance with a statutory adoption requirement pending or partial:** Virginia (HB 1186/SB 394 effective July 1, 2026, text unverified), West Virginia (HB 5205 pending), New Jersey (A4352 pending, would mandate university AI programs).

**Voluntary agency guidance (the large middle):** New Mexico, Washington, Oregon, Montana, Nevada, Mississippi, Missouri, North Carolina, North Dakota, Rhode Island, Vermont, Wisconsin, Wyoming, plus most earlier-pass states.

**Confirmed or near-confirmed absence at the state agency:** Nebraska, New Hampshire (endorsed a non-state coalition doc), South Dakota (ASBSD model policy fills the gap), South Carolina (CTE course standards only, no use guidance), Texas (no TEA guidance), New York (no NYSED guidance; bills + NYC district only), Arkansas (no ADE framework).

**The key distinction for faculty:** a *mandate to adopt a policy* (Ohio, Tennessee) is not a mandate about *content*. Even in the strongest-statute states, what students actually experienced varies by district. Assume a policy existed; do not assume a shared scale.

## 6. Higher-education hooks, ranked

This is the dimension the project cares about most. Ranked from strongest to weakest:

1. **Tennessee (statutory).** Law requires public universities to adopt AI policies on the K-12 timeline. Unmatched.
2. **Virginia (assigned roles).** A K-16 document by design; assigns AI roles to SCHEV, the community college system, and "every public college and university," with a "Work in partnership" principle naming colleges.
3. **New Jersey (pending mandate).** A4352 would require public institutions to *offer* AI certificate and degree programs. Strong but not enacted.
4. **Pennsylvania (teacher preparation).** A teacher-certification AI endorsement built for university teacher-prep programs, issued by the higher-ed office; the state's flagship AI instrument is aimed at higher ed.
5. **West Virginia (structural).** The higher-education Chancellor sits ex officio on the state board that issued the K-12 guidance.
6. **Ohio / Wyoming (committee).** Both recommend postsecondary or "local college" representatives on district AI workgroups.
7. **Maryland, Kansas (coordination/pipeline).** Maryland's four-year-institution tool certification and central-office coordinator; Kansas's KU "Pre-K-20" framing (per earlier-pass notes).
8. **Florida, Arizona (authored by universities).** State guidance produced by UF and NAU respectively (per earlier-pass notes).
9. **New Mexico (named pipeline).** NMAIC unites national labs and universities in a K-16 AI-education pipeline.
10. **Framing-only (college/career readiness):** Washington, Rhode Island, Nevada, Utah, and most others tie AI literacy to postsecondary success without assigning institutional roles.
11. **No hook:** North Dakota, Wisconsin (though Wisconsin's public-library/lifelong-learning framing is a distinct community-learning angle), and the absence states.

**The synthesis:** the states that put higher education on the hook did so in four different ways, by statute (TN), by assigned governance role (VA), by teacher-preparation (PA), and by authorship (FL, AZ). A faculty member's own state likely sits somewhere on this list, and the per-state file says where.

## 7. AI detectors: a near-consensus, with one outlier

The most striking value convergence in the corpus is skepticism of AI-detection software.

**States that caution against or discourage detectors:** Arizona, California, Colorado, Indiana, Illinois, Massachusetts, Maryland, New Mexico (earlier-pass notes), plus, confirmed in this pass, **North Carolina, Oklahoma, Vermont, Washington** (all strong), and **Utah, Oregon, West Virginia, Rhode Island, Mississippi** (cautionary-to-tolerant). The recurring rationale: false positives, and bias against non-native English writers. Washington cites Vanderbilt and Stanford research; Vermont, North Carolina, and Oklahoma make the English-language-learner bias explicit.

**The outlier: Louisiana**, which *recommends* detection tools (Turnitin, Grammarly, GoGuardian) (earlier-pass notes).

**Neutral/silent:** North Dakota (mentions detectors without endorsing or warning), Ohio, Virginia, Missouri, Nevada, Wyoming, and most absence states.

**Implication for faculty (high-value):** incoming students from a large bloc of states were explicitly taught that AI detectors are unreliable and biased. Institutions that rely on detection software for integrity enforcement will meet students trained, often with cited research, to distrust it. The defensible, K-12-continuous move is process- and disclosure-based integrity.

## 8. Cognitive offloading: who names the problem

This is the pedagogical core of the higher-ed argument, and states vary sharply in how directly they engage it.

- **Vermont is the flagship.** A dedicated "Cognitive Offloading" section quotes Ethan Mollick's "Against Brain Damage," uses the calculator analogy, and gives teachers a two-question test (does AI help students practice the skill or perform it for them). It empowers teachers to refuse AI.
- **Pennsylvania makes it a teacher competency.** Its endorsement Domain 6 requires candidates to analyze "how cognitive offloading to AI systems influences attention, memory, and problem-solving."
- **The developmental-gating cluster** (North Carolina, North Dakota, Rhode Island, Vermont) ties offloading to child development, warning that premature AI use "short-circuits" problem-solving and bypasses "productive struggle."
- **Utah and Montana** name over-reliance explicitly, Utah as a prohibited use with agentic-AI offloading language, Montana tying it to "productive struggle."
- **The scale-builders** encode offloading protection structurally, through an AI-free floor and escalating human-oversight requirements.
- **Weakest:** the absence states and the framing-only states, where offloading is not addressed at the state level.

**The synthesis:** K-12 has already named the exact risk that motivates cautious university AI policy, and named it well. Vermont's calculator analogy and Pennsylvania's competency language are ready-made for a faculty audience.

## 9. Developmental gating: the PreK-early-elementary "no chatbot" cluster

A consistent developmental pattern appears across the guidance states: **young children should not use open-ended AI chatbots.**
- **Vermont:** PreK-2 "should not use AI chatbots"; grades 3-5 no open-ended chatbots.
- **North Carolina:** PK-5 direct chatbot use "not recommended."
- **North Dakota:** K-2 must learn "AI is not a real person."
- **Rhode Island:** K-2 supervised, district-approved tools only.
- The shared rationale is anthropomorphizing (young children attribute human qualities to AI) and protecting foundational literacy.

The bands then converge on a common arc: **Awareness (elementary) → Literacy (middle) → Fluency (high school)**, with North Carolina and Vermont using nearly identical labels. Faculty should note that the youngest cohorts had *deliberately limited* early AI exposure; structured fluency is concentrated in high school.

## 10. Cross-state borrowing (states copying states)

The corpus is not 52 independent efforts. Documented borrowing:
- **TeachAI toolkit** is the shared parent of the principle-based states (Utah, West Virginia, Wyoming, Missouri, and others), which is why their "I will / We will" principles and "do no harm / prioritize integrity" language rhyme.
- **Washington → Puerto Rico** (verbatim scale translation).
- **North Carolina → Rhode Island** (RIDE reproduces NCDPI's acceptable-use guide in its appendix and cites NCDPI staff).
- **Oregon → Virginia** (Virginia points to Oregon's K-12 resource); **Oregon** is also cited by Mississippi.
- **Oklahoma ↔ Wyoming** shared intermediate AIAS source (the "Mcvaugh" misspelling).

**Implication:** the apparent 50-state consensus is real but partly a copying network. That is good news for faculty, it means a small number of source documents (Perkins et al., TeachAI, Washington OSPI, NCDPI) underlie most of what students were taught.

## 11. Distinctive single-state contributions worth knowing

- **Montana** — tribal sovereignty and Indigenous data sovereignty written into AI policy; a rule against remixing IEFA materials with AI.
- **Wisconsin** — the only state to frame AI literacy as lifelong learning through public libraries.
- **Nevada** — the STELLAR mnemonic and integration with competency-based learning (PCBL).
- **Missouri** — a workforce-readiness spine and prompt-engineering ("Five S") as a core skill.
- **Ohio** — non-consensual intimate imagery language tied to Braden's Law.
- **Vermont** — the environmental cost of AI, and the Mollick-cited offloading section.
- **Puerto Rico** — a single mandated platform (MS Copilot) and supervised-use requirement, the most prescriptive tool posture (a function of being a unified territory system).
- **Maryland / Missouri / Ohio** — a district "AI Coordinator" role.

## 13. What a faculty member should take away

1. **A shared vocabulary already exists.** The AIAS (Perkins et al. 2024) is the closest thing to a common K-16 language. Adopting it gives continuity with New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, North Carolina, Washington, and Puerto Rico students.
2. **Per-assignment, not blanket.** Students from scale-adopting states expect AI permission specified per assignment, often with required disclosure. A silent or blanket syllabus reads as less clear than their high school did.
3. **Detectors are already discredited in K-12.** A large bloc of states taught students that AI detectors are unreliable and biased. Process- and disclosure-based integrity is the K-12-continuous choice.
4. **Cognitive offloading is a named, taught concept.** Vermont and Pennsylvania give faculty ready language for why some assignments must stay AI-free.
5. **Variance is the rule.** Even in mandate states, content is local. Assume a wide range within any cohort and set a common baseline at entry.
6. **Your own institution may already be named.** Tennessee binds universities by law; Virginia assigns them roles; Pennsylvania builds teacher-prep endorsements; Florida, Arizona, and New Mexico wrote higher education into the guidance. Check the per-state file for your state.

## 14. Method and limits

- **Every per-state file reads only that jurisdiction's own documents**, tags claims by evidence level, and cites filename plus page or section.
- **Voluntariness is the universal caveat.** Most guidance is non-binding and district-implemented, so student-level claims are ceilings of possibility, not guarantees.
- **Absence is not proof of nonexistence.** The confirmed-absence states rest on thorough-for-tooling searches, not certification.
- **Several high-value primary documents still merit human eyes**, notably the New Hampshire coalition PDF, the New York NYC guidance (JS-rendered), the Virginia bill texts (JS-rendered), and the South Dakota ASBSD model policy (members-only).
- **The earlier-pass states (Alabama–New Mexico)** are represented here through their analysis files and the source-corpus cross-cutting notes; where this file leans on those notes rather than a fresh primary read, it says so.
