**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** This is a classroom-practice guidance document, not a rulebook. It repeatedly defers to district authority ("Know and follow the acceptable use policies developed by the school district," pp.8-9) and its policy section only lists what "district leaders should consider" (p.22). **Nothing in it is a statewide mandate.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. A second source, SB 2426 (2025), is a statute but is recorded in sources.md only (no bill text in the folder), and it establishes a general state AI task force, not a K-12 requirement.

## Executive Summary

Mississippi's guidance is a recommendation-level, use-case-driven document. It is organized not around an AI-permission scale but around the state's existing **five components of digital learning**, taken from the Digital Learning Instructional Guide. Those five components are Digital Citizenship, Standards-Aligned Content and Tools, Active Learning and Engagement, Formative Assessment and Feedback, and Accessibility (p.7). Under each, the document lists concrete strategies for three audiences at once, Students, Teachers, and Administrators.

This structure makes Mississippi distinct from the scale-building states. It provides no leveled student-use instrument, no 0-to-4 or 1-to-5 permission ladder, and no adaptation of the Perkins et al. AI Assessment Scale. Its governing move is to route almost every question of permission and prohibition down to the local district's acceptable use policy (pp.8-9, 22). Its most quotable position is that inaction is the real hazard. It quotes Hadi Partovi of Code.org that "the biggest risk is doing nothing" and argues that banning AI tools is "impractical" because AI already runs in the background of ordinary software (p.22).

For a higher-education reader, the useful facts are three. First, a Mississippi graduate's AI exposure was governed by a local acceptable use policy, not a state standard, so cohort variance will be high. Second, the state built a deliberate anti-detector caution into its guidance and warns that detectors falsely flag English Language learners (p.12). Third, the document was reviewed with university partners (iTeach at Kennesaw State University and Jackson State University faculty on the AI Collaborative, p.3), a teacher-preparation and review hook rather than a K-16 pipeline.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **The human element is central and cannot be replaced.** "AI-powered tools can never replace the human element of teaching" (p.7). AI is framed as an enhancement to instruction, not a substitute for the educator. *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Purpose and intent, not the tool, determine appropriateness.** "The tool, device, or process itself is not right or wrong... educators' and students' purpose and intent when using the technology determines its benefits and appropriateness" (p.6). *[Recommendation]*
- **Doing nothing is the biggest risk.** The document explicitly rejects prohibition as a strategy. "This unregulated access makes the banning of AI tools impractical... districts can create a more significant risk by doing nothing to address AI use or train stakeholders" (p.22). *[Recommendation]*
- **The guidance is scaffolded onto an existing state framework.** Its five sections mirror "the five components of digital learning found in the Digital Learning Instructional Guide" (p.7), so AI is presented as continuous with prior Mississippi digital-learning work rather than a new regime. *[Scope / framing]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Every strategy section addresses **Students, Teachers, and Administrators** as three separate lists (pp.8-21). The administrator lists lean heavily toward professional development and policy support. *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is all K-12 classrooms. The document does not break guidance into grade bands and gives no age-differentiated instrument. *[Scope statement]*
- Authority rests with districts. Students and teachers are told to "know and follow the acceptable use policies developed by the school district" (pp.8-9), and the closing note says "local schools have discretion over which technology partners and products are used" (p.25). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** The document delegates the permitted/prohibited line to each district and each assignment. Educators are told to "determine what cheating or plagiarism looks like for each assignment" and then "clearly articulate the criteria to students and families" (p.11). *[Recommendation / not a mandate]*
- The closest thing to a permission structure is the per-assignment expectation-setting instruction: "Before the assignment begins, review both appropriate and inappropriate uses of AI tools for the specific assignment. Be explicit with the websites or tools students can use" (p.11). This is a process, not a rule. *[Recommendation]*
- Prohibition is discussed only to argue against it as a blanket approach (p.22). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Digital citizenship is component one and treated as the foundation.** "The first and most essential component for ensuring proper use of AI... is developing digital citizenship skills" (p.8), covering safe, kind, and responsible use, source citation, and critical analysis of AI output (p.10). *[Recommendation]*
- **Privacy and security** are handled through federal law compliance. Administrators are told to protect student privacy "including COPPA and FERPA guidelines" (p.9) and to "carefully examine the privacy policies of AI tools to ensure compliance with FERPA and COPPA" (p.22). *[Recommendation]*
- **Accessibility is elevated to component five and called the most powerful use.** "Perhaps the most powerful use of AI tools in place today is the ability to provide personalized, targeted support for students" through text-to-speech, translation, leveled explanations, and IEP support (pp.19-21). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity** gets a dedicated "Strategies to Deter Cheating and/or Plagiarism" subsection (pp.11-12). The recommended strategies are pedagogical, not surveillance-first: know students' writing style, require process documentation and multiple drafts, review version history, redesign assessments to be AI-resistant, and enter the prompt into an AI tool yourself to see what it produces (pp.11-12). *[Instructional guidance / recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools (p.12):** skeptical. "In most cases, these tools do not accurately detect AI-generated material, especially material that contains only a few hundred words. Additionally, these tools can falsely identify the writings of English Language students as generated material simply because their writing skills are still developing." Detectors are listed as one option among many, and administrators are told to "weigh the pros and cons" and note that many "are costly" and "only somewhat effective" (pp.12, 22). *[Recommendation — a caution against over-reliance on detectors]*
- Note a mild internal tension. The teacher strategy list still says "require students to submit work to plagiarism checkers" (p.9) and "Turnitin also now features AI classifier detection" (p.12), so the document is cautionary about detectors rather than opposed to them. *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Training has its own section** (pp.23-24) listing recommended topics: informed use, accuracy checking via lateral reading, evaluating response quality, acknowledging bias, detecting plagiarism, revamping learning activities, expanding digital citizenship, and classroom policies. *[Recommendation]*
- **Curriculum integration is by embedding into existing practice**, not a standalone AI course. The five components map onto the Mississippi College- and Career-Readiness (MCCR) Standards work teachers already do, e.g. using AI as a "thought partner to create lessons, activities, and assessments that align to grade-level MCCR Standards" (p.13). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Assessment guidance** appears twice. As integrity protection, teachers are told to "rethink how they assess," replacing multiple choice with real-world projects, visual responses, and collaborative co-construction (pp.11-12). As formative practice, AI is offered to generate practice questions, provide instant feedback, and build rubrics (pp.17-18). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Implementation/governance** is a checklist of what "district leaders should consider": evaluate tools, adhere to security guidelines, promote benefits and mitigate risks, monitor use, detect plagiarism, expand digital citizenship, and gather feedback in a "continuous feedback loop" (p.22). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

The document engages over-reliance directly, though less formally than states with a named framework.

- It names the risk in its opening cautions. AI can "invite over reliance that may limit critical and original thinking" (p.6). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Its clearest anti-offloading instruction is a scaffolding-fade principle in the accessibility section.** Educators should be "mindful of properly scaffolding these supports, gradually reducing students' reliance on these tools" (p.19). The intent is that AI supports are temporary supports, withdrawn as the student gains competence. *[Recommendation]*
- It preserves human cognition through assessment redesign rather than prohibition. The deter-cheating strategies push toward tasks "difficult for AI to provide the answers," open-ended and context-specific prompts, and student-generated visual and collaborative work (pp.11-12). *[Instructional guidance]*
- It builds an interrogation habit around AI output. Students should "check the validity of the information and sources" from generative AI (p.8), and training should improve "lateral reading and fact-checking" against misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation (p.23). *[Instructional guidance]*
- It asks students to reflect on their own AI use. A recommended student practice is to "reflect on how AI tools were used for idea generation and feedback during the writing process and how this support impacted the final product" (p.17). *[Instructional guidance / example]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Mississippi graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and the operative rules were set district by district, exposure will be uneven.

- **Governed by a district acceptable use policy, not a state scale.** A Mississippi student was repeatedly directed to "know and follow the acceptable use policies developed by the school district" (p.8). A graduate is more likely to expect a local rulebook than a portable leveled vocabulary. *Basis: pp.8-9, recommendations.*
- **A citation-and-validation habit around AI sources**, where teachers followed the digital-citizenship guidance: cite AI-acquired information and check the validity of AI-provided sources (p.8). *Basis: p.8.*
- **Exposure to AI as an accessibility and study aid.** Students in adopting classrooms may have used text-to-speech, translation, dictation, leveled explanations, and personalized study plans (pp.19-20). *Basis: pp.19-20, examples.*
- **Familiarity with process-based integrity checks** rather than detector-based enforcement, if their teachers used draft submission, version-history review, and AI-resistant assignments (pp.11-12). *Basis: pp.11-12.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Because Mississippi published classroom use cases but delegated the rules, faculty should assume wide within-cohort variance, from students whose districts adopted rich AI practice to students whose district did little beyond a short acceptable use policy.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Mississippi students likely learned AI norms as a *district acceptable use policy*, not as a per-assignment permission scale. They may arrive expecting a single institutional rulebook and be less primed than students from scale-adopting states (New Mexico, Washington, Wyoming) to read assignment-by-assignment AI levels. A syllabus that specifies AI permission per task will be a new pattern to teach, not a familiar one.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught that *inaction and blanket bans are both wrong answers*. The state's own framing that "doing nothing" is the biggest risk (p.22) means incoming students may expect their institution to have an active, articulated AI position rather than silence.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students may have been taught that *AI detectors are unreliable and biased against developing and English-language writers* (p.12). Institutions relying on AI-detection software for integrity enforcement may meet students who were explicitly taught to distrust those tools, a position the K-12 guidance itself takes.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because accessibility was framed as "perhaps the most powerful use" of AI (p.19), students with support needs may arrive expecting AI accommodations (text-to-speech, translation, leveled text) as normal classroom infrastructure, and may expect the scaffolding to fade as they progress (p.19).
- **Higher-education implication:** The guidance's integrity model is process-based, built on drafts, reflection, and AI-resistant task design (pp.11-12). Students from adopting classrooms may already be accustomed to documenting their writing process, which universities can build on rather than introduce.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission statement, because Mississippi students likely did not encounter a standardized scale and will benefit from clarity. Where the learning goal is core-skill demonstration, use AI-resistant task designs the state already models (real-world projects, collaborative co-construction, context-specific prompts, pp.11-12).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor process-based and disclosure-based integrity over detector reliance, which the students' own K-12 guidance calls costly and unreliable and biased against English Language learners (p.12). This continues, rather than contradicts, what many Mississippi students were taught.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short entry diagnostic of AI literacy rather than assuming it. Cohort variance will be high because rules were set locally.
- **Accessibility:** Confirm that sanctioned campus AI tools carry the accessibility features (text-to-speech, translation, leveled explanation) Mississippi flagged as the most powerful use, and that access does not depend on ability to pay for a premium tier.
- **Faculty development:** Offer faculty the state's lateral-reading and fact-checking framing (p.23) and its "enter the prompt into an AI tool and evaluate the results" technique (p.12) as ready pedagogy, since incoming students may already have met these.
- **Governance:** Because Mississippi delegated authority to districts, universities should not assume any uniform prior AI training. Set a common baseline at entry.

## Unique Aspects

- **Organized by the state's five components of digital learning**, not by an AI-permission scale. The five are Digital Citizenship, Standards-Aligned Content and Tools, Active Learning and Engagement, Formative Assessment and Feedback, and Accessibility (p.7). This grounds AI in an existing Mississippi framework rather than a new one.
- **Three-audience structure throughout.** Every component gives parallel Student, Teacher, and Administrator strategy lists (pp.8-21).
- **An explicit anti-prohibition stance.** The document argues banning is "impractical" and quotes Hadi Partovi that "the biggest risk is doing nothing" (p.22). Few state documents state the case against bans this bluntly.
- **A scaffolding-fade principle for AI supports.** "Gradually reducing students' reliance on these tools" (p.19) is a distinctive cognitive-offloading control tied to accessibility.
- **University review partners.** The document was reviewed by iTeach at Kennesaw State University and by faculty in the Mississippi AI Collaborative powered by Microsoft, including Jackson State University (p.3). This is a teacher-preparation and review hook, not a governance pipeline.
- **Accessibility elevated to "perhaps the most powerful use" of AI** (p.19), a stronger framing than most state documents give assistive uses.

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness and delegation limit every claim about students.** The document mandates nothing and routes rules to districts, so actual student exposure to any practice is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **No date on the document.** The cover carries no publication date, and sources.md notes an earlier April 2024 version exists. The dataset's "2025" label is not confirmed on the file itself. Treat the version as uncertain. *[Unresolved]*
- **SB 2426 (2025) is recorded in sources.md only.** The Artificial Intelligence Regulation (AIR) Task Force it created is a general state AI task force whose study scope includes elementary and secondary education, per the ledger, and is **not a K-12-specific mandate**. The bill text is not in the folder, so no statutory requirement on schools can be asserted here. *[Unresolved]*
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists in Mississippi.** Unlike the scale-building states, the document offers no 0-4 or 1-5 permission ladder and no adaptation of Perkins et al. This is a genuine absence, not a retrieval gap.
- **Resources and References (pp.25-26)** are third-party links and were not treated as Mississippi policy. Not a substantive gap for policy claims.
