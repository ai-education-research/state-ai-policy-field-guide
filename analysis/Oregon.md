**Nature of the documents (read before everything below):** Both ODE documents are voluntary. The 2023 document is foundational guidance for districts; the March 2025 document is a policy-development workbook "intended to provide resources and a framework for districts developing such policies," to be used alongside the 2023 guidance (2025, Purpose). **Neither is a statewide mandate.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. A third item, SB 1546 (2026, Chapter 85), is an AI-companion/chatbot consumer-protection law with minor protections; per sources.md it is **not a K-12 instructional statute** and is not treated as school policy here.

## Executive Summary

Oregon's guidance is a recommendation-level, equity-centered pair of ODE documents. The 2023 foundational guidance frames AI as an educator-controlled tool, using the U.S. Office of Educational Technology's "electric bike, not robot vacuum" metaphor to insist that "educators are the decision makers" (2023, p.2). The March 2025 companion is a structured district policy-development workbook, organized as a series of Goals (community input, ethical use, assessment parameters, and more) with recommended tasks, guidance questions, and process takeaways.

Oregon does not author its own leveled student-use scale. Instead, the 2025 workbook points districts to external options, recommending that they "consider utilizing a scale for use of genAI on assessments, such as the Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale (AIAS)... or the Kapor Center's AI Classroom Assignment Norms Tool" (2025, Goal F). This is a lighter connection to the Perkins et al. AIAS than the states that adapted it into their own instrument (Oklahoma, New Mexico, North Carolina, Wyoming). Oregon references it as one recommended tool, not as a state-adopted scale.

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, equity is Oregon's organizing lens, the 2023 document centers equity implications, and the 2025 workbook is built on the National Educational Technology Plan's "three Digital Divides" (2025, Policy Landscape). Second, higher education helped author the 2023 guidance, University of Oregon professor Joanna Goode is a named contributor (2023, acknowledgements). Third, Oregon's stance on AI detectors is more tolerant than the anti-detector states, it tells educators to "consider using AI detectors, with an understanding that these detectors often incorrectly detect" (2023, Table 1).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Educator-as-decision-maker, human-in-the-loop.** "Whenever using AI... it is essential that educators are the decision makers and their knowledge and expertise is central" (2023, p.2), captured by the electric-bike metaphor where "the human is fully aware and fully in control" (2023, p.2). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Equity is the central lens.** The 2023 document devotes a full section to "Equity Implications," and the 2025 workbook frames AI policy around the three Digital Divides, Use, Design, and Access (2025, Policy Landscape). *[Recommendation]*
- **Policy should be "clear, attentive to the current moment, mindful of student data privacy and equity centered"** (2023, p.~104 region). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy as a career necessity.** "As AI literacy is becoming a necessary job skill for most careers, districts must consider both the benefits and risks" (2025, Policy Landscape). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The 2023 guidance addresses districts, educators, families, and support staff, "including paraeducators, secretaries, support staff" (2023, equity section). *[Scope statement]*
- The 2025 workbook is written for "school and district leaders" developing policy (2025, Purpose). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is K-12 and focused specifically on generative AI (2023, p.1). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no state-authored permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Oregon delegates permitted/prohibited decisions to districts and recommends they adopt an external scale. The 2025 workbook's Goal F says to "define when AI can or cannot be used on an assessment" and to "consider utilizing a scale... such as the AIAS... or the Kapor Center's AI Classroom Assignment Norms Tool" (2025, Goal F). *[Recommendation]*
- The 2023 document notes the national shift from banning ChatGPT toward embracing it, and the 2025 workbook echoes that districts "initially banned platforms... shifting toward embracing its potential" (2025, Policy Guidance from National Organizations). *[Framing]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Data privacy rests on a named legal stack.** The 2025 workbook cites the Oregon Student Information Protection Act (OSIPA), CIPA, COPPA, and FERPA as privacy laws with AI implications (2025, Policy Landscape). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level law]*
- **Equity is operationalized through the three Digital Divides**, with explicit district questions about creative use, professional learning time, and broadband/device access (2025, Policy Landscape). *[Recommendation]*
- **Accessibility and differentiation** are equity considerations, including whether AI-use parameters should be "modified for students with certain learning needs... students on IEPs or 504 plans or multilingual learners" (2025, Goal F). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is process-oriented.** The 2023 document lists plagiarism as an equity impact and recommends rethinking assignments and being "clear on what standards/skills are being addressed" (2023, Table 1). The 2025 workbook lists "Academic integrity: Assessments; Crediting sources/plagiarism" under student ethical use (2025, Ethical Use). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** more tolerant than the anti-detector states, but with caveats. The 2023 guidance says to "train educators to detect the presence of AI written communication" and to "consider using AI detectors, with an understanding that these detectors often incorrectly detect," naming AI detector tools and GPT Zero as having "mixed results" (2023, Table 1). It cautions about reliability without discouraging use outright. *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **The 2025 workbook is the implementation instrument.** It walks districts through Goals with recommended tasks, including forming an "AI-focused committee" (2025, Goal A) and using the CoSN Readiness Checklist as a self-assessment tool (2025). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assessment guidance favors process over product.** Districts should consider how "pedagogical practices might shift... focus on assessment of process versus product" and how AI supports authentic, performance, and project-based assessment (2025, Goal F). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Implementation favors piloting.** "Implement the policy with fidelity, it might be helpful to start with one school or grade level band before rolling out district wide" (2025, Goal region). *[Recommendation]*
- **Oregon surfaces peer district exemplars.** The 2025 workbook links shared AI policies from Salem-Keizer, North Clackamas, Tigard Tualatin, and Hillsboro (2025, Oregon District Guidance/Policies). *[Example]*
- **Curriculum resources** include Stanford's CRAFT (Classroom-Ready Resources About AI For Teaching) project (2023, p.~113). *[Example]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Oregon's offloading protection is expressed mainly through its human-in-the-loop framing and process-based assessment.

- **The electric-bike metaphor is the core anti-offloading stance.** AI should leave "the human... fully aware and fully in control, but their burden is less," as opposed to a robot vacuum that frees "the human from involvement or oversight" (2023, p.2). *[Recommendation]*
- **Caution about AI's biases and inaccuracies** is built into the foundational guidance: these tools "are subject to biases and inaccuracies... Caution must be exercised when considering a response" (2023, p.1). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Assessment redesign toward process** is the primary integrity-and-cognition mitigation (2025, Goal F; 2023, Table 1). *[Instructional guidance]*
- Oregon does not articulate a dedicated grade-band cognitive-offloading progression or a "productive struggle" doctrine; its offloading control is the educator-in-control principle plus process-focused assessment. *[Absence noted]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits an Oregon graduate *may* bring. Because both documents are voluntary and district-implemented, exposure varies.

- **Governed by a district policy, likely equity-framed**, shaped by the state's equity-centered guidance rather than a state scale (2023; 2025). *Basis: 2023, 2025.*
- **Possible exposure to an AIAS-style scale**, but only where a district chose to adopt one from the options Oregon recommended (2025, Goal F). Not a guaranteed shared vocabulary. *Basis: 2025, Goal F.*
- **An educator-in-control framing of AI**, where teachers followed the electric-bike principle (2023, p.2). *Basis: 2023.*
- **A process-over-product assessment experience**, where districts adopted the workbook's assessment guidance (2025, Goal F). *Basis: 2025.*
- **Uncertain detector expectations.** Oregon's guidance permits detectors with caveats (2023, Table 1), so students' experience of detection tools is district-dependent and not uniformly skeptical. *Basis: 2023.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Oregon set no state scale, so faculty should expect wide district variance and should not assume a shared leveled AI vocabulary.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Oregon recommended, but did not adopt, the Perkins et al. AIAS (2025, Goal F). A university adopting the AIAS may find some Oregon students recognize it, but only from districts that chose it, so prior familiarity is likely and partial rather than reliable.
- **Higher-education implication:** Oregon students were taught within an *equity-centered* frame built on the three Digital Divides (2025). Faculty may find these students attuned to access and differentiation questions, and institutions should ensure sanctioned AI tools do not depend on ability to pay.
- **Higher-education implication:** Oregon students were taught the *educator-in-control* principle (2023, p.2), so they may expect a named human authority over AI use rather than blanket permission or prohibition. A syllabus that specifies who decides and how will feel continuous.
- **Higher-education implication:** Oregon did *not* adopt a strong anti-detector stance (2023, Table 1). Unlike students from the anti-detector states, Oregon graduates may have encountered detection tools as an accepted, if imperfect, practice. Institutions should not assume a uniform prior view.
- **Higher-education implication:** Higher education helped author the foundational guidance (University of Oregon's Joanna Goode, 2023). Oregon institutions may already have faculty connected to the state's K-12 AI work.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment, and if adopting a scale, the AIAS gives continuity with the tool Oregon recommended (2025, Goal F). Favor process-based and authentic assessment, mirroring the workbook (2025, Goal F).
- **Equity:** Confirm sanctioned campus AI tools are accessible and not paywalled, matching the equity lens Oregon students were taught (2025).
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; district variance is high and no state scale anchors expectations.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor process- and disclosure-based integrity. Because Oregon's detector stance was permissive-with-caveats, set your own detector policy explicitly rather than assuming student expectations.
- **Governance:** Oregon institutions can connect with the ODE work their own faculty helped shape (2023) and with the district exemplars ODE surfaced (2025).

## Unique Aspects

- **A two-document, staged approach**, a 2023 foundational guidance plus a 2025 policy-development workbook meant to be used together (2025, Purpose).
- **The "electric bike, not robot vacuum" metaphor** for human-in-the-loop AI use (2023, p.2).
- **An equity spine built on the NETP three Digital Divides** (Use, Design, Access) (2025, Policy Landscape).
- **A workbook structure of Goals with tasks, questions, and takeaways**, plus links to real Oregon district exemplar policies (Salem-Keizer, North Clackamas, Tigard Tualatin, Hillsboro) (2025).
- **A recommend-don't-adopt posture toward scales**, pointing to the AIAS and the Kapor Center tool rather than authoring a state instrument (2025, Goal F).
- **A more detector-tolerant integrity stance** than the anti-detector states (2023, Table 1).
- **Higher-education co-authorship** via University of Oregon (2023).
