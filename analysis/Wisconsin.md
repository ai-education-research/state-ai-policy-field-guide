## Executive Summary

Wisconsin's guidance is a recommendation-level framework with a distinctive scope: it deliberately covers K-12 and library education together, including public libraries, under the banner "Empowering Lifelong Learning." Its author team reflects that scope, pairing subject and digital-learning consultants with a School Library Consultant and a Public Library Consultant (guidance p.~30). This library-and-lifelong-learning framing is Wisconsin's signature contribution and the closest thing it has to a beyond-K-12 hook.

The document does not use a leveled student-use scale. Its most concrete instrument is the "5 Training Pillars for K12 Educators," confirmed verbatim: (1) Understanding AI and Machine Learning Basics, (2) Integrating AI into Subject Areas, (3) Hands-On AI Experiences, (4) Collaboration and Networking, and (5) Continuous Learning and Adaptability (guidance p.207). Each pillar comes with named tools and Wisconsin-specific professional networks (SLATE, the CESA Instructional Technology Services Committee, WEMTA, the Wisconsin Digital Learning Collaborative).

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, Wisconsin set no state AI-permission scale or prohibition list, so a graduate's classroom AI norms came from the district. Second, the guidance's information- and media-literacy emphasis, delivered through a librarian lens, trains students to "critically examine AI-generated results" (guidance p.~162). Third, Wisconsin's distinctive move is to treat AI literacy as a lifelong, community-wide capability delivered partly through public libraries, which reframes AI literacy as adult and community learning, not only K-12.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI supports but cannot replace educators or librarians.** "AI supports human teaching and learning but cannot replace educators. The human component... teachers' and librarians' ability to inspire, motivate, and respond to individual needs, is irreplaceable" (guidance p.~136). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Lifelong learning through schools and public libraries.** The document frames AI integration "across Wisconsin's schools, libraries, and communities" (guidance p.~110). *[Recommendation / framing]*
- **Responsible, ethical, safe use** with attention to plagiarism, false resources, and bias (guidance p.~140). *[Recommendation]*
- **Information and media literacy** as the critical-thinking backbone, guiding "staff and students in critically examining AI-generated results" (guidance p.~162). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Audiences are "K-12 educators, librarians, students, and administrators," plus "Library Staff and Patrons" (guidance pp.~118, 158). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope spans K-12 schools and public libraries, explicitly extending to lifelong and community learning (guidance p.~129). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Wisconsin delegates specifics to districts and libraries and offers a policy-consideration checklist rather than a permission instrument (guidance p.~165). *[Recommendation / not a scale]*
- The guidance's operative structure is a set of policy considerations (below) plus professional-development pillars, not a graduated student-use ladder. *[Structural note]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **A policy-considerations checklist anchors governance** (guidance p.~165): Code of Conduct and Academic Integrity Policies, Digital Citizenship Standards, Transparency and Consent, Data Encryption and Storage, Copyright Policies and Citation Guidelines, Digital Equity and Student Access, FERPA and COPPA compliance, Vendor Compliance, and Continuous Monitoring. *[Recommendation]*
- **Data privacy and security have detailed key takeaways**: restrict student-data access, remove PII from datasets, define retention periods, maintain a breach-response plan, and comply with accessibility standards (guidance p.~178). *[Recommendation]*
- **Digital equity and student access** are named policy areas (guidance p.~165). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is a listed policy area** tied to code-of-conduct and citation guidelines (guidance p.~165). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** the document does not center AI detectors. Its integrity approach is policy-, citation-, and media-literacy-based rather than detection-based (guidance pp.~162, 165). *[Not emphasized]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **The 5 Training Pillars structure professional development** for both K-12 and public-library faculty (guidance pp.204-207). Each pillar names concrete tools, e.g., Teachable Machine and Machine Learning for Kids under Hands-On Experiences (guidance p.~225). *[Recommendation / instrument]*
- **Curriculum integration is cross-disciplinary.** AI should be integrated "into various subject areas, not just computer science," from math to social studies to science (guidance p.~218). *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation favors starting small.** "Start small: Begin by implementing AI in a controlled setting before a full-scale integration" (guidance p.~278). *[Recommendation]*
- **Wisconsin-specific professional networks** support ongoing learning: SLATE, the CESA Instructional Technology Services Committee, WEMTA (supporting "librarians and educators"), and the Wisconsin Digital Learning Collaborative (guidance pp.~233-239). *[Example]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Wisconsin's offloading protection runs through its human-irreplaceability principle and its media-literacy emphasis rather than a dedicated section.

- **The human component is irreplaceable.** Teachers' and librarians' ability "to inspire, motivate, and respond to individual needs" cannot be replaced by AI (guidance p.~136). *[Recommendation]*
- **Critical examination of AI output is the core habit.** The guidance promotes "information and media literacy efforts to guide staff and students in critically examining AI-generated results" (guidance p.~162). This librarian-led framing is Wisconsin's distinctive cognitive safeguard. *[Recommendation]*
- **Foundational AI understanding precedes use.** Pillar 1 gives educators "foundational knowledge about AI and machine learning, including how these technologies work, their capabilities, and their limitations" (guidance p.~213). *[Recommendation]*
- **Ethical reflection is built in.** AI use "presents opportunities to reflect on ethical concerns, such as plagiarism, false resources, and biases" (guidance p.~140). *[Instructional guidance]*
- Wisconsin does not include an explicit over-reliance/"productive struggle" section or grade-band gating; its cognitive protection is media literacy plus the human-irreplaceability principle. *[Structural note]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Wisconsin graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and district/library-implemented, exposure varies.

- **Governed by district policy, not a state scale**, shaped by the state's policy-considerations checklist (guidance p.~165). *Basis: guidance p.~165.*
- **A media- and information-literacy orientation** toward critically examining AI output, if their teachers or librarians followed the guidance (guidance p.~162). *Basis: guidance p.~162.*
- **Possible exposure to hands-on AI tools** (Teachable Machine, Machine Learning for Kids, TensorFlow Playground) where educators used the pillars (guidance p.~225). *Basis: guidance p.~225.*
- **Familiarity with AI as a cross-disciplinary topic**, not only in computer science (guidance p.~218). *Basis: guidance p.~218.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Wisconsin set no state scale, so faculty should expect district variance and no shared leveled vocabulary, but possibly a stronger-than-average media-literacy habit given the librarian lens.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Wisconsin's distinctive contribution is reframing AI literacy as *lifelong and community-wide*, delivered partly through public libraries (guidance pp.~110, 129). This is not a university-governance hook, but it means incoming Wisconsin students grew up in a state that treated AI literacy as an adult and community capability, and academic and public libraries are positioned as continuing partners.
- **Higher-education implication:** The guidance's *media- and information-literacy* emphasis (guidance p.~162), driven by librarian authors, may leave Wisconsin students better prepared than average to critically evaluate AI output. University librarians can build directly on that foundation.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because Wisconsin set no state scale, incoming students are unlikely to share a leveled AI-use vocabulary. Faculty should specify per-assignment AI expectations explicitly.
- **Higher-education implication:** Wisconsin taught AI as a *cross-disciplinary* topic (guidance p.~218), so students may expect AI to appear across subjects rather than confined to computing courses.
- **Higher-education implication:** Wisconsin's guidance is silent on AI detectors, so students' prior experience with detection tools is unknown and district-dependent. Institutions should not assume a fixed prior stance.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Leverage libraries:** Because Wisconsin frames AI literacy through libraries (guidance p.~136), university and academic libraries are natural partners for AI-literacy onboarding, continuing the model incoming students experienced.
- **Media-literacy continuity:** Build on the critical-examination-of-AI-output habit Wisconsin students may share (guidance p.~162), extending it to disciplinary source evaluation and citation.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; district/library variance is high and no state scale anchors expectations.
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment; Wisconsin students share no statewide scale or vocabulary.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor policy-, citation-, and media-literacy-based integrity, matching the state's approach (guidance pp.~162, 165); set your own detector policy explicitly, since Wisconsin took no position.

## Unique Aspects

- **An explicit K-12-and-library, lifelong-learning scope**, including public libraries and community learning, with a School Library Consultant and a Public Library Consultant among the named authors (guidance pp.~30, 129). This library/lifelong-learning framing is unique in the dataset.
- **The 5 Training Pillars for K12 Educators** (Understanding AI/ML Basics, Integrating AI into Subject Areas, Hands-On AI Experiences, Collaboration and Networking, Continuous Learning and Adaptability), each with named tools (guidance pp.207-238).
- **A librarian-led media- and information-literacy emphasis** as the core critical-thinking safeguard (guidance p.~162).
- **Wisconsin-specific professional networks** named as implementation partners (SLATE, CESA/CITSC, WEMTA, WDLC) (guidance pp.~233-239).
- **A detailed policy-considerations checklist** covering conduct, privacy, equity, copyright, and vendor compliance (guidance p.~165).
- **A cross-disciplinary integration stance**, AI "not just computer science" (guidance p.~218).
