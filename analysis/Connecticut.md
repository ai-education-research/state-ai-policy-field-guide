## Executive Summary

Connecticut's guidance is a compact, advisory overview issued by the Connecticut Commission for Educational Technology rather than a detailed classroom framework. It orients readers to what AI is (six categories), where it shows up in schools (thirteen educational arenas), how to select and use software responsibly (five best practices), and what risks to weigh (a risk-category list) (content.md, Sections 2-4). Its most higher-education-relevant feature is that it names **"Social and Emotional"** risk as a distinct risk category alongside academic and legal risk (content.md, Section 4).

The document is light on instruments. It contains no student-use permission scale, no grade-band progression, and no cognitive-offloading treatment in the transcribed text. Much of its substantive guidance points outward to linked documents (a CABE sample board policy, the state's Responsible AI Policy Framework, and federal resources) rather than prescribing practice itself (content.md, Section 5 links). For a higher-education reader, the honest takeaway is that Connecticut sets a light-touch, advisory baseline, so incoming Connecticut students will have experienced whatever their districts built on top of it rather than a uniform state model.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Connecticut's explicit naming of "Social and Emotional" as a distinct AI risk category (content.md, Section 4) suggests some students were taught to consider AI's wellbeing effects, not only its academic-integrity effects. Institutions can connect AI onboarding to student wellbeing and counseling supports rather than framing AI purely as an integrity issue.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because Connecticut's state guidance is advisory and instrument-light, incoming Connecticut students are less likely than students from instrument-heavy states to arrive with a shared per-assignment AI-use vocabulary. A college should teach its AI expectations explicitly rather than assume prior structure.
- **Higher-education implication:** Connecticut points districts to a state Responsible AI Policy Framework (OPM) and a CABE sample board policy (content.md, Section 5). Institutions in Connecticut may find governance alignment easier by referencing those same state frameworks.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Onboarding:** Given the light state baseline, run an explicit AI-literacy orientation and do not assume prior instrument exposure.
- **Assessment design and academic integrity:** Introduce a clear, explicit per-assignment AI-permission structure, since Connecticut students are unlikely to arrive with one.
- **Support services:** Build on Connecticut's social-emotional risk framing by linking AI guidance to student wellbeing resources.
- **Governance:** Reference the Connecticut Responsible AI Policy Framework and CABE sample policy (content.md, Section 5) for continuity with what Connecticut districts were pointed to.
- **AI tool availability and accessibility:** Provide sanctioned tools and address the "Equity of Access" risk the state names (Section 4).
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Because the state document offers no learning-preservation model, the institution should supply one (for example explicit productive-struggle and reasoning-defense expectations).
