**Nature of the documents (read before everything below):** Oklahoma is a two-instrument state, and the distinction between them is central. The OSDE guidance (v2.0) is **voluntary guidance** that contains the AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale; the scale is an adaptable instrument, not a rule. SB 1734, by contrast, is **enacted law** with genuine, binding requirements on districts. Where I cite the guidance, claims are Recommendation-level. Where I cite SB 1734, claims are Requirement-level. This is the anchor state for the project's Perkins et al. framing, because the scale's AIAS lineage is printed on the primary document.

## Executive Summary

Oklahoma pairs the dataset's clearest Perkins-lineage student-use scale with a substantive enacted statute. The OSDE guidance (v2.0, July 2025) carries the "AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale," a five-level (0-4) instrument running from Level 0 NO AI Use to Level 4 Full AI Use with Human Oversight, each level pairing a use description with explicit disclosure requirements. Its attribution line reads: "Adapted by Dr. Karen Leonard for the Oklahoma State Department of Education (OSDE) from the work of Dr. Leon Furze, Dr. Mike Perkins, Dr. Jasper Roe FHEA, & Dr. Jason Mcvaugh" (guidance p.18). This is a primary-source-confirmed lineage to the peer-reviewed AI Assessment Scale.

SB 1734, the Oklahoma Responsible Technology in Schools Act, was signed May 12, 2026 and takes effect July 1, 2026. It is more prescriptive than most state AI legislation. It requires that classroom AI tools be "educator-directed," operate with a "human-in-the-loop," and never be "the primary basis for student grading, discipline, placement, promotion, retention, or other high-stakes educational decisions" (SB 1734, sec.1.E). It requires annual written disclosure to parents, a parental opt-out from student-facing AI tools without penalty, and district adoption of an AI policy before the 2027-2028 school year (SB 1734, secs.1.F-I).

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, the scale is directly liftable and cites the peer-reviewed source, so a faculty member can adopt the same AIAS and expect Oklahoma students to recognize the level vocabulary. Second, a distinctive design choice, the scale requires students to submit "link(s) to AI chat(s)" as disclosure at Levels 1-4 (guidance p.18), meaning Oklahoma students may arrive habituated to documenting their AI interactions. Third, from 2027-28, Oklahoma law bars AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions, a norm students may expect to carry into college.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human-centered AI.** "AI augments, never replaces, human instruction" (guidance p.3). The statute echoes this: teachers "play an essential and irreplaceable role... artificial intelligence tools, when used in classrooms, should supplement educator-led instruction, not supplant it" (SB 1734, sec.1.A). *[Recommendation / Requirement-level finding of the Legislature]*
- **Clarity, accountability, and consistency with preserved local control.** SB 1734's stated purpose is to set "principles and guardrails," protect student data and integrity, preserve "local control and educator discretion," and support transparency (SB 1734, sec.1.B). *[Requirement]*
- **Workforce and national alignment.** The guidance grounds itself in Governor Stitt's Emerging Technologies Task Force and the 2025 federal executive order on AI education for youth (guidance p.3). *[Recommendation / framing]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The guidance addresses PK-12 schools statewide (guidance p.3). *[Scope statement]*
- SB 1734 binds all public school districts and defines classroom AI tools, student-facing AI tools, educator-directed use, and human-in-the-loop (SB 1734, sec.1.C). *[Requirement scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **The AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale (guidance p.18) is the permission instrument**, offered as an adaptable classroom framework. Its five levels, verbatim in substance:
  - **Level 0 NO AI Use** — "completed entirely without AI assistance. AI MAY NOT be used at any point... students rely solely on their own knowledge." Disclosure: none required; may require an academic-honesty pledge that AI was not used. *[Example / instrument]*
  - **Level 1 AI-Assisted Idea Generation and Structuring** — "No AI content is allowed in the final submission. AI can be used... for brainstorming, creating structures, and generating ideas." Disclosure: statement of how AI was used, plus links to AI chats. *[Example]*
  - **Level 2 AI-Assisted Editing** — "No new content can be created using AI. AI can be used to make improvements to the clarity or quality of student-created work." Disclosure: statement plus AI chat links. *[Example]*
  - **Level 3 AI for Specified Task Completion** — "AI is used to complete certain elements of the task, as specified by the teacher... requires critical engagement." Disclosure: all AI-created content cited, plus AI chat links. *[Example]*
  - **Level 4 Full AI Use with Human Oversight** — "Students may use AI throughout the activity... AI should be a 'co-pilot' to enhance human creativity." Disclosure: cite AI use, plus AI chat links. *[Example]*
- **SB 1734 sets binding use guardrails** whenever AI is used (sec.1.E): educator-directed deployment, human-in-the-loop review of outputs before use, and a prohibition on AI as "the primary basis for student grading, discipline, placement, promotion, retention, or other high-stakes educational decisions." Student-facing tools must be age- and developmentally appropriate. *[Requirement]*
- **Prohibited misuse.** The guidance requires "strict prohibition on using AI to create or distribute deepfakes or non-consensual imagery" and to impersonate others for bullying or harassment (guidance p.19). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Data privacy is both recommended and required.** SB 1734 mandates FERPA compliance, recognized security standards, and that districts "take reasonable steps to minimize the amount of student data shared" (SB 1734, sec.1.E.5). *[Requirement]*
- **Mandatory annual parental disclosure.** Districts must disclose, at least annually, all AI tools in use, their vendors, the categories of student data collected, the extent of sharing, and each tool's educational purpose (SB 1734, sec.1.F). *[Requirement]*
- **A parental opt-out with anti-penalty protection.** Parents may opt a student out of student-facing AI tools "at any time," and opt-out students "shall not be academically penalized or denied access to core instructional content" (SB 1734, sec.1.G). *[Requirement]*
- **Academic integrity is process- and disclosure-based.** The guidance directs updating integrity policies for AI, teaching "proper attribution," and designing assessments that "value process over final product" (guidance p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** cautionary. "While AI detection tools exist, they are not always reliable and may produce false positives, incorrectly flagging human-written work as AI-generated. These tools also frequently miss sophisticated AI-generated content. Instead of relying on detection software, educators should establish baseline writing samples" (guidance p.18). *[Recommendation — a caution against detectors]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Roles and responsibilities are enumerated** in the guidance (boards approve AI policies and acceptable-use guidelines, p.14) and, from 2027-28, required by statute. *[Recommendation + Requirement]*
- **SB 1734 mandates district AI policies before the 2027-2028 school year** with minimum contents: identify responsible roles, address instructional and prohibited uses, data protection and minimization, transparency to families, periodic review, and compliance with the statute's guardrails and OSDE guidance (SB 1734, sec.1.I). *[Requirement]*
- **OSDE is statutorily directed to develop guidance** (SB 1734, sec.1.H); the v2.0 guidance is that instrument. *[Requirement satisfied by guidance]*
- **Assessment guidance favors process** over product, with baseline writing samples and process conversations (guidance p.18). *[Instructional guidance]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Oklahoma builds offloading protection into both the scale's design and its integrity guidance.

- **Level 0 preserves unaided cognition by definition.** It "ensures that students rely solely on their own knowledge, understanding, and skills" (guidance p.18). *[Example / instrument]*
- **The scale keeps students as active participants.** Its rationale is to ensure "students remain active participants in the learning process, not passive recipients of machine-generated content" (guidance p.17). *[Recommendation]*
- **Per-level human-oversight requirements** at Levels 3-4 make the student "responsible for providing human oversight and evaluation of all AI-generated content" (guidance p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assessment redesign toward process** is the integrity mitigation: "Develop assessment methods that value process over final product" and "Implement project designs that make inappropriate AI use less beneficial" (guidance p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **SB 1734 legally protects high-stakes judgment from automation.** AI "shall not be used as the primary basis for student grading, discipline, placement, promotion, retention" (SB 1734, sec.1.E.3), keeping consequential decisions human. *[Requirement]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits an Oklahoma graduate *may* bring. The guidance scale is voluntary, but SB 1734 raises the statutory floor for cohorts under it (2027-28 onward).

- **Familiarity with a five-level, AIAS-derived permission scale.** A student in an adopting school will recognize levels from "NO AI Use" to "Full AI Use with Human Oversight" (guidance p.18). *Basis: guidance p.18, adaptable instrument.*
- **A strong disclosure-and-documentation habit**, specifically submitting links to AI chats alongside work at Levels 1-4 (guidance p.18). This is more granular than most states' disclosure norms. *Basis: guidance p.18.*
- **An expectation that AI cannot drive high-stakes decisions** about their grades, placement, or discipline (SB 1734, sec.1.E.3). *Basis: SB 1734, for cohorts under the statute.*
- **An expectation of parental disclosure and opt-out rights** around student-facing AI (SB 1734, secs.1.F-G). *Basis: SB 1734.*
- **An awareness that AI detectors are unreliable**, if their teachers followed the guidance (guidance p.18). *Basis: guidance p.18.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat the scale as a ceiling of possibility (voluntary) but the SB 1734 guardrails as a firmer floor for recent cohorts. The chat-link disclosure habit is distinctive enough that Oklahoma students may expect to show their AI work in a way students from other states do not.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Oklahoma's scale cites the peer-reviewed AIAS source (Furze, Perkins, Roe, McVaugh; guidance p.18). A university can adopt the same source and expect adopting-school students to recognize the 0-4 vocabulary, from "NO AI Use" to "Full AI Use with Human Oversight," rather than starting from zero.
- **Higher-education implication:** Oklahoma students may arrive habituated to *submitting links to their AI chats* as disclosure (guidance p.18). Faculty who want process transparency can build on that habit, and faculty who ban AI should know these students were taught that disclosure, not concealment, is the norm.
- **Higher-education implication:** Under SB 1734, Oklahoma students (2027-28 onward) were legally protected from AI making high-stakes decisions about them (sec.1.E.3). They may expect the same human accountability in college and may react against automated grading or placement.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught that *AI detectors are unreliable and produce false positives* (guidance p.18). Institutions relying on detection software may face students trained to distrust it.
- **Higher-education implication:** The guidance recommends that districts "develop partnerships with local industry and higher education" (guidance, roles section, p.~15). Oklahoma colleges may be invited into that pipeline.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission scale, ideally the same Furze/Perkins/Roe/McVaugh AIAS source (guidance p.18), for direct continuity. Preserve Level 0 NO-AI assessments where core-skill demonstration is the goal.
- **Disclosure practice:** Consider adopting Oklahoma's chat-link disclosure convention (guidance p.18) for AI-permitted assignments, since incoming students may already practice it.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor process- and disclosure-based integrity, mirroring the guidance (guidance p.18). Do not rely on AI detectors, which the students' own K-12 guidance calls unreliable.
- **Human-accountability alignment:** Recognize that Oklahoma students under SB 1734 expect human decision-makers for consequential judgments (sec.1.E.3); keep automated tooling out of high-stakes calls or disclose it clearly.
- **Onboarding and partnership:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry, and where invited, join district AI workgroups and industry/higher-ed partnerships the guidance envisions.

## Unique Aspects

- **The AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale**, a five-level (0-4) student instrument with primary-printed AIAS attribution to Furze/Perkins/Roe/McVaugh (guidance p.18). The clearest anchor in the dataset for the Perkins-spine instruments library.
- **Per-level disclosure requirements, including submitting links to AI chats** (guidance p.18), a granular transparency mechanism few states specify.
- **An enacted, prescriptive statute (SB 1734)** with binding guardrails: educator-directed use, human-in-the-loop review, a ban on AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions, mandatory parental disclosure, and a no-penalty parental opt-out.
- **A statutory policy-adoption mandate** before the 2027-2028 school year with enumerated minimum contents (SB 1734, sec.1.I).
- **A cross-state attribution artifact.** The guidance's "Dr. Jason Mcvaugh" spelling matches Wyoming's, suggesting a shared intermediate source for the adapted scale, useful for the cross-state comparison. *[Cross-state note]*
- **A firm anti-detector position** with a process-based alternative (guidance p.18).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **The scale is voluntary; the statute is binding, but recent.** The AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale is offered for adoption, so actual classroom use is district-dependent. SB 1734's requirements bind districts but take effect July 1, 2026, with policy adoption due before 2027-28, so the fullest effect postdates current graduates. *[Timing]*
- **SB 1734 explicitly does not require AI use.** "Nothing in this section shall be construed to require a public school district... to use artificial intelligence in any school" (sec.1.J). The statute governs AI *if used*; it does not guarantee any student encountered AI. *[Scope of statute]*
- **The scale's graphic was extracted as text, not image.** The level descriptions and disclosure columns are verbatim from guidance p.18, but the printable poster and its CC-licensed template link were not rendered as an image. *[Partial capture]*
- **Some page citations in the roles/higher-ed area are approximate.** The "partnerships with local industry and higher education" line is confirmed in the guidance's roles-and-responsibilities region; exact pagination for that bullet was not pinned. *[Minor]*
