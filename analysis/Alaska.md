**Nature of the document.** The framework states its own status plainly: it offers "recommendations and considerations rather than strict mandates" (Alaska-K12-AI-Framework.pdf, p.3). Every substantive item below is therefore a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless noted. No statewide mandate exists in the provided file.

## Executive Summary

Alaska's framework is a values-and-governance document organized around **seven guiding principles**: Human-Centered, Fair Access, Transparency, Oversight, Security, Ethical Use, and Cultural Responsiveness (Alaska-K12-AI-Framework.pdf, p.4). Its governing stance is a deliberate middle path: "an outright ban impedes skill and career development, while unrestricted access falls short of needed protections" (p.3). Its most distinctive feature is that **Cultural Responsiveness is a first-class principle**, requiring that AI tools "affirm, rather than diminish, the diverse cultures of Alaska" and aligning with the Alaska Standards for Culturally Responsive Schools (p.11).

The framework was co-developed with an advisory group that includes higher-education voices (an Assistant Professor from the UAA College of Engineering and the Executive Director of ANSEP) (p.1). It is explicit that AI should "augment human capabilities, critical thinking, and creativity, never replacing human judgment" (p.3), and it discloses that the document itself "was drafted with the assistance of artificial intelligence" (p.12). Because it is recommendation-level, actual student experience depends on district uptake. The document itself points to varied district practice (Fairbanks, Mat-Su, Wrangell, Anchorage) rather than a uniform state standard (p.8).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human augmentation over replacement.** "AI must augment human capabilities, critical thinking, and creativity, never replacing human judgment or decision-making... All AI use should begin with human inquiry and culminate in human reflection and insight" (Alaska-K12-AI-Framework.pdf, p.4, Human-Centered principle). *[Recommendation / stated principle]*
- **A balanced anti-ban, anti-free-for-all stance** (p.3). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity across a vast, rural, connectivity-challenged state** is a stated first-order objective (p.6, Fair Access). *[Recommendation]*
- **Vision:** "To empower every Alaskan student and educator to ethically and effectively engage with Artificial Intelligence, fostering critical thinking, creativity, and responsible digital citizenship" (p.1). *[Stated vision]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- "Designed to serve as a flexible resource for school boards, administrators, educators, and students" (p.3). The How-to-Use section assigns each audience a use: boards use the Guiding Principles for policy, administrators use the key areas of focus for implementation and PD, educators use it for classroom practice (p.3). *[Scope]*
- Applies to "all students, teachers, staff, administrators, and third parties who develop, implement, or interact with AI technologies... where permitted by local and state policy," covering generative AI, intelligent tutoring systems, conversational agents, automation, and analytics tools (p.3). *[Scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **Permissive-with-oversight default.** Districts "should move beyond outright bans and instead develop a balanced approach" (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- **Restriction on high-stakes automation:** "Robust human oversight is mandatory for any decisions that directly impact students, such as grading or disciplinary actions" (p.8, Oversight principle). *[Recommendation, phrased as imperative within a voluntary document]*
- **Prohibition guidance on data:** "Explicit prohibitions against entering sensitive student data, PII, or confidential information into publicly accessible AI tools are essential" (p.9, Security). The "Do's and Don'ts" suggest using anonymized data in prompts and "never uploading documents containing confidential school or district information" (p.9). *[Recommendation / model rule]*
- A printed student-use permission scale is **referenced but not reproduced**: "Districts can utilize an AI Acceptable Use Scale to build clear expectations for AI use on specific assignments" (p.10). The scale's levels are **Not found in the provided documents**. *[Unresolved]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Security** is a dedicated principle: cybersecurity, PII protection, and compliance with "the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), and the Children's Internet Protection Act (CIPA)" (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Fair Access / equity:** addresses "the digital divide, particularly concerning equitable broadband access and device availability in Alaska's diverse and often remote regions," referencing a 2023 DEED survey of device access and connectivity (p.6). *[Recommendation]*
- **Ethics / academic integrity:** "Submitting AI-generated work as original without proper citation is considered plagiarism"; policies should require disclosure and citation (p.7 Transparency; p.10 Ethical Use). *[Recommendation]*
- **Accessibility** as an explicit assistive-tech requirement comparable to some states is **Not found in the provided documents** beyond general equity framing. *[Limited]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Educator autonomy is emphasized:** "Educators must have the autonomy to make professional decisions regarding AI use in their classrooms. Districts should support educators in updating syllabi and classroom policies to include AI integrity guidelines" (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy for all** staff and students: "basic AI principles and applications," "recognize when AI is employed and an awareness of its limits," and "ethical and responsible AI use, including topics of safety, bias, and disinformation" (p.6). *[Recommendation]*
- **Curriculum:** AI aligns with existing Alaska Digital Literacy Standards (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- **Effective prompting frameworks** named: EVERY ("Evaluated, Verify, Edit, Revise, You are responsible") and PREP ("Prompt, Role, Explicit Information, Parameters") (p.10). *[Instructional guidance / examples]*
- **Implementation supports:** the framework points to a companion resource website with policy roadmaps, classroom strategies, and student guides (p.5), and provides ready templates (sample district board policy, school handbook language, family/community AI FAQ) (p.7). *[Recommendation / examples]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

- The Human-Centered principle is the core anti-offloading commitment: AI "never replacing human judgment," with "all AI use" beginning "with human inquiry" and culminating "in human reflection and insight" (Alaska-K12-AI-Framework.pdf, p.4). *[Recommendation]*
- The EVERY prompting framework builds user accountability directly into practice, ending in "You are responsible" (p.10). *[Instructional guidance]*
- The Research and Evidence Base section directs districts to assess AI's impact on "student engagement, motivation, cognition, and deep learning" and notes "robust, large-scale independent evidence on AI's effectiveness in education is still limited" (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- "Rethinking Education" frames AI as a lever for "problem-solving, innovative design, and creative expression" rather than task completion (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- **A named, developed treatment of cognitive offloading, metacognition, or over-reliance comparable to New Mexico's is Not found in the provided documents.** Alaska's guard is the human-in-the-loop principle plus the accountability-oriented prompting frameworks, rather than an explicit cognitive-science section. *[Evidence gap]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

- **Uneven by district.** Because the framework is recommendation-level and the document itself lists divergent district policies (Fairbanks, Mat-Su, Wrangell, Anchorage, p.8), a single incoming Alaska cohort will vary widely in AI exposure. *Basis: p.3 (recommendations not mandates); p.8 (district examples).*
- **Where districts adopted the framework's posture:** students may have experienced disclosure-and-citation expectations for AI use (p.7, p.10), teacher-set per-assignment expectations (possibly via an AI Acceptable Use Scale, p.10), and AI-literacy instruction covering AI's limits, bias, and disinformation (p.6).
- **Exposure to prompting frameworks** (EVERY, PREP) where educators taught them (p.10).
- **Higher-education implication:** Assume high variance and rural/connectivity effects. Some Alaska students, especially from remote regions, may have had limited device or broadband access to AI tools despite the framework's equity aims (p.6), so prior hands-on AI experience cannot be assumed uniform.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Alaska students from adopting districts may arrive expecting AI use to be governed per assignment with disclosure and citation, rather than banned outright (p.5, p.10). A silent or blanket-ban syllabus will contrast with that experience.
- **Higher-education implication:** The Cultural Responsiveness principle (p.11) means some Alaska students, particularly Alaska Native students, may have been taught to interrogate AI tools for cultural bias, misappropriation, and exclusion of local context. Institutions serving these students should expect and support that critical stance rather than treat AI as culturally neutral.
- **Higher-education implication:** Alaska's framework was co-authored with higher-education partners (UAA College of Engineering, ANSEP, p.1). In-state institutions may already be connected to the K-12 AI-readiness effort and could formalize that pipeline.
- **Higher-education implication:** The equity/connectivity emphasis (p.6) signals that "bring your own AI access" assumptions will disadvantage rural Alaska students. Institutions should provide sanctioned, no-cost AI access rather than assume students arrive with paid tools and reliable bandwidth.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **AI tool availability and equity:** Provide institution-sanctioned, no-cost AI access with reliable infrastructure, since Alaska's own framework treats access as an equity precondition (p.6).
- **Onboarding:** Run an entry AI-literacy check keyed to the framework's own literacy definition (principles, recognizing AI and its limits, ethical use, p.6), given high inter-district variance.
- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission approach continuous with the "AI Acceptable Use Scale" idea Alaska districts were pointed to (p.10). Pair assignments with disclosure-and-citation expectations (p.7).
- **Academic integrity:** Mirror the disclosure-and-attribution model rather than a detection regime, matching what adopting Alaska districts taught (p.7, p.10).
- **Accessibility and cultural responsiveness:** Extend the Cultural Responsiveness principle (p.11) into course and tool selection, especially for Alaska Native students, and involve community/cultural expertise in AI-integrated course design.
- **Governance:** Use the seven principles and the "level of human oversight the tool requires and allows" evaluation criterion (p.8) as a ready scaffold for institutional AI tool vetting.
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Adopt the human-inquiry-to-human-reflection framing (p.4) and the accountability-ending EVERY prompting framework (p.10) for continuity from high school to college.

## Unique Aspects

- **Cultural Responsiveness as a named guiding principle** (p.4, p.11), requiring AI to "affirm, rather than diminish, the diverse cultures of Alaska," aligning with the Alaska Standards for Culturally Responsive Schools, and engaging elders and community members. Distinctive in naming a specific cultural community rather than gesturing at diversity abstractly.
- **Explicit balanced stance** rejecting both outright bans and unrestricted access (p.3).
- **Named prompting frameworks** EVERY and PREP with user accountability built in (p.10).
- **AI Use Disclaimer** (p.12): the framework discloses it was itself drafted with Gemini and ChatGPT, modeling the transparency it asks of others.
- **Higher-education co-authorship** via the advisory group (UAA College of Engineering, ANSEP) (p.1).
