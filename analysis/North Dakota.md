## Executive Summary

North Dakota's guidance is a recommendation-level, developmentally organized web framework with a distinctive delivery model. Instead of a single narrative PDF and a student-use scale, it pairs a compact grade-band guidance page with five role-based implementation checklists, School Leadership, Educators, Business and IT, Policy/Ethical/Legal, and Public Outreach. The framework frames AI plainly as "the latest in a long and continuing list of tools" for teaching, operations, and personalized learning (content.md).

Its instructional heart is a four-band developmental progression, K-2, 3-5, 6-8, and 9-12, with terse but pointed guidance for each. K-2 students "need to understand that AI is not a real person." Grades 3-5 guidance warns that "an overreliance... can short-circuit students' development of problem-solving skills." Grades 6-8 should "critique AI outputs." Grades 9-12 guidance says, notably, "Rather than police AI use, teach students about how AI works and its limitations" (content.md). This is a smaller, plainer instrument than the scale-building states use, and North Carolina or New Mexico offer far more elaborate structures, but its cognitive-offloading and developmental cautions are stated crisply.

For a higher-education reader, two facts matter. First, North Dakota set no state AI-permission scale and no prohibition list, so a graduate's operative rules came from a local policy. Second, the framework contains no higher-education hook. No university, college, or postsecondary partnership appears anywhere in the captured text or the five checklists. This is a K-12-internal document.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI is framed as one more instructional tool**, not a rupture. "Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the latest in a long and continuing list of tools that can be used to transform teaching, streamline business operations, and personalize learning" (content.md). *[Recommendation / framing]*
- **Developmental appropriateness is the organizing principle**, expressed through four grade bands (content.md). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity and inclusion recur as objectives.** The leadership checklist stresses that AI "has the potential to address inequities. Without this focus, it can intensify existing inequities and make school more difficult for already marginalized students" (Checklist-School-Leadership). *[Recommendation]*
- **Local control governs adoption.** The framework is a springboard for district policy, not a rule (sources.md; content.md). *[Scope statement]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The framework is K-12 and role-segmented. Its five checklists address School Leadership, Educators, Business and IT, Policy/Ethical/Legal, and Public Outreach separately (files). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is classroom, operational, and community-facing, the Public Outreach checklist targets parents and the public with events and take-home guides (Checklist-Public-Outreach). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Permitted and prohibited uses are delegated to districts. Leadership is told to "help educators determine when, where, and how AI tools can be used on assignments" (Checklist-School-Leadership), and educators to "determine when and how AI tools can be used" (Checklist-Educators). *[Recommendation / not a mandate]*
- **The high-school posture is explicitly anti-policing.** "Rather than police AI use, teach students about how AI works and its limitations, including the effects of bias, stereotypes, and inaccuracies" (content.md). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **A dedicated Policy/Ethical/Legal checklist anchors compliance.** It asks whether tools comply with FERPA and HIPAA and local privacy policies, how they protect and communicate breaches of student and staff data, and how copyright, licensing, and plagiarism will be handled (Checklist-Policy-Ethical-Legal). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level law]*
- **Accessibility is a named section** ("AI for Accessibility"), and educators are prompted to modify lessons "to make them more accessible to all students (special needs, EL, etc.)" (Checklist-Educators; Checklist-School-Leadership). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity of access to tools** is explicit, including attention to "free and paid versions of tools" and whether "all students have equal access to tools both in and out of school" (Checklist-Educators). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is handled by assignment redesign and disclosure.** Educators should "rethink assignments and set clear guidelines on what standards/skills are being addressed" and decide "how you will require students to reference" AI-generated research (Checklist-Educators). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** neutral, not a caution. The educators checklist notes only that "the internet has multiple tools to help educators recognize original or AI generated content" (Checklist-Educators). It neither endorses nor warns against detectors, unlike the anti-detector states. *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Curriculum integration is developmental**, following the grade bands, with critique of AI outputs beginning in middle school (content.md). *[Recommendation]*
- **Professional development is a leadership checklist item**, prompting districts to assess PD needs for administrative staff, classroom staff, and "IT staff, media specialists, and librarians" (Checklist-School-Leadership). *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation favors piloting.** "It may be helpful to start with a single school or grade level/band before a districtwide roll-out," followed by feedback and review of whether learning goals were met (Checklist-School-Leadership). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assessment guidance is process-oriented.** Educators should "support students in sharing their writing processes" and "create opportunities for students to problem solve, analyze, and synthesize results" (Checklist-Educators). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Librarians and media specialists are explicitly named** on the recommended AI-policy team (Checklist-School-Leadership), a distinctive inclusion. *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

North Dakota states the offloading concern directly in its grade-band text, which is unusual for so compact a document.

- **The upper-elementary band names offloading as the core risk.** "Technology, powered by AI, can be good at answering questions, but an overreliance on that kind of technology can short-circuit students' development of problem-solving skills" (content.md, grades 3-5). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **The K-2 band protects against anthropomorphizing.** Young students "need to understand that AI is not a real person," because at that developmental stage they "are more likely to attribute human qualities to artificially intelligent technologies like smart speakers and chatbots" (content.md). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **The middle-school band builds critique as the mitigation.** Students should "ask a generative AI chatbot to answer a question or write an essay and then critique it, looking for factual errors and the like" (content.md, grades 6-8). *[Instructional guidance / example]*
- **The high-school band substitutes understanding for surveillance.** Teaching "how AI works and its limitations" replaces policing (content.md, grades 9-12). *[Recommendation]*
- **The educators checklist reinforces synthesis over generation.** It asks teachers to "create opportunities for students to problem solve, analyze, and synthesize results" and to help students "critically analyze results" (Checklist-Educators). *[Instructional guidance]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a North Dakota graduate *may* bring. Because the framework is voluntary and district-implemented, exposure varies.

- **Governed by a district policy, not a state scale.** A North Dakota student's AI rules came from a local policy the framework encouraged (content.md). *Basis: content.md, sources.md.*
- **A developmental trajectory from "AI is not a real person" to critique to understanding-not-policing**, where teachers followed the grade bands (content.md). *Basis: content.md.*
- **A habit of critiquing AI output for factual errors and bias**, if their middle- and high-school teachers used the recommended exercises (content.md). *Basis: content.md.*
- **Some experience sharing their writing process** as an integrity practice (Checklist-Educators). *Basis: Checklist-Educators.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. North Dakota's framework is compact and district-adopted, so faculty should expect uneven exposure and no shared leveled vocabulary.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** North Dakota provides no higher-education hook. No college, university, or postsecondary partnership appears in the framework or its checklists, so a faculty member should not expect an institutional pipeline or shared K-16 vocabulary from the state document.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught to *understand rather than be policed* on AI (content.md, grades 9-12). Incoming North Dakota students may respond better to AI policies framed as literacy and responsibility than to surveillance-first enforcement.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because the state set no permission scale, incoming students are unlikely to share a leveled AI-use vocabulary. Faculty should specify per-assignment AI expectations explicitly.
- **Higher-education implication:** The state emphasized *critiquing AI output for factual errors and bias* from middle school onward (content.md). This verification habit is a foundation universities can build on toward source evaluation and citation.
- **Higher-education implication:** North Dakota did not adopt an anti-detector stance (Checklist-Educators). Unlike students from the anti-detector states, North Dakota graduates were not necessarily taught to distrust detection tools, so their expectations on that point are unknown.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry. District-level variance will be high and there is no state scale to anchor assumptions.
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment. Preserve tasks that require independent problem-solving, mirroring the state's grades 3-5 offloading caution (content.md).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor process-based integrity (sharing writing processes) that continues the state's approach (Checklist-Educators). Do not assume any particular prior stance on detectors.
- **Literacy framing:** Lean into the "understand, don't police" posture North Dakota students may have absorbed (content.md), extending their critique habit toward disciplinary source evaluation.

## Unique Aspects

- **A web-native framework with no single PDF.** North Dakota publishes its guidance as a page plus five downloadable checklists rather than a bound document (content.md, sources.md).
- **Five role-based implementation checklists**, School Leadership, Educators, Business and IT, Policy/Ethical/Legal, and Public Outreach, an operations-and-community delivery model few states match.
- **Compact but pointed grade-band cognitive cautions**, especially the K-2 "AI is not a real person" and grades 3-5 "short-circuit... problem-solving skills" language (content.md).
- **An explicit anti-policing high-school posture** ("Rather than police AI use, teach students about how AI works," content.md).
- **Librarians and media specialists named on the AI-policy team** (Checklist-School-Leadership).
- **A dedicated public-outreach instrument** with concrete events (AI literacy week, Hour of Code nights, take-home parent guides) (Checklist-Public-Outreach).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness limits every claim about students.** The framework mandates nothing and is district-implemented, so actual exposure is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **The framework is web-native and undated.** No publication date appears on the page, and no separate PDF exists; to fix an original in hand, the page should be printed to a date-stamped PDF (sources.md). *[Unresolved by nature]*
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists.** North Dakota offers grade-band guidance, not a 0-4 or 1-5 permission scale, and adapts no external scale. This is a genuine absence.
- **The grade-band text is terse.** content.md captures verbatim guidance for each band, but the framework's fuller narrative (definitions, teacher resources, implementation roadmap, accessibility section) was summarized rather than transcribed in full. The analysis rests on the captured verbatim text plus the five checklists. *[Partial capture]*
- **No statute or bill is recorded** for North Dakota in this folder. No enacted or pending AI-in-education requirement can be asserted. *[Unresolved]*
