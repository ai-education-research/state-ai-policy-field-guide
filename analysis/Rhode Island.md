**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** RIDE states its own posture plainly: "The distribution of this guidance doesn't constitute formal recommendations or regulations" (guidance p.2). **Nothing in it is a statewide mandate.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. Notably, RIDE does not author its own student-use permission scale; it reproduces North Carolina's "General Guide on Acceptable Use of AI" as an appendix and points LEAs to external examples.

## Executive Summary

Rhode Island's guidance is a recommendation-level, unusually broad document (roughly 53 pages) organized around instruction, equity, diverse learners, security, and a full "College and Career Readiness" section. It leans heavily on developmental appropriateness and on cross-state borrowing. Rather than build its own leveled scale, RIDE presents a developmental grade-band use-case table (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12) and reproduces North Carolina's acceptable-use guide in its appendix, citing NCDPI's Vera Cubero and Ashley McBride directly (guidance pp.17-18, 51).

Its instructional heart is the grade-band table of "Developmentally Appropriate Use Cases and Caveats" (guidance p.~536). K-2 students should "understand that AI is not a real person"; grades 3-5 should "avoid overreliance on AI... as this will impede students' development of problem-solving skills"; grades 9-12 should "exercise self-discipline to avoid over-reliance" and "demonstrate knowledge and skills with performance-based and in-person assessments to ensure they can perform without AI assistance" (guidance p.~536). This developmental frame closely resembles North Carolina's and North Dakota's.

For a higher-education reader, Rhode Island is distinctive for making college and career readiness an explicit section. It argues that "students will perform better in college with AI literacy skills" and that "schools who do not teach these skills are not adequately preparing students for college and career readiness" (guidance p.32). It references the New England Association of Schools and Colleges (NEASC) accreditation process, quotes an Old Dominion University professor, and treats AI-assisted college application support as a use case (guidance pp.32-33). It also foregrounds equity for Differently-Abled Students in integrity and grading.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI as a tool for higher and more equitable achievement.** RIDE's goal is to use AI "to inspire higher and more equitable levels of achievement in our classrooms" (guidance p.~96). *[Recommendation]*
- **Reimagining teaching for postsecondary and career success.** Teaching "must be reimagined to effectively prepare students for postsecondary and career success" (guidance p.~121). *[Recommendation]*
- **Developmental appropriateness** structures the instructional guidance through four grade bands (guidance p.~536). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity for historically underserved and Differently-Abled students** is a throughline (guidance pp.~204, 563). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The guidance addresses LEAs, with sections for instruction, operations, and family/community engagement (TOC). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is K-12, with a dedicated "College and Career Readiness" section bridging to postsecondary (guidance Section 9). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no RIDE-authored permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** RIDE instead offers "four examples of guidelines for AI use by students," the first being "General Guide on Acceptable Use of AI by the North Carolina Department of Public Instruction," reproduced in the appendix (guidance pp.17-18, 51). *[Recommendation / borrowed instrument]*
- **The grade-band table sets developmental use cases and caveats** (guidance p.~536), for example K-2 AI "only used under direct supervision of a teacher with district-approved AI tools," and 9-12 use for "polishing papers, brainstorming ideas, research, worked examples." *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Age gating is noted.** "Age 13 is when children can create a GenAI account with their parent's permission" (guidance p.~536, grades 6-8). *[Instructional guidance]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Dedicated Security and Safety and Equity and Bias sections** anchor privacy and fairness (guidance Sections 6, 8). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity in integrity and grading is distinctive.** RIDE calls for "explicit instruction for Differently-Abled Students (DAS) on AI usage" and grading approaches "that do not penalize students for accommodations or modifications that are supported by AI" (guidance p.~563). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is LEA-defined.** "RIDE encourages LEAs to develop an academic integrity approach that makes sense in their context and culture," and it surfaces a teacher's call for "a universal policy... defining what the meaning of cheating is" (guidance pp.~563, 580). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** the guidance does not center AI detectors. Its integrity approach is policy- and citation-based, and it directs LEAs to develop their own integrity approach and to use the borrowed NC guide (guidance pp.17-18). *[Not emphasized]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Two implementation checklists** appear in the document: an "LEA Getting Started With AI Checklist" and an "AI Software Procurement" checklist (guidance appendices; sources.md). *[Recommendation / instrument]*
- **Assessment guidance favors performance-based, in-person demonstration** at the upper grades, "to ensure they can perform without AI assistance" (guidance p.~536). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Family engagement includes a Sample Parent Letter with a Spanish version** (guidance Section 11; sources.md). *[Example]*
- **Companion supports** on the landing page include a NotebookLM podcast, a custom guidance GPT, and a six-part webinar series (Oct-Nov 2025) (sources.md). *[Example]*
- **Librarians are treated as stakeholders**, quoted among the educator voices (guidance p.~1094). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Rhode Island engages over-reliance directly and repeatedly, and it names productive struggle.

- **The grade-band caveats are its primary offloading control.** Grades 3-5: "Avoid overreliance on AI for answering questions that require critical thinking, as this will impede students' development of problem-solving skills." Grades 9-12: "Students should exercise self-discipline to avoid over-reliance on AI" (guidance p.~536). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It protects productive struggle by name** (guidance p.~866) and warns that "Over-reliance on AI-generated content may lead to a false sense of understanding or inflated" confidence (guidance p.~414). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It flags reduced human interaction.** "Over-reliance on AI systems may decrease valuable human interaction" (guidance p.~698). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It preserves unaided demonstration** through performance-based, in-person assessment at 9-12 (guidance p.~536). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It teaches critique of AI output** from middle school on, students "ask a generative AI chatbot to answer a question or write an essay and then critique it, looking for errors" (guidance p.~536, grades 6-8), and assess AI text for "biases, stereotypes, and inaccuracies" at 9-12. *[Instructional guidance]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Rhode Island graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and LEA-implemented, exposure varies.

- **Governed by a district policy, likely developmentally framed**, since RIDE offered grade-band use cases rather than a state scale (guidance p.~536). *Basis: guidance p.~536.*
- **Possible familiarity with North Carolina's acceptable-use structure**, if their LEA adopted the borrowed NC guide RIDE reproduced (guidance p.51). Not a RIDE-original vocabulary. *Basis: guidance pp.17-18, 51.*
- **A developmental trajectory from "AI is not a real person" to critique to self-discipline** (guidance p.~536). *Basis: guidance p.~536.*
- **Experience with performance-based, in-person assessment** meant to demonstrate ability without AI (guidance p.~536). *Basis: guidance p.~536.*
- **A college-and-career framing of AI literacy**, if their school followed Section 9 (guidance p.32). *Basis: guidance Section 9.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Rhode Island set no state scale, so faculty should expect LEA-level variance and, where a vocabulary exists, it may be North Carolina's rather than a Rhode Island original.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Rhode Island explicitly frames AI literacy as college preparation. RIDE argues "students will perform better in college with AI literacy skills" and that failing to teach them leaves students unprepared (guidance p.32). Incoming Rhode Island students may arrive expecting AI to be treated as a college-readiness competency, not just a classroom risk.
- **Higher-education implication:** RIDE ties its work to postsecondary accreditation, referencing the NEASC process (guidance p.~1106) and quoting an Old Dominion University professor (guidance p.~391). Rhode Island institutions and the region's accreditor are part of the framing RIDE used.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught to *demonstrate ability without AI* through performance-based, in-person assessment at 9-12 (guidance p.~536). They may expect, and be prepared for, university assessments that verify unaided competence.
- **Higher-education implication:** RIDE emphasizes *equity for Differently-Abled Students*, including not penalizing AI-supported accommodations (guidance p.~563). Institutions should expect students who were taught that AI accommodations are legitimate and should align disability-services practice accordingly.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because Rhode Island borrowed North Carolina's acceptable-use guide (guidance p.51), any leveled-vocabulary familiarity Rhode Island students carry likely traces to North Carolina's framework, useful continuity if a university adopts an AIAS-style scale.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment. Preserve performance-based and in-person assessments where unaided competence matters, mirroring RIDE's 9-12 guidance (guidance p.~536).
- **College-readiness continuity:** Recognize that Rhode Island students may have been taught AI literacy as college preparation (guidance p.32); build on that rather than treating AI as purely an integrity threat.
- **Disability services:** Align AI-accommodation practice with the DAS-equity stance Rhode Island students were taught (guidance p.~563), ensuring AI-supported accommodations are not penalized.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; LEA variance is high and Rhode Island set no state scale.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor citation- and policy-based integrity. Because RIDE pointed LEAs to North Carolina's guide, a university adopting an AIAS-style scale offers helpful continuity.

## Unique Aspects

- **Cross-state borrowing as policy.** Rhode Island reproduces North Carolina's "General Guide on Acceptable Use of AI" in its appendix and cites NCDPI staff directly (guidance pp.17-18, 51), rather than authoring its own scale, a clear example of state-to-state adoption.
- **A full "College and Career Readiness" section** treating AI literacy as postsecondary preparation, with NEASC accreditation and college-application use cases (guidance Section 9).
- **A developmental grade-band use-case table** with explicit caveats, including performance-based in-person assessment at 9-12 (guidance p.~536).
- **Strong equity focus on Differently-Abled Students**, including non-penalizing grading for AI-supported accommodations (guidance p.~563).
- **Two implementation checklists** (Getting Started, AI Software Procurement) and a **bilingual (English/Spanish) sample parent letter** (guidance appendices; sources.md).
- **A rich companion-resource ecosystem**, a NotebookLM podcast, a custom guidance GPT, and a six-part webinar series (sources.md).
