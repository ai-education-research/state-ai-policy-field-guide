**Two provenance facts that govern everything below.** First, the guidance is **authored by a university institute (NAU), not the state education agency**. It states it "provides information rather than instructions" (AZ-GenAI-Guidance-NAU.pdf, p.7). Arizona Department of Education staff appear among named reviewers and authors (p.5), but this is not an ADE-issued framework. Second, Arizona has **no enacted K-12 AI statute** in the provided files: HB 4005 (which would have required AI instruction) was vetoed, and HB 4040 (universities) died in committee (sources.md). Every item below is therefore a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example. Nothing here is a statewide mandate.

## Executive Summary

Arizona's guidance is the most pedagogically and ethically developed document reviewed so far, and it is aimed squarely at the questions a higher-education faculty member cares about. Its through-line is "The Human Agency and Oversight Imperative" (AZ-GenAI-Guidance-NAU.pdf, p.7). It supplies eight ethical considerations (pp.10-20), a five-goal AI-literacy framework aligned to state standards and the State 48 Graduate Profile (p.23), teacher and student use cases (pp.24-25), and — unusually — an explicit, research-cited treatment of **cognitive offloading** (pp.26-27).

The document's stance on assessment and integrity is strikingly close to current higher-education debates: it holds that AI detectors "should never be used as the sole source" and disproportionately punish "non-native English speakers and neurodivergent students" (p.28), that large language models are "unreliable for grading" because they score the same response differently on repeat evaluation (p.27), and that "the primary evaluation of student work should rely on a teacher's judgment" per the MLA statement on AI and Assessment (p.27). For a higher-education reader, the caution is that this is guidance from a university institute, not a state mandate, so actual student exposure varies by district. The document itself points to divergent district models (Chandler, Brophy College Preparatory, Maricopa Unified) rather than a uniform standard (pp.28, 34).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human agency and oversight at every stage.** "This guidance was built around a core value of human agency and oversight at every stage of GenAI use and implementation" (p.7). *[Recommendation / stated value]*
- **AI literacy as the central imperative** ("A Call for AI Literacy," cover; p.22): AI literacy "includes technical understanding, ethical reasoning, and human judgment needed to engage thoughtfully with AI systems" (p.22). *[Recommendation]*
- **A balanced posture:** "AI presents extraordinary opportunities... It also raises important questions related to bias, misinformation, privacy, security, mental well-being, and access. Both realities deserve our thoughtful attention" (p.4). *[Stated stance]*
- **Alignment to Arizona's own frameworks:** the State 48 Graduate Profile (4 Futures, 8 Essential Skills) and Arizona Academic Standards anchor instructional decisions (pp.6, 21, 23). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Designed "to support Arizona education leaders as they lead GenAI implementation" (p.7), with role-based use: boards for policy, administrators for implementation and PD, educators for classroom practice (p.7 organization table). *[Scope]*
- Covers instruction, operations, and administration; addresses generative AI, multimodal, reasoning, and agentic AI (pp.8-9). *[Scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **No statewide permission or prohibition rules** exist; the document models district-level approaches rather than issuing rules. *[Not a mandate]*
- It endorses **per-assignment AI-use expectations set locally**, citing Chandler Unified's AI Integration Model (adapted from an AI Scale) (p.28), Brophy College Preparatory's AI Usage Guide (GenAI use for research, brainstorming, minor revisions as default permission), and Maricopa Unified's student-use system (p.34). *[Examples]*
- **Data-privacy prohibition guidance:** board policy should "mandate that no personally identifiable information (PII) be entered into non-vetted or public AI platforms" (p.35); "never uploading documents containing confidential school or district information" (implied throughout the security discussion, pp.14, 19). *[Recommendation / model rule]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
The eight ethical considerations (pp.10-20) are the document's spine:
- **Bias** (p.11): data, model (including sycophancy), and human bias; "GenAI will never be bias-free." *[Instructional guidance]*
- **False and misleading content** (p.12): "stochastic parrot," hallucination, synthetic media/deepfakes. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Intellectual property** (p.13): authorship/ownership, training-data fair use, and **Indigenous/tribal cultural knowledge** governed by community-specific protocols. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Data and information privacy** (p.14): FERPA; explicit reference to honoring "the data authority of the state's 22 sovereign tribal nations." *[Recommendation]*
- **Equitable access** (p.16): the digital use gap, digital design gap, and digital access gap (from the 2024 NETP); IDEA and Section 504; accessibility to "WCAG 2.1 Level AA." *[Recommendation]*
- **Mental well-being** (p.17): anthropomorphism, parasocial attachment, sycophancy; note that "72% of teens have used AI companions." *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Environmental impact** (p.18): data-center energy/water, with Arizona-specific data-center context. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Cybersecurity** (p.19): AI-enhanced phishing, agentic AI as attack surface, prompt injection, "narrow access" principle. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Academic integrity** is handled under Transparency and Ethical Use plus the Responsible Classroom Use section: disclose and cite AI use, build a framework rather than a "gotcha" regime (pp.28, 34). *[Recommendation]*
- **Ten recommendations** close the ethics chapter (p.20), including "Establish a culture of human oversight" and treating "human in the loop" as "more than a catch phrase."

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Five AI-literacy goals** (p.23), each mapped to Arizona Academic Standards and State 48 Essential Skills: Foundational Understanding of AI Systems; Intentional and Strategic Use of AI; Ethical, Responsible, and Human-Centered Use; Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs and Information; Human Agency and the Human Advantage. *[Instructional guidance / framework]*
- **Teacher use cases** (p.24): instructional thinking and design, designing for learner variability, professional workflow support. **Student use cases** (p.25): sense-making and exploration, creation and iteration, access and agency. *[Examples]*
- **Assessment and grading** (p.27): LLMs are "unreliable for grading" (Xue et al., 2026); teacher judgment is primary (MLA, 2026); Arizona students "tend to prefer teachers, as opposed to AI, to provide guidance, feedback, and grades." *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Implementation** (pp.32-36) uses a "boat" analogy and a "3ssential" early-moves framework: determine and communicate approved tools; train staff on top-line guardrails; clarify expectations for GenAI use. Plus PD (build then deepen AI literacy) and adaptation/refinement cycles. *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Arizona's treatment here is the most explicit and research-grounded in the corpus so far.
- The **"Cognitive Offloading"** subsection (pp.26-27) argues "not all cognitive offloading is harmful" and reframes the question as "not whether students offload thinking, but which thinking they offload and why" (p.26). *[Instructional guidance]*
- It cites research on both sides: overreliance where students "complete entire tasks from beginning to end... may retain less understanding" (Kosmyna et al., 2025), and "purposefully-designed AI tutors may help students manage cognitive load... resulting in higher achievement" (Kestin et al., 2025) (p.27). *[Instructional guidance, cited]*
- Its mitigation is a **microtask / productive-friction** model (Furze, 2026a): teachers decide "which cognitive muscles to focus on and which ones rest," design "AI-infused tasks in which students explain their reasoning, defend their choices, or apply ideas in performance-based tasks," and use "checkpoints and reflection to make thinking visible." The goal is "productive resistance that support[s] learning," not eliminating all offload (p.27). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Over-reliance** is named as a distinct risk: "Offloading mental tasks can... remove productive struggle that helps learners develop persistence and strengthen problem-solving skills" (p.26). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **The Redesign: AI Inspiration** (p.29) proposes preserving higher-order learning by redesigning around "authentic creation and problem-solving," project-based and interdisciplinary learning, portfolios, exhibitions, and performance-based assessment, with teachers as "Designers of Learning, Coaches of Growth, and Expert Guides." *[Recommendation]*
- The five AI-literacy goals explicitly protect "Human Agency and the Human Advantage" and "Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs" as learning targets (p.23). *[Framework]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

- **Uneven by district; no guaranteed statewide baseline.** The guidance is voluntary and university-authored, HB 4005 (AI-instruction requirement) was vetoed, and the document itself cites divergent district models (pp.28, 34). *Basis: p.7; sources.md.*
- **Where districts adopted the guidance's approach**, students may bring: familiarity with per-assignment AI-use expectations (Chandler AI Integration Model, Brophy, Maricopa examples, pp.28, 34); a disclosure-and-citation habit (p.28); exposure to the five AI-literacy goals including critical evaluation of AI outputs and bias awareness (p.23); and the idea that they are accountable for AI-assisted work.
- **A learned skepticism toward AI detectors**, which the guidance calls unreliable and biased against non-native English speakers and neurodivergent students (p.28).
- **Exposure to cognitive-offloading language** ("tool not a crutch," "productive struggle") where teachers taught it; student quotes in the document itself reflect this framing (pp.26-27).
- **Higher-education implication:** Assume wide variance within any Arizona cohort. The students most shaped by this guidance will arrive fluent in the exact vocabulary (human-in-the-loop, per-assignment AI levels, critical evaluation) that a well-designed college syllabus uses; others will have had little formal exposure.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Arizona's guidance mirrors mature higher-education thinking on integrity. Students from adopting districts may arrive expecting disclosure-based integrity, per-assignment AI permissions, and *no reliance on AI detectors* (p.28). Institutions still using detectors as primary evidence may face students taught that those tools are unreliable and discriminatory.
- **Higher-education implication:** The cognitive-offloading framing (pp.26-27) means some Arizona students were taught to distinguish productive from unproductive AI use. Faculty can build on this rather than introduce it cold, using the same microtask/productive-friction language.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because the guidance is authored by a university institute (NAU) and reviewed by ASU-affiliated and ADE staff (p.5), Arizona higher education is already a producer of K-12 AI guidance. In-state institutions can formalize that role and align entry expectations to the five AI-literacy goals (p.23).
- **Higher-education implication:** The equity and accessibility emphasis (digital use/design/access gaps, WCAG 2.1 AA, IDEA/504, p.16) signals that Arizona students may expect accessible, sanctioned AI access. Rural and tribal students may have had constrained access despite the guidance's aims.
- **Higher-education implication:** Tribal data-sovereignty content (pp.13-14) means some Arizona students, particularly from the state's 22 sovereign tribal nations, may have been taught that cultural knowledge carries community-specific use protocols. Institutions handling Indigenous materials should expect and respect that stance.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Academic integrity:** Move away from AI-detector-based enforcement toward disclosure-and-process approaches, matching what adopting Arizona districts taught (p.28). Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission structure continuous with the district models students saw (p.28, p.34).
- **Assessment design:** Apply the microtask / productive-friction model (p.27) — specify which cognitive work students must do unaided and where AI may assist — and favor performance-based tasks, portfolios, and reasoning-defense (p.29).
- **AI tool availability and accessibility:** Provide sanctioned, accessible (WCAG 2.1 AA) AI access so access does not depend on ability to pay, echoing the equity framing (p.16).
- **Onboarding and faculty development:** Use the five AI-literacy goals (p.23) as an entry rubric and a faculty-development scaffold, since incoming students may already have met them.
- **Governance:** Adopt the "human agency and oversight imperative" (p.7) and the eight-consideration ethics map (pp.10-20) as a ready institutional AI-governance scaffold, including tribal data sovereignty where relevant.
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Import the document's central reframing — the question is which thinking students offload and why (p.26) — into course design so AI supports targeted skills while core reasoning stays with the student.

## Unique Aspects

- **An explicit, research-cited cognitive-offloading section** (pp.26-27) with the microtask / productive-friction model and citations to Kosmyna et al. (2025), Kestin et al. (2025), and Furze (2026a). Rare depth for a state-level document.
- **Five AI-literacy goals mapped cell-by-cell to state academic standards and the State 48 Essential Skills** (p.23).
- **Eight-consideration ethics chapter** including tribal data sovereignty (22 sovereign nations), environmental impact with Arizona data-center specifics, and mental well-being (anthropomorphism, sycophancy) (pp.13-19).
- **University authorship** (NAU's Arizona Institute for Education and the Economy) rather than a state agency — a higher-education-produced state guidance, comparable to Florida's UF-coordinated effort.
- **Direct anti-AI-detector stance** with equity rationale (p.28) and an explicit statement that LLMs are unreliable graders (p.27).
