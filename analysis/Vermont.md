**Nature of the documents (read before everything below):** The AOE guidance is voluntary. It is framed to help schools decide, not to mandate; it includes "A Note on Using This Guidance" and role-based Quick Start Guides (guidance p.7, TOC). **Nothing in it is a statewide mandate.** Guidance claims are Recommendation-level unless noted. Act 156 is enacted law but is **not education code**: it adds 18 V.S.A. § 7115 "Prohibited Uses of Artificial Intelligence" in the mental-health-services context and makes prohibited AI use unprofessional conduct under 3 V.S.A. § 129a. It is relevant to student mental-health tools but is not a K-12 classroom mandate.

## Executive Summary

Vermont's guidance is a recommendation-level, developmentally staged framework, and it is the dataset's most direct engagement with cognitive offloading. It is organized into four parts: Foundations, Educational Uses of AI, Responsible AI Usage, and Implementation and School Leadership, with role-based Quick Start Guides (guidance TOC). Its instructional structure rests on two devices: a developmental progression (PreK-2, Grades 3-5 AI Awareness, Grades 6-8 AI Literacy, Grades 9+ AI Fluency) and a Four Principles Framework for how AI can be used (guidance pp.8-19).

What sets Vermont apart is that it gives cognitive offloading its own section and treats it as the central pedagogical challenge. "AI gives us the ability to 'cognitively offload' tasks, essentially outsourcing our thinking to a tool... offloading the wrong tasks can interfere with learning and skill development" (guidance p.24). It cites Ethan Mollick's "Against Brain Damage" and uses the calculator analogy: "Just as we wouldn't want a student to use a calculator when they are first learning multiplication and division, there are situations where AI usage... is harmful to the learning process. Teachers should feel empowered to say when AI usage is inappropriate" (guidance p.24). This is exactly the concern that motivates the higher-education argument of this project.

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, Vermont's developmental gating is strict and reasoned: PreK-2 students "should not use AI chatbots," and grades 3-5 should not use open-ended chatbots at all (guidance p.8). Second, Vermont takes a clear anti-detector stance: "Do not rely on AI auto-detection tools. These tools are not reliable enough" and produce false positives (guidance p.~903). Third, Vermont has no explicit higher-education hook, but its Grades 9+ band is explicitly workforce- and metacognition-oriented, framing AI as a "thinking partner" students manage "while maintaining intellectual ownership" (guidance p.9).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Developmental appropriateness is the organizing principle** (guidance pp.8-9). *[Recommendation]*
- **The Four Principles Framework** categorizes AI use (guidance pp.10-19): (1) streamline administrative and planning work, (2) support personalized learning, (3) develop students' AI literacy, and (4) use AI-infused tools for cognitively demanding tasks. *[Recommendation]*
- **Balance over prohibition or free rein.** The Responsible AI Usage part opens with "Risk of Doing Nothing" (guidance p.22), rejecting inaction while insisting on careful use. *[Recommendation]*
- **Learning objectives drive AI decisions.** Before assigning an AI task, teachers should ask what skills they want developed and whether AI "will perform the skill for them" (guidance p.24). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The guidance serves educators, leaders, and staff, with Quick Start Guides by role (e.g., Elementary Educators PreK-5) (guidance p.44, TOC). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is PreK through grade 9+ (labeled AI Fluency), covering instruction, operations, and leadership (guidance pp.8-9, Part 4). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no leveled permission scale; permission is set by developmental band and by learning objective.** The developmental bands function as age-gated restrictions:
  - **PreK-2:** "Students in grades PreK-2 should not use AI chatbots." Teachers may use AI-incorporating tools (guidance p.8). *[Recommendation, as restriction]*
  - **Grades 3-5 (AI Awareness):** "Students should not use open-ended AI chatbots," only curriculum-embedded, constrained AI; teachers may demonstrate AI (guidance p.8). *[Recommendation, as restriction]*
  - **Grades 6-8 (AI Literacy):** age-appropriate, education-specific chatbots "under a teacher's direction," fully scaffolded, not generic chatbots (guidance p.9). *[Recommendation]*
  - **Grades 9+ (AI Fluency):** AI as "a cognitive assistant tool" for research, learning, and co-creation, with academic-integrity systems in place (guidance p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Prohibited uses sit under Part 3, Responsible AI Usage** (guidance p.22 onward), covering academic honesty, cognitive offloading, data privacy, and mental-health risks. *[Recommendation]*
- **Teachers are empowered to prohibit AI** for specific tasks when it would harm learning (guidance p.24). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Data privacy is strict.** Schools must conform to FERPA and COPPA, and Vermont warns that "it is not enough to remove student-identifiable information" because student intellectual property still trains the system; SU/SDs should have vendor privacy agreements (guidance pp.25-26). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level law + stronger]*
- **A dedicated Ethics, Bias and Fairness, and Equity and access set of sections** anchors fairness (guidance pp.28-30). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic honesty is "the single most cited concern"** (guidance p.22). Vermont recommends reviewing honesty policies for AI, modeling transparency, and, compassionately, "treating the academic honesty issue as a cry for help from students" (guidance pp.~864, 898). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** clearly against. "Do not rely on AI auto-detection tools. These tools are not reliable enough to be used in academic honesty disputes, sometimes show false positives" (guidance p.~903). *[Recommendation — a caution against detectors]*
- **Mental-health risks get their own section** (guidance p.26), including AI-companion toys with which "kids can form intense relationships" (guidance p.~1012), connecting to Act 156. *[Recommendation]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **A Tool Vetting Framework** categorizes AI tools (general-purpose vs. education-specific chatbots) and guides selection (guidance pp.34-36). *[Recommendation / instrument]*
- **Role-based Quick Start Guides** support implementation (guidance p.44 onward). *[Recommendation]*
- **A Needs Assessment** structures leadership implementation across policy, staff readiness, student learning, infrastructure, and community engagement (guidance pp.31-33). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assessment adjustment is expected at grades 9+**, where "teachers need time to adjust assessments to respond to student usage of AI" (guidance p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Principle #4 targets cognitively demanding tasks**, "most appropriate for grades 9-12, where" students can demonstrate genuine understanding (guidance p.~752). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

This is Vermont's signature strength; it engages offloading more directly and by name than any other state in the dataset.

- **A dedicated Cognitive Offloading section** frames the central question: "The central challenge for educators is to determine when AI serves as a valuable learning tool and when it becomes a crutch that hinders skill development" (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It cites research directly**, Ethan Mollick's "Against Brain Damage," and uses the calculator/multiplication analogy to explain when offloading is harmful (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It empowers refusal.** "Teachers should feel empowered to say when AI usage is inappropriate" (guidance p.24). *[Recommendation]*
- **It gives a two-question test** before assigning AI tasks: what skill should students develop, and "will AI's role in this task help them practice that skill, or will it perform the skill for them?" (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **The developmental bands are themselves offloading controls.** Each band warns that premature or unstructured AI use "may bypass the essential struggle needed to develop" core competencies (guidance p.8, grades 3-5) and "may serve as a crutch, bypassing the struggle necessary for the learning process" (guidance p.9, grades 6-8). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **It names productive struggle repeatedly** (guidance pp.8-9, 24) and warns against "learners... not always the best judges of how well they are learning" (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Vermont graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and recent (Jan 2026), exposure varies and is only now taking hold.

- **A developmental trajectory from no-chatbot (PreK-5) to scaffolded use (6-8) to managed "thinking partner" fluency (9+)**, where the school followed the guidance (guidance pp.8-9). *Basis: guidance pp.8-9.*
- **An explicit understanding of cognitive offloading** and when AI is a crutch versus a tool, if teachers used the guidance's framing (guidance p.24). *Basis: guidance p.24.*
- **A metacognitive, intellectual-ownership orientation** at the upper grades, managing AI "while maintaining intellectual ownership" (guidance p.9). *Basis: guidance p.9.*
- **An expectation that AI detectors are unreliable and not used in disputes** (guidance p.~903). *Basis: guidance p.~903.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility. Because the guidance is new and voluntary, current graduates likely predate its full effect; its influence will grow for future cohorts. Where it took hold, Vermont students may arrive with an unusually articulate sense of when AI helps versus harms learning.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Vermont students may arrive already fluent in the *cognitive-offloading* vocabulary that motivates university AI policy, when AI is a "crutch" versus a "thinking partner" (guidance pp.9, 24). This is rare and valuable; faculty can build on a shared conceptual frame rather than introduce it.
- **Higher-education implication:** Vermont's developmental gating means younger cohorts had *deliberately limited* early AI exposure (no chatbots PreK-5). Faculty should not assume long, unstructured AI histories; structured fluency is concentrated at grades 9+.
- **Higher-education implication:** Vermont students were taught that *AI detectors are unreliable and should not decide honesty disputes* (guidance p.~903). Institutions relying on detection software may face students explicitly trained to distrust it.
- **Higher-education implication:** The Grades 9+ band is workforce- and metacognition-oriented, preparing students "for a workforce where AI usage will be expected" while "maintaining intellectual ownership" (guidance p.9). Faculty can extend that ownership framing into disciplinary practice.
- **Higher-education implication:** Vermont's framework has *no explicit higher-education hook* and its enacted statute (Act 156) is mental-health consumer protection, not education code. Vermont institutions are not directed by the state on AI and should develop their own policies.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Cognitive-offloading continuity:** Adopt Vermont's two-question test, does AI help students practice the skill or perform it for them (guidance p.24), as a shared design principle, since incoming Vermont students may already know it.
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment, reserving AI-free work where core skills are the objective, mirroring the productive-struggle logic of the developmental bands (guidance pp.8-9).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Do not rely on AI detectors, consistent with what Vermont students were taught (guidance p.~903); favor process- and transparency-based integrity, and treat suspected misuse as a teachable/support moment (guidance p.898).
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic. The guidance is new and voluntary, so exposure varies and younger-grade experience was deliberately limited.
- **Institutional governance:** Because the state does not direct higher-education AI use, Vermont institutions should build their own policies, drawing on the AOE framework's structure.

## Unique Aspects

- **A dedicated Cognitive Offloading section** citing Ethan Mollick's "Against Brain Damage" and the calculator analogy (guidance p.24), the dataset's most direct treatment of the project's central concern.
- **Strict, reasoned developmental gating**, PreK-2 and grades 3-5 barred from open chatbots, with explicit developmental rationale about anthropomorphizing and bypassing "essential struggle" (guidance p.8).
- **A Four Principles Framework**, including Principle #4 on using AI for "cognitively demanding tasks" (guidance pp.10-19).
- **A clear anti-detector stance** with a compassionate "cry for help" framing of honesty violations (guidance pp.~898, 903).
- **A Tool Vetting Framework and role-based Quick Start Guides** (guidance pp.34-36, 44).
- **A strong data-privacy position** that goes beyond de-identification to student intellectual-property protection in AI training (guidance p.26).
- **A companion mental-health statute (Act 156)** adding 18 V.S.A. § 7115 prohibited AI uses, connecting to the guidance's mental-health-risk section.

- **Vermont upgrades from tracker to primary.** The guidance URL was wrong by one token in prior passes (`edu-AOE-...` not `edu-ai-...`); the correct file is confirmed 50 pages, Issue Date January 23, 2026. The grade-band gating claim is confirmed. *[Resolved]*

- **No leveled student-use instrument exists.** Vermont uses developmental bands plus a Four Principles Framework, not a 0-4 or 1-5 permission scale, and adapts no external scale. This is a deliberate design choice.
- **Act 156 is not education code.** It is mental-health consumer protection (18 V.S.A. § 7115; unprofessional conduct under 3 V.S.A. § 129a), relevant to student mental-health tools but not a K-12 classroom mandate (sources.md). *[Scope clarification]*
- **No explicit higher-education hook** appears in the guidance; the "Sentio University" reference in the text is an illustrative example, not a real institutional partnership. Vermont's postsecondary connection is inferential (workforce framing at grades 9+). *[Absence]*
