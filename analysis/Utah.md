## Executive Summary

Utah pairs a voluntary USBE framework with one enacted curriculum statute. The Artificial Intelligence Framework for Utah P-12 Education (March 2026) is organized around Purpose, Existing Policies, Scope, eight Guiding Principles, Responsible Use, Prohibited Use, and Special Considerations for security, privacy, safety, and academic integrity (framework TOC). It does not use a leveled student-use scale. Instead it sets a Responsible-Use / Prohibited-Use structure under eight first-person "We" principles, and it is unusually grounded in Utah statute, citing a long list of state codes including "Utah FERPA" (framework pp.4-5).

Its instructional spine is human agency and balance. The framework rejects both extremes: "Providing unrestricted access falls short of providing needed protection. Conversely, an outright ban on AI in schools impedes the development of essential future workforce skills" (framework p.3). Guiding Principle 6 commits to keeping staff and students as "critical consumers" who "lead any organizational and academic decisions" (framework p.7).

The enacted statute, HB 218 (2026), is a genuine curriculum requirement. It amends the required grade 7 or 8 digital skills course to include "artificial intelligence literacy, including understanding artificial intelligence" capabilities and limitations, alongside cybersecurity fundamentals and safe online practices (HB 218, sec.53E-4-208). It also authorizes the state superintendent to establish an advisory tech council (HB 218, sec.3).

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, Utah embeds AI literacy into a required middle-grades course by law (HB 218), so future Utah graduates will have had at least one mandated exposure to AI concepts. Second, the framework takes a strong cognitive-offloading position, listing "Over-reliance" among prohibited uses and requiring humans-in-the-loop for any system that "offloads decision making and/or cognitive effort" (framework p.12). Third, Utah's framework contains no explicit higher-education hook; its connection to postsecondary is indirect, through workforce-skills framing.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **A balanced, neither-ban-nor-unrestricted approach** is the stated objective (framework p.3). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Eight Guiding Principles** anchor the framework (framework pp.6-7): use AI to help all students achieve goals; reaffirm existing policies; educate staff and students about AI; explore opportunities and address risks; advance academic integrity; maintain student and teacher agency; audit/monitor/evaluate; and recognize AI is a tool inside and outside the classroom. *[Recommendation]*
- **Deep grounding in Utah statute.** The framework lists Utah State Code provisions on internet policy, student data protection, "Utah FERPA," sensitive instructional materials, and GRAMA, alongside federal law and NIST/CIS standards (framework pp.4-5). *[Recommendation citing Requirement-level law]*
- **HB 218 sets a curriculum objective by law:** AI literacy in the grade 7-8 digital skills course (HB 218, sec.53E-4-208). *[Requirement]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The framework applies to "all students, teachers, staff, administrators, and third parties who develop, implement, or interact with AI technologies," across instruction, administration, and operations, "where permitted by local policy" (framework p.4). *[Scope statement]*
- HB 218's scope is the grade 7 or 8 digital skills course statewide (HB 218, sec.53E-4-208). *[Requirement scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide leveled permission scale.** Utah uses a Responsible-Use / Prohibited-Use structure and delegates specifics to teachers per activity: "Teachers will clarify if, when, and how AI tools will be used, with input from students and families" (framework p.8). *[Recommendation / not a scale]*
- **Responsible uses** include aiding creativity, collaboration, translation, content creation, tutoring, assessment design, differentiation, and operations, with human review throughout (framework pp.8-10). *[Recommendation / examples]*
- **Prohibited uses are explicit** (framework pp.11-12): AI to "manipulate media to impersonate others for bullying, harassment, or any form of intimidation"; over-reliance; plagiarism and cheating; diminishing student and teacher agency; and privacy violations. *[Recommendation, phrased as prohibitions]*
- **Student access equity is required within the framework's logic:** "If an assignment permits the use of AI tools, the tools will be made available to all students" (framework p.10). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Privacy and security are heavily developed**, with a dedicated Special Consideration section, an explicit no-PII rule ("We will not share personally identifiable information with consumer-based AI systems," framework p.6), and prohibition on entering confidential/PII data (framework p.~432). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level law]*
- **Accessibility and the digital divide** are named in Principle 1, "bridging the digital divide" and evaluating tools "for biases and ethical concerns" (framework p.6). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is a guiding principle** grounded in "honesty, trust, fairness, respect, and responsibility," with proper crediting of sources and tools (framework p.7). Documentation guidance directs citing AI in the school's preferred format (APA, MLA) (framework p.8). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** cautionary-tolerant. "Use caution when using AI plagiarism checkers, because they can sometimes produce false positives. Consider using plagiarism checkers as one of several tools in a comprehensive approach" (framework p.8). Utah permits detectors as one input, with a false-positive warning, rather than discouraging them outright. *[Recommendation]*
- **A safety-monitoring use is described.** AI "could have the ability to monitor written student communications on LEA provided accounts" to flag distress and alert personnel (framework p.9). *[Recommendation / example]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **HB 218 mandates AI literacy in a required course.** The grade 7-8 digital skills course must incorporate "artificial intelligence literacy" and its capabilities/limitations, cybersecurity, and safe online practices (HB 218, sec.53E-4-208). *[Requirement]*
- **An advisory tech council may be established** by the state superintendent to recommend digital-skills standards and resources (HB 218, sec.3; note the statute uses "may," making the council discretionary). *[Requirement/discretionary]*
- **Assessment stays human-led.** "Teachers will ultimately be responsible for evaluation, feedback, and grading... AI will not be solely responsible for grading" (framework p.8). *[Recommendation]*
- **Professional development** is supported through AI-recommended strategies and simulation-based training (framework p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Library Media Standards are among the informing policies** (framework p.5). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Utah's framework treats over-reliance as a named prohibited use and speaks directly to cognitive offloading.

- **Over-reliance is listed under Prohibited Use.** "Dependence on AI tools can decrease human discretion and oversight. Important nuances and context can be overlooked and accepted," so teachers must clarify use and review outputs (framework p.11). *[Recommendation, as prohibition]*
- **The framework names cognitive offloading explicitly.** "Teachers and students need to understand how agentic AI, or any AI system, that offloads decision making and/or cognitive effort in the classroom, operate and how to use them appropriately with a human in the loop" (framework p.12). *[Recommendation]*
- **Student and teacher agency is a guiding principle.** Staff and students serve as "critical consumers" and remain accountable for decisions (framework p.7); core teaching practices "will remain the teacher's responsibility" and students "remain active participants" (framework pp.11-12). *[Recommendation]*
- **Plagiarism mitigation is assignment redesign.** Teachers "restructure assignments to reduce opportunities for plagiarism by requiring personal context, original arguments, or original data collection" (framework p.11). *[Recommendation]*
- **Human review of AI output is required throughout** the Responsible-Use and Prohibited-Use sections (framework pp.8-12). *[Recommendation]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Utah graduate *may* bring. The framework is voluntary, but HB 218 raises the statutory floor for cohorts under it.

- **A mandated AI-literacy exposure in grade 7 or 8**, for students under HB 218, covering AI capabilities, limitations, and ethical/societal implications (HB 218, sec.53E-4-208). *Basis: HB 218. [Requirement, for cohorts under the statute.]*
- **A Responsible-Use / Prohibited-Use framing rather than a leveled scale**, where the district followed the framework (framework pp.8-12). *Basis: framework.*
- **A strong human-in-the-loop and anti-over-reliance orientation** (framework pp.11-12). *Basis: framework.*
- **A citation habit** using the school's preferred format for substantial AI-generated text (framework p.8). *Basis: framework.*
- **Uncertain detector expectations.** Utah permits detectors with a false-positive caution (framework p.8), so students' experience is district-dependent and not uniformly skeptical. *Basis: framework.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat the framework habits as ceilings of possibility (voluntary) but the grade 7-8 AI-literacy exposure as a firmer floor for recent cohorts under HB 218. Utah students may lack a leveled AI-use vocabulary but share a balanced, agency-centered framing.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Utah is one of the few states to *require* AI literacy in a specific course by law (HB 218). Incoming Utah students (under the statute) will have had at least one mandated exposure to AI capabilities, limitations, and ethical implications in grade 7 or 8. A university can build on that shared floor rather than assume none.
- **Higher-education implication:** Utah students may arrive framing AI use through *human agency and over-reliance avoidance* rather than a permission scale (framework pp.7, 11-12). Faculty policies that emphasize keeping the human in the loop and reviewing AI output will feel continuous.
- **Higher-education implication:** Utah's framework contains *no explicit higher-education hook*, and its enacted statutes do not reach public universities. This is the opposite of Tennessee. Utah institutions are not directed by the state on AI governance and should not expect a state framework to bind them.
- **Higher-education implication:** Utah's detector stance is *permissive-with-caveats* (framework p.8), so Utah students were not uniformly taught to distrust detection tools. Institutions should not assume a fixed prior view.
- **Higher-education implication:** Utah grounds its framework in specific state privacy statutes ("Utah FERPA," student-data-privacy code; framework pp.4-5), so Utah students may arrive with clear expectations about not entering PII into AI tools.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Onboarding:** Recognize that Utah students under HB 218 had a mandated grade 7-8 AI-literacy unit; use a short diagnostic to place them, but expect a real baseline in AI capabilities and limitations.
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment. Preserve assignments that require personal context and original work, mirroring the framework's plagiarism-mitigation approach (framework p.11).
- **Agency framing:** Build on the human-in-the-loop and anti-over-reliance orientation Utah students were taught (framework pp.11-12), extending it to disciplinary practice.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor citation- and process-based integrity (framework pp.7-8). Because Utah's detector stance is permissive-with-caveats, set your own detector policy explicitly.
- **Institutional governance:** Since the state does not direct higher-education AI governance, Utah institutions should develop their own policies rather than await a state framework.

## Unique Aspects

- **An enacted AI-literacy curriculum requirement.** HB 218 embeds AI literacy into the required grade 7-8 digital skills course by law (sec.53E-4-208), a firmer step than voluntary guidance.
- **A Responsible-Use / Prohibited-Use structure under eight "We" principles**, rather than a leveled student-use scale (framework pp.6-12).
- **Unusually deep grounding in Utah statute**, including "Utah FERPA" and state student-data-privacy code, plus NIST/CIS cybersecurity standards (framework pp.4-5).
- **Explicit cognitive-offloading language** naming agentic AI and the offloading of "decision making and/or cognitive effort" (framework p.12).
- **A described AI safety-monitoring use** flagging student distress to school personnel (framework p.9).
- **A guiding principle for AI use outside the classroom** (personal devices), with USBE committing to support families (framework p.7).
