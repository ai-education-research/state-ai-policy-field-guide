**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** The guidance describes itself as "a dynamic resource designed to help local school districts develop their own approaches" (p. Introduction) and, for its central instrument, as "a framework example that educators and students can use... Educators may adapt or customize" (p.19). **Nothing in this document is a statewide mandate.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted, and none of it is a Requirement on districts.

## Executive Summary

New Mexico's guidance is a recommendation-level framework built on a single organizing commitment: human-centric AI, in which "human oversight, inquiry and reflection ultimately drive meaningful education" (p. Introduction). It is unusually instrument-rich for a voluntary document. It provides a five-level student AI-permission scale (the AI Assessment Scale, p.19), a four-step human-in-the-loop reasoning cycle (the 4-I framework, p.4), a tool-vetting mnemonic (M.A.Z.E., p.10), a grade-banded AI-literacy progression (pp.7-9), and a teaching-with-AI versus teaching-about-AI distinction (p.13). It grounds its ethics in the 2022 White House Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (p.10) and its integration model in SAMR (p.16).

For a higher-education reader, the single most important fact is that New Mexico's student-use scale is an adaptation of **Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024), the AI Assessment Scale** (attribution printed on p.19). A New Mexico graduate may have encountered the same five-level vocabulary that a faculty member can lift directly from the peer-reviewed source onto a syllabus. The document is explicit that this is voluntary and locally adaptable, so actual student exposure will vary by district (the guidance names Gadsden, Santa Fe as district examples, pp.5, 14).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human-centric AI is the spine of the document** (Guidance, p.3). "Human-centric AI values the person over the machine throughout the engagement process" and positions AI "as a tool to support rather than replace" human relationships (p.3). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Digital equity is a first-order objective**, tied to the NM Digital Equity in Education Act of 2023 (p. Introduction; p.12). The guidance frames AI as a potential widener of disparity "particularly for students from under-resourced schools or rural areas" (p.11). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy for both educators and students**, "modeled after Digital Promise's Framework (2024)": users should be able to "(1) understand how AI systems function, (2) evaluate its potential and risks, and (3) apply it thoughtfully in real-world contexts" (p.6). *[Recommendation / instructional guidance]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Explicitly addressed to "educators, administrators and communities" (p. How to Use this Guidance, p. following Introduction). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is all of K-12, with content broken into Early Grades (K-5), Middle Grades (6-8), and High School (pp.7-9). *[Scope statement]*
- The document is a state-agency resource that defers to local governance. "Local governance allows schools to adapt AI guidelines to their unique needs" (p.20). *[Recommendation]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **This is guidance, not a rulebook. It contains no statewide prohibition list.** *[Not a mandate]*
- The AI Assessment Scale (p.19) is the closest thing to a permission structure, but it is offered as an adaptable example, not a rule. Its five levels, verbatim:
  - **Level 1 NO AI** — "The assessment is completed entirely without AI assistance in a controlled environment... You must not use AI at any point." *[Example]*
  - **Level 2 AI PLANNING** — "AI may be used for pre-task activities such as brainstorming, outlining and initial research... final submission should show how you have developed and refined these ideas." *[Example]*
  - **Level 3 AI COLLABORATION** — "AI may be used to help complete the task, including idea generation, drafting, feedback, and refinement. Students should critically evaluate and modify the AI suggested outputs." *[Example]*
  - **Level 4 FULL AI** — "AI may be used to complete any elements of the task, with students directing AI... Focus on directing AI to achieve your goals while demonstrating your critical thinking." *[Example]*
  - **Level 5 AI EXPLORATION** — "AI is used creatively... Students and educators co-design assessments to explore unique AI applications within the field of study." *[Example]*
- Restriction posture on tooling is a **recommended vetting process**, not a banned-tools list: educators "must evaluate tools with a critical lens" before purchase (p.10). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Five ethical protections** drawn from the 2022 Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (p.10): "Safe and effective systems; Algorithmic discrimination protection; Data privacy; Notice and explanation; Human alternatives, consideration and fallback." *[Recommendation]* Each carries a one-line gloss, e.g. Data privacy requires tools to "respect data sovereignty (where data are stored, who owns the data, and how or when the data is deleted)" (p.10).
- **Accessibility is written into procurement**: "Accessibility features, such as text-to-speech and translation tools powered by AI, must be incorporated into procurement processes" (p.11). *[Recommendation, phrased as an imperative but within a voluntary document]*
- **Academic integrity (pp.18-19)** is handled as a values question, not an enforcement one. The core principle: "all submitted work should reflect a student's own understanding, effort and originality, even when supported by AI tools" (p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools (p.18):** "AI detectors do not have high reliability in detecting plagiarism (Center for Democracy & Technology, 2023) and are frequently found to be biased against non-native English writers (Liang et al., 2023)." Educators are told to "critically evaluate the validity, accuracy and ethical implications of AI detection tools." *[Recommendation — a caution against detectors]*
- Disclosure and citation of AI use is encouraged as an integrity habit: "Properly citing AI tools or providing disclosure... demonstrates honesty" (p.18). *[Recommendation]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Professional development** is framed as prerequisite: PD "should provide the knowledge, skills and confidence educators need" (p.6), with a linked resource list (Digital Promise, MIT, Code.org, TeachAI, Common Sense Media, the Friday Institute's "AI with the FI" course) (p.6). *[Recommendation + examples]*
- **Curriculum integration** favors embedding over standalone units: "it is equally important to embed these skills into existing standards and core curriculum" (p.13, Standards Alignment). *[Recommendation]*
- The **Teaching WITH AI vs. Teaching ABOUT AI** table (p.13) separates using AI as an instructional tool from teaching AI as subject matter, and says both are needed. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **SAMR** (Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition) structures the Scaffolded Technology Integration chart (p.16), explicitly labeled "an example to inspire further exploration... not as an endorsement of any tool" (p.16). *[Example]*
- **Implementation/governance:** districts are encouraged to build local AI policies on existing Responsible/Acceptable Use Policies, review them throughout the year, and make "age-appropriate versions" for students (p.20). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

This document engages cognitive offloading directly and by name, which is unusual.

- The **Critical Thinking & Problem Solving** section (p.17) states the risk explicitly: "concerns persist that over-reliance on AI might erode essential skills by encouraging students to accept AI-generated outputs without questioning them or bypassing the process of independent reasoning." *[Instructional guidance]*
- Its stated mitigation: "AI should be positioned as a tool or assistant, not as a partner or replacement for human thinking. Teachers should guide students in critically evaluating AI outputs, identifying errors and understanding the logic behind AI-generated results" (p.17). *[Recommendation]*
- It supplies concrete interrogation prompts for teachers to model: "'What assumptions does AI make?' and 'How do these results compare to other evidence we've reviewed?'" (p.17). *[Instructional guidance / example]*
- The **4-I framework** (Inquiry, Input, Interpretation, Insight, p.4) is itself an anti-offloading structure. It begins with "human-driven curiosity" and requires humans to "analyze and evaluate the AI-generated output... assess its accuracy, biases and alignment" before integrating findings, so that "AI does not replace human judgment" (p.4). *[Recommendation / framework]*
- The **Human-in-the-Loop / teacher-in-the-loop** construct (p.3) reinforces that "beginning and ending AI use with human inquiry and reflection" is the intended pattern. *[Recommendation]*
- **Preserving subject-matter learning:** the guidance ties AI-literacy skills to core-content standards rather than treating AI as a separate subject (p.13), and its Level 1 NO AI option (p.19) preserves the option of assessing "core skills and knowledge" without AI at all. *[Recommendation / example]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

The following describe capabilities and habits a New Mexico graduate *may* bring, each tied to what the document actually recommends. Because the guidance is voluntary and locally adaptable, exposure will be uneven across districts.

- **Familiarity with a leveled AI-permission vocabulary.** A student in a district that adopted the AI Assessment Scale will recognize the idea that each assignment carries an explicit AI level from "No AI" to "AI Exploration" (p.19). *Basis: p.19, offered as adaptable example.*
- **Exposure to AI as subject matter, scaled by grade.** High-school content recommends "neural networks, deep learning and natural language processing," building text classifiers, AI auditing for bias, and "Career Pathways" connecting AI to professions (p.9). Middle grades recommend building simple models in Scratch or Python and analyzing datasets for bias (p.8). *Basis: pp.8-9, recommendations.*
- **A disclosure-and-citation habit around AI use** where districts adopted it (p.18). *Basis: p.18.*
- **An interrogation stance toward AI output**, if their teachers modeled the p.17 prompts. *Basis: p.17.*
- **Higher-education implication:** These are *ceilings of possibility, not guarantees.* Because the document is voluntary, a faculty member should assume a wide range within a single New Mexico cohort, from students who ran district AI tools daily (Gadsden, Santa Fe examples, pp.5, 14) to students whose district adopted little of this.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Incoming New Mexico students may already speak the exact AIAS vocabulary that a university syllabus can adopt. Because the K-12 scale and the peer-reviewed source (Perkins et al. 2024) share level names, a faculty member can align a course to that scale and expect partial prior familiarity rather than starting from zero (basis: p.19 attribution).
- **Higher-education implication:** Students from adopting districts may arrive expecting AI use to be *specified per assignment* rather than globally banned or allowed. A blanket "no AI" or silent syllabus will read as less clear than what they experienced (basis: the per-assignment logic of p.19).
- **Higher-education implication:** Students may have been taught that AI *detectors are unreliable and biased* (p.18). Institutions that rely on AI-detection software for integrity enforcement may face students who were explicitly taught to distrust those tools, which is a defensible position the K-12 document itself takes.
- **Higher-education implication:** New Mexico's guidance names an explicit K-16 pipeline. The New Mexico AI Consortium (NMAIC) unites Los Alamos and Sandia National Laboratories with UNM, NMSU, NM Tech, and Central New Mexico Community College "to establish pathways for AI research and education" and to integrate "AI literacy into school curricula, leveraging the resources of higher education" (p.21). A New Mexico higher-education institution may already be a named partner in the pipeline feeding it.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because exposure is uneven, the safest planning assumption is high variance in AI literacy within any New Mexico cohort, driving a need for a common baseline at entry rather than assumed competence.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **AI tool availability and equity:** New Mexico's equity framing (pp.11-12) implies incoming students may expect accessibility features (text-to-speech, translation) as standard. Institutions should confirm their sanctioned AI tools carry those features and that access does not depend on a student's ability to pay for a premium tier.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short entry diagnostic of AI literacy rather than assuming it. The state's own AI-literacy definition (understand, evaluate, apply thoughtfully, p.6) is a ready rubric for such a diagnostic.
- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission scale. Because New Mexico students may recognize the AIAS levels, adopting the same Perkins et al. (2024) source gives continuity from high school to college. Preserve "Level 1 NO AI" assessments where the learning goal is core skill demonstration (mirrors p.19).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Reconsider reliance on AI detectors, which the students' own K-12 guidance calls unreliable and biased against non-native English writers (p.18). Favor process-based and disclosure-based integrity approaches, which is exactly the habit the guidance tried to build (p.18).
- **Faculty development:** Offer faculty the teaching-with vs. teaching-about distinction (p.13) and the anti-offloading interrogation prompts (p.17) as ready-made pedagogy, since incoming students may already have met them.
- **Governance:** Where the institution sits in New Mexico, check whether it is a NMAIC partner (p.21) and coordinate entry expectations with that pipeline.
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** The 4-I cycle (p.4) and the p.17 mitigation language give a shared K-16 framework for keeping the human in the loop. Adopting the same language lowers the translation cost for incoming students.

## Unique Aspects

- **The AI Assessment Scale with explicit academic attribution** to "Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024). The AI Assessment Scale," printed under the table with a CC BY-NC-SA mark (p.19). This is a primary-source-confirmed lineage to the peer-reviewed AIAS, per the folder's sources.md.
- **The 4-I framework** (Inquiry → Input → Interpretation → Insight), a named, cyclical human-in-the-loop reasoning model (p.4). Distinctive terminology.
- **M.A.Z.E.** tool-vetting mnemonic — "Monitor data privacy, Assess for accuracy, Zero-in on bias, Evaluate value" — credited to the Friday Institute (p.10).
- **National-laboratory partnership.** The NMAIC brings Los Alamos and Sandia into a K-16 AI-education pipeline (p.21), a hook few other states can claim.
- **Cultural/equity grounding** in the NM Digital Equity in Education Act (2023) and a three-divide model (digital use divide, digital design divide, digital access divide, p.11).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness limits every claim about students.** The document mandates nothing, so actual student exposure to any instrument is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **No statute in the provided files.** sources.md notes HB 330 (AI ethics as a CS elective) as pending; the bill text is not in this folder, so no enacted requirement can be asserted. *[Unresolved]*
- **M.A.Z.E. wording** was flagged in earlier project passes as tracker-sourced; it is now confirmed on p.10 of the primary document, attributed to the Friday Institute. The prior data.js gloss of the scale endpoints ("No AI to AI Exploration") is correct on endpoints; the full internal level names (AI Planning, AI Collaboration, Full AI) are now verbatim-confirmed (p.19).
- **Glossary, acknowledgements, and references (pp.22-24)** were not required for this analysis and were not transcribed in detail. Not a substantive gap for policy claims.
- **District examples** (Gadsden p.5, Santa Fe p.14) are illustrative spotlights, not evidence of statewide practice. Treat as Examples only.
