**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** The guidance is explicitly voluntary and rests on Missouri's local-control statute. It cites Section 171.011 RSMo, under which "school boards have local control" and "LEAs may develop policies and procedures best suited for their stakeholders, including policies... regarding use of AI" (p.4). DESE "encourages all LEAs" to use AI well but mandates nothing (p.4). **Nothing in the document is a statewide requirement on districts.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. HB 3139 is a pending bill whose final status is unconfirmed.

## Executive Summary

Missouri's guidance is a recommendation-level policy-development aid aimed at Local Education Agencies (LEAs). Its structure is administrative rather than instrument-based. It moves through Purpose, Legislative Context, What is AI, Challenges and Benefits, Best Practices, AI Integration, and AI Policies (TOC, p.2). It reads as a document written to help a district write its own AI policy, not to give a teacher a classroom permission scale.

It has no leveled student-use scale. In one line it nods toward the idea, telling educators to "encourage the use of AI acceptable rating scales for both students and overall school AI use" (p.11), but it supplies none and adapts no external scale such as Perkins et al. Its concrete instructional content is concentrated in two places. First, a Best Practices frame built on five named values: Responsible Implementation, Transparency, Rigor, Curiosity, and Ensuring Human Oversight (pp.12-13). Second, a prompt-engineering section anchored on the "Five S" model from AI for Education, Set the scene, be Specific, Simplify, Structure the output, Share the feedback (p.16).

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, the document was written by DESE's Computer Science Advisory Council, whose named membership includes faculty from the University of Central Missouri, the University of Missouri-Columbia, Missouri State University, and Northwest Missouri State University (p.4). This is a K-16 authorship hook rather than a governance pipeline. Second, the entire document is framed around workforce readiness, leaning on the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs projections (p.5). Third, it takes a firm line on assessment integrity, stating that "AI should not be used in standardized testing without explicit state approval" (p.11), one of the few near-prohibitions in the file.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI supplements, does not replace, human thought.** "While AI serves as a powerful tool, it should supplement human thought and decision-making rather than replace it" (p.5). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Workforce readiness is the organizing rationale.** The document leans on the WEF 2024 Future of Jobs Report that "75 percent of the companies surveyed intend to integrate generative AI by 2027" and argues "educators must devise strategies to equip students for economic empowerment" (p.5). AI literacy is tied throughout to "prospective job market expectations for students" (p.5). *[Recommendation / framing]*
- **The primary purpose of AI in schools is to enhance student outcomes** (p.4). *[Recommendation]*
- **Local control is the governing legal frame.** Under RSMo 171.011, districts write their own AI policies; DESE only "highlights key points for LEAs to consider" (p.4). *[Scope statement]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The document addresses "LEAs, administrators, educators, and educational support personnel" (Purpose, p.4). Its Challenges and Benefits section is organized by stakeholder tier: Students, Building-level (Schools and Administrators), District-Level (Leadership and Policy), and Stakeholders (Parents and Community) (pp.8-9). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is all Missouri K-12. It urges LEAs to "infuse AI literacy into all grade levels and curriculum areas" (p.14) but gives no grade-band instrument. *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Permitted and prohibited uses are delegated to each LEA's Acceptable Use Policy (AUP). LEAs "need to clearly outline AI usage and expectations for students, staff, and community stakeholders" (p.9). *[Recommendation / not a mandate]*
- **The one firm restriction is on assessment.** "AI should not be used in standardized testing without explicit state approval" (p.11). This is stated as an integrity rule, not merely a suggestion. *[Recommendation, phrased as a restriction]*
- **Parental permission is set as a threshold for minors.** LEAs should "obtain explicit parental/guardian permission for students under 18" and observe that "many generative AI tools have age restrictions... usually on students under the age of 13" (p.9). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **A federal-law compliance list anchors the ethics section.** The AI Policies section names FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, CIPA, Section 504, and the ADA as "relevant to the use of AI" (p.17). *[Recommendation]*
- **Data privacy gets the most detailed treatment.** Districts should evaluate security protocols, define personally identifiable information and forbid entering it into generative tools, train users, obtain parental permission, and negotiate that vendors "not... use their data or information to train the vendor's models" (pp.9, 15, 17). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity is framed as an AI-driven digital divide.** "AI could create a divide between students who can access AI and those who can't" through cost or connectivity (pp.9, 18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is handled through assessment redesign and honor codes.** The response to cheating is to "restructure the learning environment," use conferences and formative assessments, "assess and emphasize the student honor code," and "encourage the use of AI acceptable rating scales" (p.11). *[Instructional guidance / recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** the document is notably quiet on detectors. It does not endorse detection software and does not build a caution against it either. Its integrity approach is process- and honor-code-based rather than detector-based (p.11). *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Professional development is treated as prerequisite** and recurs throughout. The AI Integration section recommends workshops, "micro-credentials or micro-badges," guided share sessions (Ed Camp), and access to the DESE Computer Science webpage and listserv funded through the Computer Science Education Grant Fund (p.14). *[Recommendation + examples]*
- **Prompt engineering is elevated to a "key skill for educators and students alike"** (p.15). The document teaches prompt types (direct, open-ended, instructional, role-based, refinement) and the "Five S" model from AI for Education (p.16). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Curriculum integration is by embedding** AI literacy across "all grade levels and curriculum areas" (p.14), not through a standalone AI course. *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation is a cyclical policy process.** LEAs "should adopt a cyclical process for their AI policies to ensure continuous improvement," reviewing and updating as technology evolves (p.17). The document reproduces a cyclical-process graphic credited to the TeachAI toolkit principles (p.17). It also recommends appointing "an AI Coordinator" (p.20). *[Recommendation]*
- **Procurement guidance** requires alignment to educational goals, vendor data-security compliance, stakeholder engagement, and pilot testing (p.18). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Over-reliance is one of the document's most consistently named risks.

- It lists overreliance among the top student challenges. "Students may use AI to write essays, complete assignments, or answer questions without fully understanding the underlying concepts" (p.8), and separately warns of a "Loss of Human Interaction" leading to "loneliness, isolation, and anxiety" (p.8). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Its central mitigation is teacher modeling.** For "Student Dependence," the response is that "educators should model how they use AI as a starting point for writing to show students how to analyze AI responses so that they can craft appropriate prompts" (p.10). *[Recommendation]*
- **Human oversight is named as its own Best Practice** and given a rationale list, bias, hallucination, lack of ethical judgment, and no contextual understanding (p.13). Both educators and students "must assess AI-generated results to ensure accuracy and avoid bias" (p.12). *[Recommendation]*
- **It defends the irreplaceability of the human educator.** AI "cannot determine the context that exists within the learning environment or develop empathy," and "students still require a human presence to listen and show empathy" (p.11). *[Instructional guidance]*
- It preserves cognition through self-regulation and integrity. Educators should teach "self-regulation" and use "conferences, formative assessments, and opportunities for students to demonstrate mastery" (p.11). *[Instructional guidance]*
- Its "Rigor" principle explicitly rejects using AI to reduce difficulty: "Rather than simplifying tasks, AI can provide students with opportunities to tackle more complex challenges" (p.12). *[Recommendation]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Missouri graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and rules were set by each LEA, exposure will vary widely.

- **Governed by a district Acceptable Use Policy, not a state scale.** A Missouri student's AI rules came from a local AUP (p.9). A graduate is more likely to expect a district rulebook than a portable leveled vocabulary. *Basis: pp.4, 9, recommendations.*
- **Possible facility with prompt engineering.** Where teachers followed the guidance, students may have been taught prompt types and the "Five S" model as an explicit skill (p.16). *Basis: p.16, instructional guidance.*
- **A workforce-and-careers framing of AI.** The document repeatedly ties AI literacy to future jobs (p.5). Students may arrive understanding AI primarily as an economic and career competency. *Basis: p.5.*
- **Exposure to AI as a study, accessibility, and tutoring aid** where adopted (individualized explanations, summarization, immediate feedback, personalized tutors) (p.8). *Basis: p.8, benefits list.*
- **An assessment-integrity expectation that AI is off-limits in formal testing** without approval (p.11). *Basis: p.11.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Because Missouri delegated the operative rules to LEAs, faculty should assume high within-cohort variance in both AI literacy and permitted-use expectations.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Missouri students likely learned AI norms from a *district AUP*, not a per-assignment permission scale, and the state offered no standardized instrument. Faculty should not assume familiarity with leveled AI-use scales and should teach a per-assignment permission structure explicitly.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because the guidance frames AI heavily as *workforce readiness* (p.5), incoming students may expect AI instruction to connect to careers and may value hands-on tool fluency over conceptual restraint. Faculty can meet this by tying course AI policy to disciplinary and professional practice.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students may arrive already trained in *prompt engineering* using the "Five S" model (p.16). A university can build on that shared vocabulary rather than starting from zero, and can extend it toward source evaluation and verification.
- **Higher-education implication:** Missouri's integrity model is *process- and honor-code-based*, not detector-based (p.11). Students from adopting districts may expect conferences, drafts, and mastery demonstrations rather than surveillance, and may have been taught that AI is disallowed in formal testing without approval.
- **Higher-education implication:** The document was written with substantial *university faculty involvement* on the Computer Science Advisory Council (p.4). Missouri institutions may already have faculty who shaped the K-12 guidance and can align entry expectations to it.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission statement, because Missouri students likely encountered a district AUP rather than a standardized scale. Preserve AI-free assessment where core-skill demonstration is the goal, mirroring the state's standardized-testing restriction (p.11).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor process-based and honor-code integrity, which continues what many Missouri students were taught (p.11). Do not over-rely on AI detectors, which the state guidance neither endorses nor recommends.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry. Cohort variance will be high because rules were set locally.
- **Prompt-literacy scaffolding:** Because prompt engineering was taught as a named skill (p.16), universities can extend it toward critical evaluation and citation rather than reteaching the basics.
- **Careers framing:** Since Missouri framed AI as workforce preparation, institutions can connect course AI policy to disciplinary and professional norms to match student expectations.
- **Governance:** Because Missouri delegated authority to LEAs, universities should set a common baseline at entry and consider naming an AI point person, mirroring the state's "AI Coordinator" recommendation (p.20).

## Unique Aspects

- **Written by a Computer Science Advisory Council with named university faculty** from the University of Central Missouri, University of Missouri-Columbia, Missouri State, and Northwest Missouri State (p.4). This is now primary-source confirmed, upgrading the prior tracker-only attribution.
- **A workforce-readiness spine.** Few state documents lean as hard on labor-market projections (WEF Future of Jobs) as the organizing case for AI literacy (p.5).
- **Prompt engineering treated as a core competency**, with prompt taxonomies and the "Five S" model made explicit (pp.15-16).
- **A named Best Practices set:** Responsible Implementation, Transparency, Rigor, Curiosity, Human Oversight (pp.12-13). The "Rigor" principle's insistence that AI raise task difficulty rather than lower it is distinctive.
- **A firm standardized-testing restriction:** "AI should not be used in standardized testing without explicit state approval" (p.11).
- **Explicit parental-permission threshold for students under 18** (p.9), stronger than most state guidance.
- **Recommends appointing an AI Coordinator** at the district level (p.20), an administrative hook comparable to Maryland's central-office coordinator model.

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness and delegation limit every claim about students.** The document mandates nothing and routes rules to LEAs, so actual student exposure is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists.** The document gestures at "AI acceptable rating scales" (p.11) but supplies none and adapts none. This is a genuine absence, not a retrieval gap.
- **The "cyclical" policy process is a reproduced TeachAI graphic**, not a Missouri-authored numbered process (p.17). Any specific step count should be treated cautiously; the extractable text presents it as a cycle, not an enumerated sequence.
- **HB 3139 (2026) is a pending bill of unconfirmed final status.** Per sources.md, it adds cybersecurity, social media training, and AI to the definition of a "computer science course" and, from 2027-28, would require State Board performance standards and a half-unit CS credit for graduation. The folder holds only the House bill-summary PDF, and the session adjourned sine die without confirmed passage, so no enacted requirement can be asserted. *[Unresolved]*
- **Resources and References (pp.19-20)** are third-party links (MSBA AI Toolkit, TeachAI, aiEDU, EDSAFE) and were not treated as Missouri policy. Not a substantive gap for policy claims.
