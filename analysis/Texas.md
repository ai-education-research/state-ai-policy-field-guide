## Executive Summary

Texas has issued no statewide classroom AI guidance. TEA has published none, and multiple 2025-2026 news sources report that Texas districts operate without state direction (sources.md). The state's AI legislation is not about classrooms. HB 2060 (2023) created a seven-member AI advisory council to study AI use by state agencies, with a sunset of January 1, 2025 (sources.md). HB 149 (2025), the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA), signed June 22, 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, is a general AI governance statute.

TRAIGA's one education-relevant provision is a carve-out, and it runs toward higher education specifically. Per sources.md, the enrolled text excludes from the definition of "governmental entity" (Sec. 552.001) "an institution of higher education, as defined by Section 61.003, Education Code" (along with hospital districts). That is a higher-education exclusion from the governmental-entity duties the act imposes, not a blanket statement that K-12 education is excluded. The broader claim that "education was deliberately excluded" is directionally supported by this higher-ed exclusion plus the absence of any K-12 classroom mandate, but the precise, sourceable fact is the higher-education carve-out.

For a higher-education reader, the operative facts are two. First, Texas set no state K-12 AI-permission scale, prohibition list, or student-use vocabulary, so a graduate's classroom AI norms came entirely from the district (the largest districts, like Houston ISD, have their own guidebooks). Second, Texas public higher-education institutions were carved out of TRAIGA's governmental-entity obligations, meaning the state's general AI-governance duties largely do not bind them, a notable difference from Tennessee, where the legislature affirmatively required universities to adopt AI policies.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Texas is a no-state-K-12-guidance jurisdiction. A faculty member cannot assume any statewide shared AI vocabulary, permission scale, or integrity norm among incoming Texas students, because TEA authored none.
- **Higher-education implication:** Texas is the near-inverse of Tennessee on the higher-education question. Where Tennessee law *required* public universities to adopt AI policies, Texas's general AI-governance act (TRAIGA) *excludes* institutions of higher education from its governmental-entity duties (sources.md). Texas public institutions are largely left to their own devices on AI governance, not directed by the state.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because the most influential prior frameworks are district-level (large-district guidebooks), institutions drawing heavily from a particular district may find more common ground there, but the specifics were not retrievable here and should be checked directly.
- **Higher-education implication:** The state baseline may remain thin for some time. TEA has issued nothing, and the enacted statutes point away from classroom regulation, so entry planning should assume no state K-12 baseline for the foreseeable future.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Onboarding:** Assume no statewide baseline. Give incoming Texas students an explicit AI-use orientation and a short literacy diagnostic.
- **Check large-district frameworks directly.** For institutions drawing heavily from districts like Houston ISD, retrieve and review the district's AI guidebook to understand what many incoming students actually experienced.
- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment; Texas students share no statewide scale or vocabulary.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Set integrity expectations from first principles; Texas established no statewide stance on AI detectors or disclosure.
- **Institutional governance:** Because TRAIGA excludes higher-education institutions from its governmental-entity duties (sources.md), Texas public institutions should not assume the state will supply an AI-governance framework and should develop their own.

## Unique Aspects

- **A general AI-governance act with a higher-education carve-out.** TRAIGA (HB 149) excludes "an institution of higher education, as defined by Section 61.003, Education Code" from the definition of "governmental entity," so its state duties largely do not reach public colleges (sources.md).
- **The Tennessee contrast.** Texas is the clearest counterpoint to Tennessee's affirmative university mandate: Texas leaves higher education out of its AI-governance duties rather than binding it.
- **A state-government, not classroom, legislative posture.** Both enacted statutes (HB 2060's advisory council, HB 149's governance act) address government and general AI use, not K-12 instruction.
- **District-led practice at scale.** In the absence of TEA guidance, large districts (Houston ISD and others) have produced their own AI guidebooks, so Texas practice is defined at the district level.
