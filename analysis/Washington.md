## Executive Summary

Washington's guidance is a recommendation-level, human-centered framework built around a five-level student AI-permission scale. The scale is confirmed verbatim in the main v3.0 guidance (p.20) and in the standalone "5 Step Scaffolding Scale for Students": Level 1 No AI Assistance, Level 2 AI-Assisted Brainstorming, Level 3 AI-Supported Drafting, Level 4 AI-Collaborative Creation, Level 5 AI as Co-Creator. OSPI also publishes an educator matrix that crosses assignment types (longform essays, podcasts/video, research, math problems) against those same five levels, giving teachers concrete, liftable exemplars for each cell.

The document's spine is a human-centered principle stated crisply: "A human-centered AI learning environment always starts with human inputs and inquiry, and always concludes with human reflection and edits" (guidance p.14). It takes one of the strongest anti-detector positions in the dataset: "It is highly recommended that educators do not utilize AI detection software in evaluating student work," citing Vanderbilt and Stanford research that the tools are "less accurate than advertised, as well as biased against non-native English speakers and writers" (guidance p.40).

For a higher-education reader, three facts matter. First, Washington's five-level scale is a directly adoptable per-assignment instrument, and it is the source that Puerto Rico translated into Spanish, an independent corroboration of its structure. Second, the anti-detector stance is explicit and research-cited. Third, Washington's higher-education connection is college-and-career-readiness framing rather than institutional governance; the guidance prepares students "for college, career, and life" (guidance p.14) but does not, unlike Tennessee or Virginia, assign roles to universities.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human-centered AI is the organizing commitment.** The environment "always starts with human inputs and inquiry, and always concludes with human reflection and edits" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **AI literacy, ethics, and equity** are the stated leadership priorities, developing students' AI literacy, ensuring "ethical, equitable, and safe use," and providing teacher PD (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Student agency in AI use.** "Empower students in how and to what degree AI is utilized in their learning journey" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Workforce and college-career-life preparation** frames the purpose (guidance pp.14, ~986). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The guidance addresses education leaders, teachers, students, and administrators (guidance pp.14, Sections). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is K-12 public schools, with companion instruments for students (the scale) and educators (the matrix). *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **The 5 Step Scaffolding Scale for Students is the permission instrument** (guidance p.20; standalone companion), offered as an adaptable classroom tool. Its five levels, verbatim:
  - **Level 1 No AI Assistance** — "No AI tools are used at any point. Students rely solely on their knowledge and skills." *[Example / instrument]*
  - **Level 2 AI-Assisted Brainstorming** — "AI tools can help generate ideas. Final content must be created by the student without direct AI input. AI assistance must be cited." *[Example]*
  - **Level 3 AI-Supported Drafting** — "AI can help with drafting initial versions. The final version must be significantly revised by the student. Clear distinction between AI input and student's contributions." *[Example]*
  - **Level 4 AI-Collaborative Creation** — "AI-generated content can be included. Student must critically evaluate and edit AI contributions. AI usage must be transparent and cited." *[Example]*
  - **Level 5 AI as Co-Creator** — "Extensive use of AI in content creation. Student provides a rationale for AI use and ensures original thought. Work adheres to academic integrity with proper citations." *[Example]*
- **The educator matrix applies the same five levels across assignment types** (reading discussion, longform drafting, reflective/creative writing, podcasts/video, research, math word problems), giving worked examples per level (educator matrix). *[Instrument]*
- **A co-created decision rubric is encouraged.** Educators should "co-create and share an AI decision-making rubric with students" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Ethics, equity, and safety** are a leadership priority, protecting data privacy and security and "addressing potential biases and harms" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Accessibility and special education** are named: "Empower students receiving special education services to use AI to personalize and increase their access to learning" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity is built into every scale level** through citation and transparency requirements (student scale, Levels 2-5). *[Instrument]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** among the strongest cautions in the dataset. OSPI "highly recommended that educators do not utilize AI detection software," citing Vanderbilt and Stanford research on inaccuracy and bias "against non-native English speakers and writers," and recommends knowing "students' voice and writing style through samples of work gathered over time" plus a conversation if misuse is suspected (guidance p.40). A guiding principle also warns against "sole reliance on... 'AI detection' tools" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation — a strong caution against detectors]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Professional development** for integrating AI into "pedagogy, curriculum, and assessment" is a leadership priority (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assessment integrates the scale.** The educator matrix is explicitly assignment-and-assessment oriented, and detector conversations "can form the basis" for integrity discussions when "an AI Matrix has been implemented and clearly communicated" (guidance p.40). *[Instrument / recommendation]*
- **CTE is highlighted** as "crucial in preparing students for the AI workforce" (guidance p.~986). *[Recommendation]*
- **Human-centered design and policy-building** sections guide implementation (guidance Sections 1). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Washington's offloading protection is embedded in its human-centered spine and the scale's design.

- **The human-in-the-loop principle bookends every AI interaction.** Learning "always starts with human inputs and inquiry, and always concludes with human reflection and edits" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Level 1 preserves unaided cognition** by definition: "Students rely solely on their knowledge and skills" (student scale). *[Instrument]*
- **Escalating student responsibility across levels.** By Level 5, the student "provides a rationale for AI use and ensures original thought" (student scale). *[Instrument]*
- **Critical evaluation is required at higher levels.** At Level 4, the "student must critically evaluate and edit AI contributions" (student scale). *[Instrument]*
- **Aligning AI with learning principles.** OSPI directs "supporting student agency, fostering collaboration, enhancing feedback, and promoting critical thinking" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- Washington does not include a standalone cognitive-offloading section by that name (unlike Vermont), but the scale's leveled design is itself an offloading-management instrument. *[Structural note]*

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Washington's five-level scale is a directly adoptable per-assignment instrument, and its educator matrix supplies ready exemplars. A university can adopt the same level structure and expect adopting-district students to recognize "No AI Assistance" through "AI as Co-Creator." Note that Puerto Rico translated this exact scale, so it has cross-jurisdiction reach.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students were taught that *AI detectors are unreliable and biased against non-native English writers*, with research citations (guidance p.40). Institutions relying on detection software may face students explicitly and confidently trained to distrust it, a research-backed position.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students from adopting districts may expect AI permission to be *specified per assignment*, *cited*, and *transparent*, and may expect suspected misuse to be handled through a conversation rather than a detector verdict (guidance p.40). A silent or blanket syllabus will read as less clear than what they experienced.
- **Higher-education implication:** Washington's higher-education connection is college-career-life readiness, not institutional governance. Unlike Tennessee or Virginia, the guidance assigns no roles to universities, so faculty should not assume a state-level K-16 pipeline, only a college-readiness framing.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students receiving special-education services may arrive having been empowered to use AI for access and personalization (guidance p.14); institutions should align disability-services practice accordingly.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission scale; Washington's five-level scale and educator matrix are ready to lift and give continuity for adopting-district students (guidance p.20; educator matrix). Preserve Level 1 No-AI assessments where core skills are the objective.
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Do not rely on AI detectors, consistent with what Washington students were taught and the research OSPI cites (guidance p.40). Favor process-based integrity and voice-familiarity, and use conversations to resolve suspected misuse.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; the scale's five levels are a ready rubric for placement.
- **Human-centered framing:** Build on the "starts with human inquiry, ends with human reflection" principle (guidance p.14), which maps cleanly onto university expectations of original thought.
- **Special education:** Continue the access-and-personalization framing Washington students may have experienced (guidance p.14).

## Unique Aspects

- **A five-level student AI-permission scale plus a companion educator matrix** (student scale; educator matrix), one of the most complete and liftable instrument pairs in the dataset.
- **Cross-jurisdiction reach.** Puerto Rico's five-level scale is a verbatim Spanish translation of Washington's, corroborating the structure and extending its influence beyond the state (see Puerto Rico analysis).
- **A strong, research-cited anti-detector position** naming Vanderbilt and Stanford studies and English-language-learner bias (guidance p.40).
- **A crisp human-centered principle** that bookends AI use with human inquiry and reflection (guidance p.14).
- **Explicit special-education empowerment** to use AI for access (guidance p.14).
- **An educator matrix organized by real assignment types** with worked, discipline-specific examples at each of the five levels (educator matrix).
