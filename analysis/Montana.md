**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** The guidance is explicitly voluntary and built for local adoption. It is "designed to support district leaders in creating and refining implementation guidance," it recommends that "districts adopt the following principles" (p.5), and it repeatedly defers to local control. **Nothing in it is a statewide mandate on districts.** Every item below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example unless explicitly noted. Where the document references Montana statutes on student data privacy, those underlying statutes are Requirement-level even though the guidance restating them is not.

## Executive Summary

Montana's guidance is a recommendation-level, governance-first document produced by OPI and the Frontier Learning Lab at the Montana Digital Academy. It is organized around five Core Principles, then benefits and risks, teaching and learning, professional learning, accessibility, data privacy, policy and governance, and a five-phase implementation roadmap (TOC, p.3). The Montana School Boards Association (MTSBA) is releasing companion model policies districts are urged to consult (p.5).

It has no leveled student-use scale, and it makes a deliberate point of not importing one. Its "Frameworks - Merits and Risks" note says external frameworks "should be treated as adaptable references, rather than mandates," because "not all frameworks align with Montana's cultural, legal, or rural context" (p.7). The document's identity is Montana-specific rather than instrument-specific, and its most distinctive content is tribal. It embeds tribal sovereignty, the Indian Education for All (IEFA) Act, and Indigenous data sovereignty directly into AI policy (pp.10, 12).

For a higher-education reader, Montana carries an unusually explicit K-16 hook. Its "Constitutional Players" governance map names the Board of Regents of Higher Education as a formal actor, and a dedicated "Higher Education/Workforce Connections" section states that "The Montana University System plays a critical role in preparing teachers who can effectively integrate AI tools and in conducting research that informs K-12 AI implementation" (pp.12-13). The implementation roadmap repeatedly names universities, community colleges, and Montana's tribal colleges as partners for training, research, and educator preparation (pp.14-15).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human-centered AI is the spine.** The guidance "advocates for a human-centered approach to AI, emphasizing that AI should serve to strengthen human capabilities... rather than undermining human intelligence, agency, or the fundamental human-to-human relationships" (p.7). *[Recommendation / stated philosophy]*
- **Five Core Principles anchor local policy (p.5):** Data Privacy and Security; Transparency and Accountability; Bias Awareness and Information Verification; Human Oversight and Educator Judgment; Academic Integrity. Districts "are encouraged to adopt the following principles" (p.5). *[Recommendation]*
- **Montana's rural, urban, and tribal context is a first-order design constraint**, named on the cover-page framing and throughout (pp.2, 10, 12). *[Framing]*
- **Local control governs adoption.** "AI decisions ultimately rest with individual school districts" (p.12). *[Scope statement]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Prepared for "Montana educators, administrators, families, and policymakers" (p.1), and written primarily to help "district leaders" develop policy (p.2). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is all K-12, with integration meant to be "thoughtfully planned across subjects and grade levels in age-appropriate and progressively scaffolded ways" (p.7). No grade-band instrument is provided. *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no statewide permission scale and no statewide prohibition list.** Permitted and prohibited uses are delegated to districts, grounded in Montana law. District policy should be "tied explicitly to MCA and ARM references" (p.12). *[Recommendation / not a mandate]*
- **A distinctive restriction protects tribal materials.** Districts should "use IEFA-aligned resources as provided, without altering or remixing them with AI tools" (p.11). This is a specific do-not-use boundary tied to Indigenous data sovereignty. *[Recommendation, phrased as a restriction]*
- **PII is off-limits in tools.** The roadmap says to "avoid entering personally identifiable information (PII) into unauthorized tools" (p.15). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Data privacy rests on both federal and Montana statute.** Beyond FERPA and COPPA, the document cites the Montana Pupil Online Personal Information Protection Act, specifically 20-7-1325 (prohibiting targeted advertising, sale of student information, and non-educational use) and 20-7-1326 (requiring privacy safeguards in vendor contracts), plus a Montana Student Data Privacy Agreement where applicable (p.13). *[Recommendation restating Requirement-level statute]*
- **Indigenous data sovereignty is written into the privacy section.** "Tribes maintain jurisdiction over their data and digital materials" and have "the right to determine whether and how AI systems interact with their cultural knowledge" (pp.11-12). *[Recommendation grounded in constitutional commitment]*
- **Accessibility by design** requires tools to meet WCAG and Section 508 and provide text-to-speech, translation, and adaptive interfaces (p.10). *[Recommendation]*
- **Environmental impact is named as an ethics topic**, "energy, materials, water, and... carbon emissions" (p.7). Few state documents raise this. *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Academic integrity** is framed around "originality, transparency, and reflection," with clear expectations for "when and how students use AI tools" (p.5). Authorship and intellectual property get their own treatment, emphasizing "proper attribution, and acknowledging AI's contributions" (p.7). *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** the document does not discuss AI detectors. Its integrity approach is expectation- and reflection-based rather than detection-based. *[Instructional guidance]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Professional learning has guiding principles and recommended practices** (p.9): build foundational AI literacy, embed ethics and equity, support role-specific skills, emphasize human oversight, and encourage continuous growth. Recommended practices include tiered training, hands-on low-stakes practice, PLCs and cross-district networks, and parallel family learning. *[Recommendation]*
- **Curriculum integration is interdisciplinary, not a standalone course.** AI concepts "should be integrated interdisciplinarily across various subjects... rather than being confined to a discrete subject," built on "basic literacy, numeracy, coding, and media and information literacy" (p.7). *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation is a five-phase roadmap (pp.14-16):** I. Establishing a Strong Foundation and Vision, II. Developing Policies and Practices for Responsible Use, III. Capacity Building and Professional Learning, IV. Accessibility and Implementation Pathways, V. Monitoring, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement. *[Recommendation]*
- **Capacity-building leans on shared and higher-education infrastructure**, MTDA/Frontier Learning Lab, regional service cooperatives, state education organizations, and "Higher Education Partnerships" including tribal colleges (p.14). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

Montana names over-reliance directly and ties it to a specific pedagogical value, productive struggle.

- Its risk list states the offloading danger plainly: "Overreliance and erosion of learning processes: Students depending too heavily on AI may bypass critical thinking and the deeper learning that comes from productive struggle" (p.6). *[Instructional guidance]*
- It adds a social dimension few states name: "Erosion of interpersonal skills: Excessive AI use can reduce face-to-face interaction and social-emotional development, particularly in younger learners" (p.6). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Its mitigation is human judgment and critical evaluation.** "AI should prompt deeper critical thinking, not diminish it. Learners must learn to evaluate AI-generated content for accuracy, relevance, and fairness, cross-referencing with reliable sources... nurturing human judgment and avoiding over-reliance or passive acceptance" (p.8). *[Recommendation]*
- **Developmental scaffolding is a stated control.** Integration "should account for students' cognitive and emotional development, especially their ability to critically evaluate AI outputs" (p.7), and the ethics section flags "age limits for unsupervised AI interaction" (p.7). *[Recommendation]*
- **Human oversight is a Core Principle.** "AI should support, but not replace, educators. Teachers bring context, empathy, and moral reasoning that no machine can replicate" (p.5). All AI-generated content "should be critically reviewed before classroom use" (p.15). *[Recommendation]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a Montana graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and shaped by rural capacity, exposure will vary widely, and rural or tribal-district students may have had less tool access than urban peers.

- **Governed by a district policy grounded in Montana law, not a state scale.** A Montana student's AI rules came from a local, board-approved policy tied to MCA and ARM (p.12). *Basis: pp.5, 12, recommendations.*
- **Possible exposure to AI as an interdisciplinary literacy** rather than a discrete subject, built on foundational literacies (p.7). *Basis: p.7.*
- **Familiarity with critical evaluation of AI outputs** for accuracy, bias, and fairness, where teachers followed the guidance (p.8). *Basis: p.8.*
- **For students in tribal communities, an expectation that cultural materials are handled under tribal authority** and not remixed by AI (pp.11-12). *Basis: pp.11-12.*
- **Awareness of AI's environmental and ethical costs**, if their district taught the ethics content (p.7). *Basis: p.7.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. Rural and connectivity constraints (p.12) mean access-driven variance in Montana may be even wider than in states without those barriers, so a faculty member should expect a genuinely mixed cohort.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Montana embeds higher education into K-12 AI governance more explicitly than most states. The Board of Regents is a named "Constitutional Player," and the Montana University System is charged with "preparing teachers who can effectively integrate AI tools and... conducting research that informs K-12 AI implementation" (pp.12-13). A Montana institution may already be a formal partner in the pipeline feeding it, and can coordinate entry expectations accordingly.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because access is constrained by rural infrastructure (p.12), incoming Montana students may show high variance in hands-on AI experience regardless of aptitude. Faculty should plan for a common baseline at entry rather than assuming uniform prior fluency.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students from tribal communities may arrive with a clear norm that *cultural knowledge is subject to tribal data sovereignty* and should not be fed to or remixed by AI tools (pp.11-12). Institutions handling Indigenous materials or working with tribal students should respect and build on that norm.
- **Higher-education implication:** Montana taught AI as an *interdisciplinary literacy* tied to critical thinking and productive struggle (pp.6-8). Students from adopting districts may respond well to AI policies that protect difficulty and reward reasoning rather than output.
- **Higher-education implication:** The guidance names universities and tribal colleges as partners for *educator preparation and research* (pp.14-15). Teacher-preparation programs in the Montana University System are positioned to align their AI training with the state's K-12 framework.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Governance:** Where the institution is in Montana, confirm whether it is engaged through the Board of Regents role or the University System's teacher-preparation and research mandate (pp.12-13), and align entry expectations with the state framework.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry. Access-driven and district-driven variance will be high, especially between rural, tribal, and urban graduates.
- **Assessment design:** Adopt an explicit per-assignment AI-permission statement, since Montana offered no standardized scale. Protect assessments that require productive struggle, mirroring the state's stated concern (p.6).
- **Respect tribal data sovereignty:** For programs working with Indigenous materials or tribal students, adopt handling norms consistent with the IEFA and Indigenous data-sovereignty principles Montana students were taught (pp.11-12).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Favor reflection-, attribution-, and process-based integrity, which continues what Montana emphasized (pp.5, 7). The state guidance offers no detector endorsement to build on.
- **Faculty development and partnership:** Teacher-preparation faculty can align coursework with Montana's five Core Principles and roadmap, and can partner with tribal colleges as the roadmap envisions (pp.14-15).

## Unique Aspects

- **Tribal sovereignty and Indigenous data sovereignty written into AI policy.** The document grounds this in the Montana Constitution and the Indian Education for All Act, and sets a concrete restriction against remixing IEFA-aligned materials with AI (pp.10-12). This is Montana's most distinctive contribution.
- **An explicit "Constitutional Players" governance map** that formally includes the Board of Regents of Higher Education alongside K-12 authorities (p.12), plus a dedicated Higher Education/Workforce Connections section (p.13).
- **Rural realities treated as a design constraint**, including intermittent connectivity, multi-role educators, and reliance on regional cooperatives and shared services (p.12).
- **Environmental cost of AI named as an ethics topic** (p.7), rare among state guidance.
- **A refusal to treat external frameworks as mandates.** "Not all frameworks align with Montana's cultural, legal, or rural context... adaptable references, rather than mandates" (p.7). Montana deliberately does not adopt a Perkins-style scale.
- **State statutory anchors for data privacy** in the Montana Pupil Online Personal Information Protection Act (20-7-1325, 20-7-1326) and a Montana Student Data Privacy Agreement (p.13).
- **Named companion infrastructure:** MTSBA model policies and the Frontier Learning Lab at MTDA providing statewide implementation support (pp.5, 16).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness and local control limit every claim about students.** The document mandates nothing itself and routes decisions to districts, so actual student exposure is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **Rural access variance is structural.** The document itself flags that rural, tribal, and low-income districts may lack the infrastructure to adopt AI (pp.6, 12), so absence of exposure in some cohorts should be expected, not treated as a gap in this analysis.
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists**, by design (p.7). This is a genuine, deliberate absence.
- **MTSBA companion model policies and exemplar district policies are referenced but not in the folder** (p.5). Their content cannot be assessed here. *[Unresolved]*
- **Underlying Montana statutes (MCA 20-7-1325, 20-7-1326) are cited but not provided as documents.** The guidance's summary is taken at face value; the statutory text was not read for this analysis. *[Unresolved]*
- **Glossary and References (pp.17-20)**, including the "How AI Supported the Development of this Document" note, were not required for policy claims and were not transcribed in detail.
