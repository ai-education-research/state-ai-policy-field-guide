**Nature of the document.** Indiana's guidance is a **concise, voluntary** resource — "key considerations related to AI and education" with clickable resource icons and promised future professional development (Indiana-AI-Guidance.pdf, header). It is organized around AI Literacy, Instruction and Learning, Impact, Security, and Key Considerations for AI Policy. Nothing in it is a mandate; sources.md records "No enacted statute identified." Because the document is graphic-heavy, claims are cited by section name.

## Executive Summary

Indiana's guidance is short, literacy-forward, and framed around empowerment: "Teaching AI literacy is imperative to developing empowered learners and citizens" (Indiana-AI-Guidance.pdf, "AI Literacy"). It expresses student competencies as "I CAN" statements (for example, "Properly cite AI usage when writing or creating," "Act as an informed citizen," and, for AI/CS integration, "Ethically evaluate AI systems to minimize bias"). Its "Impact" section repeatedly frames AI as a support for, not a replacement of, student thinking: students "use AI to boost thinking and creativity, using tools to support, rather than replace, their own critical thought and original expression," while educators "intentionally preserv[e] academic rigor, promoting student independence, and cultivating transferable skills" ("Impact").

For a higher-education reader, two features stand out. First, Indiana explicitly advises districts to "Reconsider the use of AI detection tools as the sole source of intervention related to academic dishonesty" and links to a resource titled "AI Detection Software Doesn't Work. Do This Instead." ("Key Considerations for AI Policy"). Second, it recommends **folding AI into existing policies rather than creating a standalone AI policy** ("Policy Prerequisites"). The document is voluntary, so student exposure varies by district.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI literacy as civic and workforce empowerment:** literacy "transforms students into versatile problem-solvers and promotes critical thinking," quoting Code.org's definition of AI literacy (Indiana-AI-Guidance.pdf, "AI Literacy"). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI supports, not replaces, human judgment:** the "human-in-the-loop" approach is named explicitly ("Impact," Educator Experience). *[Recommendation]*
- **Integrate into existing policy** rather than build anew ("Policy Prerequisites," "Examine Existing Policies"). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Addresses educators, students, administrators, and parents/caregivers, with side-by-side educator/student framing throughout ("Instruction and Learning," "Impact," "Security"). *[Scope]*
- Policy guidance directs districts to "Provide clarity about who your policy applies to, including students, administrators, teachers" ("Process Guidance"). *[Scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **No statewide permission scale**; the document guides local policy. It advises districts to "Define acceptable and unacceptable uses of AI, but avoid restricting innovative educational uses or instituting overly broad restrictions" ("Process Guidance"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Avoid naming specific platforms** because "AI tools evolve quickly" ("Process Guidance"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Detection tools:** "Reconsider the use of AI detection tools as the sole source of intervention related to academic dishonesty" ("Process Guidance"), reinforced by a linked resource, "AI Detection Software Doesn't Work. Do This Instead." *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Security/privacy:** "Ensure inputs into public-facing AI tools are free from personal identifiable information (PII)," "Ensure compliance with FERPA/COPPA before using AI with students," and use age-appropriate approved tools ("Security"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Bias/ethics:** AI/CS integration competency includes "Ethically evaluate AI systems to minimize bias," and students should "understand how data is used to train AI and how that can lead to biased results" ("AI Literacy"; "Impact"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity:** handled via citation of AI usage ("Properly cite AI usage," "AI Literacy") and the detection-tool caution ("Process Guidance"). *[Recommendation]*
- A dedicated accessibility section is **Not found in the provided documents.** *[Limited]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **AI + Computer Science integration** is a named pairing, with competencies like "Break down complex problems," "Analyze data critically," "Identify patterns and trends" ("AI Literacy"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Educator sandbox:** "Develop a 'sandbox' where educators can practice making decisions about security issues related to AI" ("Security"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Feedback and time:** AI can make feedback "more immediate and actionable" and open time for "building relationships, developing content, and providing feedback" ("Instruction and Learning"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Policy process:** prerequisites include AI-literacy capacity before policy work, defining goals first ("Simply using AI should not be the goal"), stakeholder input, and flexibility ("Policy Prerequisites"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Resources:** TeachAI, ISTE, the CGCS/CoSN/AWS Gen AI Readiness Checklist, and an Indiana Learning Lab collection ("Resources"). *[Examples]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

- Indiana's central anti-offloading commitment is explicit: students "use AI to boost thinking and creativity, using tools to support, rather than replace, their own critical thought and original expression" (Indiana-AI-Guidance.pdf, "Impact"). *[Recommendation]*
- Educators "design learning experiences that leverage AI to enhance content understanding while intentionally preserving academic rigor, promoting student independence, and cultivating transferable skills" ("Impact"). *[Recommendation]*
- AI-as-scaffold framing: "Students using AI tools as a scaffold can open up time for higher level thinking and problem solving" ("Instruction and Learning"). *[Recommendation]*
- The "human-in-the-loop" model and educator modeling of output-monitoring ("Impact") reinforce human judgment. *[Recommendation]*
- A developed cognitive-science treatment (metacognition research, over-reliance studies) comparable to Arizona's is **Not found in the provided documents**; Indiana's guard is the supplement-not-replace and preserve-rigor framing. *[Limited]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

- **Uneven by district (voluntary).** Exposure depends on district adoption. *Basis: voluntary guidance; no statute.*
- **Where adopted**, Indiana students may bring: an "empowered learner/citizen" framing of AI literacy; the "I CAN" competencies including properly citing AI use and evaluating AI systems for bias ("AI Literacy"); a supplement-not-replace stance toward their own thinking ("Impact"); and awareness of PII/data-privacy cautions ("Security").
- **Higher-education implication:** Indiana-shaped students may arrive expecting AI to be framed as a scaffold that preserves rigor and independence, and expecting to cite AI use. Assume variance given voluntariness.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Indiana students from adopting districts may have been taught that AI detectors don't work as sole evidence ("Process Guidance"). Institutions relying on detectors will diverge from that experience.
- **Higher-education implication:** Indiana's citation expectation ("Properly cite AI usage when writing or creating," "AI Literacy") aligns with a disclosure-based integrity model; a college can build on that habit.
- **Higher-education implication:** The "Impact" section frames K-12 AI explicitly toward students who "will enroll in higher education, enlist in the military, or seek employment" ("Impact") — a college/career-readiness framing, though not a binding higher-education hook.
- **Higher-education implication:** Indiana's "human-in-the-loop" and preserve-rigor language ("Impact") gives a shared vocabulary a college can adopt for continuity.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Academic integrity:** Do not rely on AI detectors as sole evidence, matching Indiana's caution ("Process Guidance"); require AI-use citation as Indiana teaches ("AI Literacy").
- **Assessment design:** Design assessments that preserve rigor and student independence while allowing AI as a scaffold ("Impact," "Instruction and Learning").
- **Onboarding and faculty development:** Use Indiana's "I CAN" AI-literacy competencies as an entry rubric; consider an educator/faculty "sandbox" for safe practice, mirroring Indiana's recommendation ("Security").
- **Governance:** Fold AI into existing policies (acceptable-use, integrity) rather than creating a standalone AI policy, per Indiana's "Examine Existing Policies" ("Policy Prerequisites").
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Adopt the supplement-not-replace and human-in-the-loop framing ("Impact") for continuity from high school to college.

## Unique Aspects

- **"I CAN" competency framing** for AI literacy and AI/CS integration, expressed from the student's perspective (Indiana-AI-Guidance.pdf, "AI Literacy").
- **Explicit caution against AI detection tools** as sole intervention, with a linked resource arguing detection software "Doesn't Work" ("Process Guidance").
- **An educator "sandbox"** for practicing AI security decisions ("Security").
- **A concise, resource-linked format** (clickable icons) rather than a long framework — an accessibility choice for busy educators.
- **Integrate-don't-duplicate policy stance** ("Examine Existing Policies," "Policy Prerequisites").
