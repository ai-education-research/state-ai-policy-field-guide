**Nature of the document.** The roadmap is a **voluntary, dynamic resource** "designed to support our local school districts as they develop their own approaches" (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, p.3). Colorado is a strong local-control state; the document notes the roadmap operates within a context that limits "the state department of education's ability to dictate local technology policies" (p.17). It was co-produced by a nonprofit (CEI) with CDE, drawing on more than 100 stakeholders across five working groups plus a student advisory panel (pp.3, 6). Everything below is a Recommendation, Instructional guidance, or Example. Nothing is a mandate.

## Executive Summary

Colorado's roadmap is a collaboratively produced, principles-over-policy guidance document organized in three working-group domains: Reshaping Teaching and Learning, Advancing Equitable Access for All Learners, and Developing Policy for Transparent and Ethical Use (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, pp.6-19). Its defining stance is that "AI must serve to make the experience of teaching and learning more human, not less; to support students to build deeper critical thinking skills, not just stop at the easily accessible answer" (p.7).

Three features stand out for a higher-education reader. First, Colorado supplies two reusable instruments built for adoption: the **"If and How Checklist"** and an **AI Resource Evaluation Tool** based on ISTE's Teacher Ready Evaluation Tool (p.13). Second, it recommends an **appeal process "allowing students to contest AI grading and accusations of AI use"** (p.19) — an unusually student-protective due-process idea. Third, it takes a clear position that **current AI detection tools "are already failing to be helpful"** and that districts should rely on ethical-use guidelines integrated into conduct policies instead (p.15). Because it is voluntary and local-control, actual student experience varies by district, and the document leans deliberately toward "fluid guidance over rigid policy" (p.18).

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **AI to make learning more human and deepen critical thinking**, not to stop at "the easily accessible answer" (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, p.7). *[Recommendation]*
- **The four Cs of digital literacy** — critical thinking, creativity, communication, and collaboration — are "core to a student navigating an AI-infused future" (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity across a largely rural state**, framed around three digital divides (p.15). *[Recommendation]*
- **Principles over policy** given the pace of change (p.18). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Designed for K-12 public education, addressed to "educators, stakeholders, and community members," with role-based recommendations for districts and statewide organizations (pp.3, 20-21). *[Scope]*
- Higher education is present in the governance: the steering committee includes University of Colorado Denver and the CU Boulder Institute of Cognitive Science (p.24). *[Scope / higher-ed involvement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **A deliberately positive framing:** "focus on a list of 'yes's' and 'do's' rather than 'no's' and 'don'ts'" (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, p.18, "Promote a Positive Approach"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Acceptable-use modeling:** districts should "define specific use cases for both students and staff" to "delineate permissible, cautious, and prohibited uses of AI," and disclose when AI is used in critical decisions (p.18, "Acceptable Use"). *[Recommendation / model]*
- **Limit blocking tools:** "Limit the use of blocking tools, as students will likely use AI at home, which could inadvertently deepen the access divide" (p.15). *[Recommendation]*
- **No standalone prohibition list**; prohibitions are to be defined locally within acceptable-use frameworks and conduct policies (pp.18-19). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **AI detection tools discouraged:** "Current AI detection tools are already failing to be helpful to districts as AI 'learns' and improves. A more effective approach is to establish guidelines for the ethical use of AI and integrate these into existing conduct policies" (p.15). *[Recommendation]*
- **Student data privacy:** Colorado's "current Student Data Privacy rules are among the most protective in the nation"; regular policy and vendor-contract review recommended (p.17-18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Three digital divides** defined: the digital access divide (connectivity/devices/content), the digital design divide (educator capacity to design tech-rich learning), and the digital use divide (students as passive consumers versus creators) (p.15). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Accessibility:** UDL alignment and AI assistive technologies (screen readers, text-to-speech) for diverse learners (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity:** "Be open to rethinking academic integrity; include clear definitions of plagiarism and academic dishonesty while emphasizing how students can responsibly use AI as a tool, eliminating the need for a separate AI plagiarism policy" (p.19, "Student Use of AI"). *[Recommendation]*
- **Discrimination and harassment:** integrate AI into safe-school policies with disciplinary actions for inappropriate AI use and parent notification (p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **Procurement:** "avoid long-term vendor contracts and instead opt for pilot windows," with student-driven feedback to guard against bias (p.18, "Procurement"). *[Recommendation]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **AI literacy** defined via TeachAI and illustrated with the AI4K12 Five Big Ideas and Gwinnett County's AI Learning Framework (p.8). *[Instructional guidance / examples]*
- **Graduation requirements:** the roadmap recommends updating "local graduation requirements to include AI literacy, data literacy, and ethical AI use," building on Colorado's Computer Science Standards (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Balanced capability map:** eight ways "AI can support students," seven ways it "can support teachers," and five areas "where AI falls short" (emotional/cultural nuance, complex decisions, personalized interaction, contextual adaptation, and the need for human oversight) (pp.10-12). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Two named reusable instruments** (p.13): the **If and How Checklist** ("a guide to 'if and how' for AI usage in the classroom") and an **AI Resource Evaluation Tool** ("based on ISTE's Teacher Ready Evaluation Tool"). *[Instruments / recommendations]*
- **PD:** dedicated time and coaching so teachers can "experiment with AI in low-risk ways"; ongoing professional development as tools evolve (p.16). *[Recommendation]*
- **Implementation:** semi-annual review of AI plans (p.18); district and statewide next-step recommendations including device-access audits and student-inclusive task forces (pp.20-21). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

- Colorado's central anti-offloading commitment is explicit in the Reshaping-Teaching sidebar: AI should "support students to build deeper critical thinking skills, not just stop at the easily accessible answer" (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, p.7). *[Recommendation]*
- The **four Cs** (critical thinking, creativity, communication, collaboration) are positioned as the durable human skills AI must reinforce (p.9). *[Recommendation]*
- **Reflection on skill development:** "Teach students to leverage AI productively and ethically, including through reflection on their own academic skill development," and "teach students and educators to critically evaluate AI outputs" (p.15-16). *[Recommendation]*
- **"First Draft Thinking":** a guiding tenet encouraging safe experimentation while protecting PII and guarding against bias (p.18). *[Recommendation]*
- **The digital use divide** frames offloading structurally: students should use technology "to analyze, build, produce, and create," not "solely as passive consumers" (p.15). *[Instructional guidance]*
- A single named cognitive-offloading section is not present, but the critical-thinking-preservation framing, the "where AI falls short" list (pp.11-12), and the reflection recommendation together constitute Colorado's learning-preservation approach. *[Recommendation]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

- **Uneven by district (local control).** The roadmap is voluntary and Colorado emphasizes local control, so a Colorado graduate's AI experience reflects district choices. *Basis: pp.3, 17.*
- **Where districts adopted the roadmap's approach**, students may bring: the four Cs framing and AI-literacy grounding (p.9); experience with responsible-use expectations rather than blanket bans (p.18); a taught habit of verifying AI outputs and reflecting on their own skill development (pp.15-16); and possibly the expectation of an appeal process for AI-related grading or accusations (p.19).
- **Rural/connectivity variance:** given the state's rural profile and a documented access gap (64,878 households without reliable internet, p.15), some Colorado students may have had limited hands-on AI access despite the roadmap's equity aims.
- **Higher-education implication:** Assume variance. The most roadmap-shaped students may arrive expecting a "yes's and do's" culture, disclosure-based integrity, and due-process protections around AI accusations — expectations a college should be ready to meet or explicitly reset.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Colorado students from adopting districts may arrive expecting that AI detectors are not trusted and that AI-misuse concerns are handled through engagement and process, not detection software (p.15). Institutions relying on detectors will diverge from that experience.
- **Higher-education implication:** The recommended student **appeal process for AI grading and for accusations of AI use** (p.19) is more protective than most university integrity processes. Colorado students may expect a right to contest an AI-use accusation; institutions should define how they handle such contests.
- **Higher-education implication:** Colorado explicitly involved higher education (CU Denver, CU Boulder) in producing the roadmap (p.24) and recommends embedding AI literacy in graduation requirements (p.9). In-state institutions are positioned to align entry expectations with that pipeline.
- **Higher-education implication:** The "positive approach" (yes's and do's, p.18) means some Colorado students experienced AI as encouraged-with-guardrails. A restrictive or silent college posture will feel like a step backward to them.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Academic integrity:** Follow Colorado's lead — do not rely on AI detectors; define AI expectations within existing integrity policy rather than a separate AI plagiarism rule (mirrors p.15, p.19). Establish an explicit process for students to contest AI-use accusations, which Colorado students may expect (p.19).
- **Assessment design:** Adopt per-context acceptable-use definitions (permissible, cautious, prohibited) and design assessments that reward analysis, building, and creation over passive completion (pp.15, 18).
- **AI tool availability and equity:** Provide sanctioned, no-cost access and avoid over-blocking, addressing the rural access gap the roadmap documents (p.15).
- **Onboarding:** Use the four Cs and AI-literacy framing (p.9) plus the If and How Checklist and AI Resource Evaluation Tool (p.13) as ready scaffolds for student orientation and institutional tool vetting.
- **Governance:** Borrow Colorado's guiding tenets (transparency, positive approach, regular review, principles over policy, pp.18) and its procurement caution (pilot windows over long contracts, p.18).
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Adopt the "deeper critical thinking, not the easily accessible answer" framing (p.7) and the reflection-on-skill-development practice (p.15) for continuity from high school to college.

## Unique Aspects

- **A student appeal process for AI grading and for accusations of AI use** (Colorado-Roadmap-AI-K12-Aug2024.pdf, p.19) — a notably student-protective due-process recommendation.
- **Two ready-to-use instruments:** the If and How Checklist and an ISTE-based AI Resource Evaluation Tool (p.13).
- **A "positive approach"** framing ("yes's and do's rather than no's and don'ts," p.18) and "principles over policy" stance (p.18).
- **Three-divide equity model** (access, design, use) with an explicit push against over-blocking (p.15).
- **Embedded student voice** (a student advisory panel and student quotes throughout) and **higher-education co-governance** (CU Denver, CU Boulder on the steering committee, p.24).
- **Clear anti-AI-detector position** (p.15).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntary and local-control.** District adoption and student exposure are **Not found in the provided documents**; the roadmap is guidance, not a mandate (pp.3, 17).
- **The two instruments are named but not reproduced** in the roadmap body; the actual If and How Checklist and AI Resource Evaluation Tool live on the CEI website and are **Not found in the provided documents.** *[Unresolved]*
- **Colorado's "Consumer Protections for Artificial Intelligence law"** is referenced (p.18), but sources.md notes Colorado repealed/replaced its AI Act in May 2026 with a narrower disclosure framework that carves out FERPA-covered institutions; that statutory change is **Not found in the provided documents** and should be confirmed before citing the law's current form. *[Unresolved]*
- **Issuer nuance:** the roadmap is a CEI (nonprofit) product in collaboration with CDE, funded by the Gill Foundation — not a unilateral CDE issuance (pp.3, 24). Weight accordingly.
- **No enacted Colorado K-12 AI statute** appears in the provided files. *[Absence in provided files]*
