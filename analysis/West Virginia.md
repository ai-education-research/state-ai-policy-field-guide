**Nature of the document (read before everything below):** The WVDE guidance is voluntary and adapted from the TeachAI toolkit under a Creative Commons license; WVDE "is a Participating Government Agency in the work of TeachAI" (guidance p.~141). **Nothing in the guidance itself is a statewide mandate.** Its principles are Recommendation-level. However, the guidance repeatedly cross-references existing, binding West Virginia Board of Education (WVBE) policies (2460, 4373, 2520.14), and those underlying policies are Requirement-level. HB 5205 is an **introduced bill, not enacted.**

## Executive Summary

West Virginia's guidance is a recommendation-level, TeachAI-derived framework. It shares its DNA with several other states (notably Utah) that adapted the same TeachAI toolkit, and it uses the toolkit's signature device: first-person principle statements. Educators get five "I will..." Guiding Principles, and schools and districts get a parallel set of "We will..." principles, followed by a Prohibited Use of AI Tools section (guidance TOC, pp.14, 23-24). It does not use a leveled student-use scale.

Its principles track the familiar TeachAI set: use AI to help students reach goals; reaffirm existing policies and regulations; educate oneself about AI; explore opportunities and address risks; and advance academic integrity (guidance pp.14, 23). Its Prohibited Use section names bullying/harassment, overreliance, plagiarism and cheating, freedom-of-expression limits, equal access, and inappropriate material, each anchored to an existing WVBE policy (guidance p.24). It takes a clear anti-detector position: "it is advised that educators refrain from using programs that claim to detect the use of generative AI... due to concerns about their reliability" (guidance p.21).

For a higher-education reader, West Virginia's distinctive hook is structural. The West Virginia Board of Education roster lists, ex officio, the Chancellor who leads both the West Virginia Higher Education Policy Commission and the West Virginia Council for Community and Technical College Education (guidance pp.28-29). The same board that issued this K-12 guidance has higher education seated at its table by design, an unusual built-in K-16 governance link, though the guidance does not assign universities operational AI roles.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Human agency and existing-policy alignment.** The educator principles commit to using AI "in alignment with existing policies and regulations" and not sharing PII with consumer-based AI (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy for students and self.** "I will educate myself about AI. Promoting AI literacy among students is central to addressing the risks of AI use" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity as a culture.** "I will use AI in my classroom to advance a culture of academic integrity," preserving honesty, trust, fairness, respect, and responsibility (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Workforce awareness.** Students "should be informed of the possible ways in which AI will impact the job market they will eventually enter" (guidance p.~200). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- The guidance addresses educators (the "I will" principles) and schools/districts (the "We will" principles) (guidance pp.14, 23). *[Scope statement]*
- Scope is West Virginia public schools, grounded in WVBE governance and existing board policies. *[Scope statement]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **There is no leveled permission scale.** West Virginia uses a principles-plus-prohibited-use structure. Educators "may decide to permit the use of generative AI tools for certain parts or types of assignments, providing clear explanations for its restricted use" (guidance p.21). *[Recommendation]*
- **A Prohibited Use section sets explicit boundaries** (guidance p.24), each tied to a binding WVBE policy:
  - **Bullying/harassment** via AI-manipulated media is "strictly prohibited" (WVBE Policy 4373). *[Recommendation citing Requirement-level policy]*
  - **Overreliance** on AI is a named prohibited use (below). *[Recommendation]*
  - **Plagiarism and cheating**, tied to WVBE Policy 2460, which prohibits plagiarism and requires teaching its consequences. *[Recommendation citing Requirement-level policy]*
  - **Equal access:** "If an assignment permits the use of AI tools, the tools will be made available to all students." *[Recommendation]*
  - **Inappropriate material**, tied to WVBE Policy 2460's prohibitions. *[Recommendation citing Requirement-level policy]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Privacy is a principle.** Educators "will not share personally identifiable information with consumer-based AI" (guidance p.14). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity of access** is a prohibited-use safeguard, ensuring AI tools reach all students when permitted (guidance p.24). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity has a dedicated section and recommendations** (guidance p.21): permit AI selectively with clear explanations; refrain from unreliable detectors; and require citation of AI use, with MLA, APA, and Chicago resources provided. *[Recommendation]*
- **Position on AI detection tools:** advises against. Educators should "refrain from using programs that claim to detect the use of generative AI for cheating or plagiarism purposes due to concerns about their reliability" (guidance p.21). *[Recommendation — a caution against detectors]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Curriculum integration is grounded in existing standards.** WVBE Policy 2520.14 (College- and Career-Readiness Standards for Technology and Computer Science) is repeatedly referenced as the standards backbone (guidance pp.~687, 781, 926). *[Recommendation citing Requirement-level policy]*
- **A companion Canvas hub** supports educator development (sources.md). *[Example]*
- **Assessment guidance favors redesign.** Teachers "restructure assignments to reduce opportunities for plagiarism by requiring personal context, original arguments, or original data collection" (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **A living-document practice** with a version-history table tracks updates (guidance Version History). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

West Virginia names over-reliance as a predictable risk and a prohibited use.

- **Over-reliance is flagged as a predictable risk** early in the document, "predictable risks include over-reliance on AI" (guidance p.~186). *[Instructional guidance]*
- **Overreliance is a named Prohibited Use.** "Dependence on AI tools can decrease human discretion and oversight. Important nuances and context can be overlooked and accepted. Teachers will clarify if, when, and how AI tools should be used... through discussions and modeling, and teachers and students are expected to review outputs generated by AI before use" (guidance p.24). *[Recommendation, as prohibition]*
- **Human agency is a school/district principle.** Staff and students serve as "critical consumers" of AI, and "humans, and not the AI, will be responsible and accountable for pedagogical or decision-making processes" (guidance p.23). *[Recommendation]*
- **Assignment redesign protects original work**, requiring "personal context, original arguments, or original data collection" (guidance p.24). *[Instructional guidance]*
- West Virginia does not include a dedicated cognitive-offloading section or grade-band gating; its offloading control is the overreliance prohibition plus the human-agency principle. *[Structural note]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

These describe habits a West Virginia graduate *may* bring. Because the guidance is voluntary and district-implemented, exposure varies.

- **Governed by district policy grounded in existing WVBE policies**, not a state scale (guidance pp.14, 24). *Basis: guidance.*
- **A citation-and-transparency habit** around AI use, with MLA/APA/Chicago guidance (guidance p.21). *Basis: guidance p.21.*
- **An overreliance-aware, human-agency framing** where teachers followed the principles (guidance pp.23-24). *Basis: guidance.*
- **An expectation that AI detectors are unreliable and discouraged** (guidance p.21). *Basis: guidance p.21.*
- **Higher-education implication:** Treat these as ceilings of possibility, not guarantees. West Virginia set no state scale, so faculty should expect district variance and no shared leveled vocabulary, but a shared TeachAI-style integrity and human-agency framing where the guidance took hold.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** West Virginia has a built-in K-16 governance link. The WVBE that issued this K-12 guidance seats, ex officio, the Chancellor of the WV Higher Education Policy Commission and the WV Council for Community and Technical College Education (guidance pp.28-29). Higher education is structurally present in the body that governs K-12 AI policy, even though the guidance does not assign universities operational roles.
- **Higher-education implication:** West Virginia students were taught that *AI detectors are unreliable and should be avoided* (guidance p.21). Institutions relying on detection software may face students trained to distrust it.
- **Higher-education implication:** Students from adopting districts may arrive expecting *selective, per-assignment AI permission with required citation* (guidance p.21) and an overreliance-aware, human-in-the-loop framing (guidance pp.23-24). A syllabus that specifies AI use per assignment and requires citation will feel continuous.
- **Higher-education implication:** Because West Virginia grounds AI in existing College- and Career-Readiness standards (WVBE Policy 2520.14), incoming students may connect AI literacy to career readiness rather than to a standalone AI regime.
- **Higher-education implication:** West Virginia shares TeachAI DNA with several other states (e.g., Utah). A university drawing students from multiple TeachAI-derived states may find a common principle vocabulary (help students, reaffirm policy, educate about AI, explore/address risks, advance integrity) worth building on.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Assessment design:** State AI permission explicitly per assignment with required citation, mirroring the guidance (guidance p.21). Preserve assignments requiring personal context and original work to protect against overreliance (guidance p.24).
- **Academic-integrity processes:** Do not rely on AI detectors, consistent with what West Virginia students were taught (guidance p.21); favor citation-, disclosure-, and process-based integrity.
- **Onboarding:** Give a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry; district variance is high and no state scale anchors expectations.
- **Governance:** West Virginia institutions, particularly those under the Higher Education Policy Commission and the community/technical college council, can leverage their ex officio presence on the WVBE to align entry expectations with K-12 policy (guidance pp.28-29).
- **Human-agency framing:** Build on the "critical consumers" and human-accountability language West Virginia students may share (guidance p.23).

## Unique Aspects

- **A built-in K-16 governance link.** The WVBE roster seats the higher-education Chancellor ex officio (guidance pp.28-29), so the body governing K-12 AI policy includes higher education by design, an unusual structural feature.
- **A TeachAI-adapted, first-person principle structure**, five "I will" educator principles and parallel "We will" school/district principles (guidance pp.14, 23), shared with other TeachAI-derived states.
- **A Prohibited Use section anchored to existing WVBE policies** (2460, 4373, 2520.14), tying voluntary guidance to binding board policy (guidance p.24).
- **A clear anti-detector advisory** with a citation-first alternative (guidance p.21).
- **Grounding in College- and Career-Readiness standards** (WVBE Policy 2520.14), connecting AI to career readiness.
- **A version-controlled living document** with a change-history table (guidance Version History).

## Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

- **Voluntariness limits every claim about students.** The guidance mandates nothing itself and is district-implemented, so actual exposure is unknown and district-dependent. Per-district adoption data is Not found in the provided documents.
- **No leveled student-use instrument exists.** West Virginia uses a principles-plus-prohibited-use structure, not a 0-4 or 1-5 scale, and adapts TeachAI rather than the Perkins AIAS. This is a deliberate design choice.
- **The higher-education link is structural, not operational.** The Chancellor's ex officio WVBE seat (guidance pp.28-29) shows K-16 governance overlap, but the guidance does not assign universities AI roles or tasks. The hook is weaker than Tennessee's mandate or Virginia's assigned roles. *[Scope of hook]*
- **HB 5205 (WV Balance Act) is introduced, not enacted.** Per sources.md it would add screen-time limits by grade band, parental access to tools/credentials/browsing history, an AI sandbox course, and AI in CS standards, with State Board model-policy templates by Dec 1, 2026 and county adoption by July 1, 2027. All pending; no current requirement can be asserted. *[Unresolved]*
- **The underlying WVBE policies (2460, 4373, 2520.14) are cited but not provided as documents.** Their summaries are taken from the guidance's cross-references; the policy texts were not read for this analysis. *[Unresolved]*
- **PDF extraction produced font/ligature warnings**, but the principles, detector, and prohibited-use passages extracted cleanly and are verbatim-anchored; some page citations are approximate. *[Minor]*
