**Nature of the document.** A concise, **voluntary** guidance grounded in the U.S. Department of Education's 2023 Office of Educational Technology report and the National Center on Education and the Economy's Framework for AI-Powered Learning Environments (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.1). It offers guiding principles and a district-guidance-development process rather than rules; nothing is a mandate. Citations are by page.

## Executive Summary

Minnesota's guidance is short and principle-forward. Its five guiding principles are **Vision and Values, Center People, Advance Equity, Ensure Safety/Ethics/Effectiveness, and Continuous Improvement** (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.1). "Center People" is the through-line: "People, not machines, should be at the center of decision-making, and educators, students and families should retain their agency as the primary decision-makers" (p.1).

For a higher-education reader, Minnesota's standout is an unusually clean **cognitive-offloading statement**: "Without clear boundaries, students may rely on AI to complete tasks they should be practicing themselves, bypassing... the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity. Guidelines should distinguish between AI as a learning aid versus a replacement for thinking, ensuring students develop genuine understanding rather than simply producing AI-assisted work" (p.3). It also names "Overreliance on Technology" as a distinct challenge (p.3). Two other features: Minnesota explicitly encourages **learning from other states** (citing Digital Promise's review of seven states, p.5) and takes an **anti-ban** stance ("outright bans can shut down opportunities for learning and growth," p.4). It is voluntary, so student exposure varies by district.

## K–12 AI Policy and Guidance

### Primary themes and policy objectives
- **Five guiding principles** (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.1): Vision and Values; **Center People** ("agency as the primary decision-makers"); Advance Equity (digital equity); Ensure Safety, Ethics, and Effectiveness; Continuous Improvement. *[Recommendation]*
- **Learning aid vs replacement for thinking** is the framing objective: "guidelines should distinguish between AI as a learning aid versus a replacement for thinking" (p.1, p.3). *[Recommendation]*

### Intended audiences and scope
- Addresses school leaders, teachers, and district decision-makers, with a "Creating District-level Guidance" section (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.3). *[Scope]*

### Permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI uses
- **No statewide permission scale.** The guidance directs districts to "begin with existing guidance, procedures, and policies," particularly on data privacy, assessment, and academic integrity (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.3). *[Recommendation]*
- **Anti-ban:** "outright bans can shut down opportunities for learning and growth" (p.4). *[Recommendation]*
- Teacher tool-selection screen: age-appropriateness, value to learning, unintended consequences, data practices, and needs/framework identified before purchase (p.2). *[Recommendation]*

### Privacy, security, equity, accessibility, ethics, and academic integrity
- **Privacy:** "Ensure Safety, Ethics, and Effectiveness" principle; data privacy/security/content appropriateness as primary considerations; references Minn. Stat. 13.32 (Educational Data) (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, pp.1-3). *[Recommendation]*
- **Equity:** "Advance Equity" principle, invoking digital equity and awareness of biased data; "Access and Equity" named as a challenge (potential to widen the digital divide) (pp.1-2). *[Recommendation]*
- **Accessibility:** AI can "translate lessons and provide language supports for English Learners" and "generate audio descriptions" for students with disabilities (p.2). *[Recommendation / opportunity]*
- **Bias:** "Bias and Discrimination" named as a challenge; thoughtful prompt-crafting to improve results (p.2). *[Recommendation]*
- **Academic integrity:** flagged as an area to address with existing policy and community conversation before setting expectations (p.3). A specific AI-detection-tool position is **Not found in the provided documents.** *[Recommendation / limited]*
- **Youth mental health:** resources include "AI Guidelines for Youth Mental Health Tools (Children's Hospital Association)" for procurement of AI mental-health tools (p.4). *[Resource — notable]*

### Teacher training, curriculum integration, assessment, and implementation
- **Prioritize Staff Learning:** "differentiated professional learning opportunities for not only teachers, but all staff" (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.3). *[Recommendation]*
- **AI literacy for all learners**, including "how AI works and how it affects the world around us" (p.4). *[Recommendation]*
- **Knowledge sharing between districts:** create opportunities for ideas "to be shared, refined, and built upon"; include student voice in creating district guidance (p.4). *[Recommendation]*
- **Start from existing frameworks** rather than building anew (p.3). *[Recommendation]*

## Learning and Cognitive-Offloading Strategies

- Minnesota's cognitive-offloading statement is unusually explicit: "Without clear boundaries, students may rely on AI to complete tasks they should be practicing themselves, bypassing... the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity. Guidelines should distinguish between AI as a learning aid versus a replacement for thinking, ensuring students develop genuine understanding rather than simply producing AI-assisted work" (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.3). *[Recommendation]*
- **"Overreliance on Technology"** is named as a distinct challenge: "Excessive dependence on AI could reduce critical thinking, creativity and social interaction in the classroom" (p.3). *[Instructional guidance]*
- The **Center People** principle preserves teachers' role in "guiding students' development of critical thinking, collaboration, and application of knowledge" (p.1). *[Recommendation]*
- This is a clear learning-preservation posture, though not a research-cited section; it functions as Minnesota's cognitive-offloading strategy. *[Recommendation]*

## Expected Graduate AI Profile

- **Uneven by district (voluntary).** Exposure depends on district adoption. *Basis: pp.1, 3.*
- **Where adopted**, Minnesota students may bring: a "center people/human agency" framing; awareness that AI is a learning aid, not a replacement for thinking (p.3); AI-literacy grounding (how AI works and affects the world, p.4); and experience in districts that avoided outright bans.
- **Higher-education implication:** Minnesota-shaped students may arrive with a balanced, agency-centered view of AI and an understanding that some tasks require unaided practice. Assume variance given voluntariness.

## Implications for Higher Education

- **Higher-education implication:** Minnesota's learning-aid-vs-replacement-for-thinking framing (p.3) is a ready, plain-language principle a college can adopt to explain why some coursework must remain unaided — and Minnesota students may already recognize it.
- **Higher-education implication:** Minnesota's "start from what you already have" stance (p.3) applies directly to institutions: most already have academic-integrity, accessibility, and data-governance policies that cover more AI ground than assumed.
- **Higher-education implication:** The youth-mental-health AI procurement resource (p.4) is relevant to campus wellness and counseling AI tools; institutions can borrow the vendor-evaluation framing.
- **Higher-education implication:** Minnesota's explicit encouragement to learn from other states (p.5) models cross-institutional knowledge sharing that higher education could emulate for AI policy.

## Recommended Higher-Education Response

- **Governance:** Adopt the five principles (esp. Center People) and "begin with existing policy" approach (pp.1, 3) rather than building a standalone AI regime from scratch.
- **Assessment design and academic integrity:** Use the learning-aid-vs-replacement framing (p.3) to define where unaided work is required; address integrity through expectations and community conversation, not (per the silence on detectors) a detection regime.
- **Onboarding:** Teach AI literacy explicitly (how AI works and affects the world, p.4); run an entry check given voluntariness.
- **Support services:** Apply the youth-mental-health AI procurement guidance (p.4) to campus wellness tools.
- **Advancing learning rather than replacing thinking:** Center the overreliance caution and the foundational-skills/cognitive-capacity language (p.3).

## Unique Aspects

- **"Center People" as a named guiding principle** foregrounding human agency (MDE-AI-in-Education.pdf, p.1).
- **An explicit cognitive-offloading / foundational-skills statement** distinguishing AI as a learning aid from a replacement for thinking (p.3).
- **Explicit endorsement of learning from other states** (Digital Promise's seven-state review, p.5) and knowledge-sharing between districts (p.4).
- **A youth-mental-health AI procurement resource** (Children's Hospital Association, p.4) — a distinctive inclusion.
- **An anti-ban stance** paired with a "start from existing policy" pragmatism (pp.3-4).
