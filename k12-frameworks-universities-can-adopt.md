# Frameworks, Guides, and Best Practices Universities Can Adopt from K-12

*A curated catalog drawn from the project's 52-jurisdiction review of state K-12 AI policy (all 50 states, DC, Puerto Rico, primary documents). Organized by university function, not by state, so a reader can move from "I need X" to the best available instrument for X. Sources cite the state document as transcribed in the project's per-state analyses; verified URLs for the load-bearing documents are in `higher-education-ai-readiness-guidelines.md`, Annex B, and the complete ledgers live in `resources/<State>/sources.md`.*

*A note on borrowing. The states did this to each other first. Rhode Island reproduced North Carolina's acceptable-use guide in its own appendix. Puerto Rico translated Washington's scale verbatim. Virginia pointed its districts to Oregon's resource. Arkansas adapted Michigan's rubric. Adaptation with attribution is the established norm of this policy space, and a university joining it is following precedent, not shortcutting.*

---

## If you adopt only three things

1. **A per-assignment permission scale** with a genuine AI-free level (start from Oklahoma's, below, or the peer-reviewed AIAS it adapts).
2. **Vermont's two-question test** as the faculty habit for deciding when AI belongs in an assignment.
3. **A published institutional position on AI detectors and appeals**, informed by the fourteen-state caution documented below.

Everything else in this catalog strengthens those three.

---

## 1. Course-Level Permission Scales

The single most transferable K-12 invention. Each scale makes AI permission a property of the assignment, not the course, and pairs every level with a disclosure expectation.

**The peer-reviewed spine.** Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024), *The Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale (AIAS)*. Four states print this citation in their official guidance. A university adopting the AIAS can cite scholarship rather than a state PDF, and gains vocabulary continuity with students from the adapting states.

**Oklahoma — AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale** (OSDE Guidance v2.0, July 2025, p.18). Five levels, 0 through 4, each with a use description and a disclosure requirement. Level 0 NO AI Use. Level 1 AI-Assisted Idea Generation and Structuring. Level 2 AI-Assisted Editing. Level 3 AI for Specified Task Completion. Level 4 Full AI Use with Human Oversight. Its distinctive feature is the disclosure column, which escalates from an optional honesty pledge at Level 0 to citation plus **links to AI chat transcripts** at Levels 1 through 4. *University adaptation: the most directly liftable version for a syllabus. The chat-link convention gives faculty process evidence without surveillance.*

**Washington — 5 Step Scaffolding Scale plus the Educator Matrix** (OSPI Human-Centered AI Guidance v3.0, p.20, with standalone companions). Levels run No AI Assistance, AI-Assisted Brainstorming, AI-Supported Drafting, AI-Collaborative Creation, AI as Co-Creator. The companion **Example AI Assignment Scaffolding Matrix** crosses real assignment types (longform essays, reflective writing, podcasts and video, research, problem sets) against the five levels, with a worked example in every cell. *University adaptation: the matrix is a course-design template. Fill one row per assessment type in your course and the AI policy writes itself. Puerto Rico teaches a verbatim Spanish translation of this scale, useful for bilingual campuses.*

**New Mexico — the AIAS with verbatim level names** (NMPED AI Guidance, signed April 2025, p.19, printed Perkins attribution with CC BY-NC-SA mark). NO AI, AI PLANNING, AI COLLABORATION, FULL AI, AI EXPLORATION. *University adaptation: the cleanest one-to-one bridge to the peer-reviewed source.*

**North Carolina — the "0 to Infinity" AI Use Continuum v2.0** (NCDPI living document, Appendix B). Five levels, 0 through 4, published in **two versions**: a teacher edition with assessment-design guidance and a student edition with plain-language dos and disclosure requirements. NCDPI's implementation tips include annotating unit plans with the intended level and starting the term at Level 0 or 1 before progressing to 3 and 4. *University adaptation: the two-audience design is the insight. Publish a faculty version and a student version of the same scale, and sequence levels across the semester.*

**A numbering caution.** Oklahoma and North Carolina number from 0. New Mexico, Washington, and Puerto Rico number from 1. Pick one numbering institution-wide and say so, because incoming students may know either.

## 2. Assignment Design and Decision Tools

**Vermont — the two-question test** (Vermont AOE AI Guidance v1.0, Jan 2026, Cognitive Offloading section). Before permitting AI on any task, ask what skill students should develop in the activity, and whether AI will help them practice that skill or perform it for them. The same document empowers instructors to say no. "Teachers should feel empowered to say when AI usage is inappropriate." *University adaptation: this is a complete assignment-design heuristic in two sentences. Put it in every course-design workshop.*

**Arizona — microtask and productive-friction design** (NAU Institute guidance, pp.26-27). The most sophisticated offloading treatment in the corpus argues that not all cognitive offloading is harmful, and that the real question is which thinking students offload and why. Instructors decide "which cognitive muscles to focus on and which ones rest," and design AI-infused tasks where students must explain their reasoning. *University adaptation: the framework for AI-permitted assignments. Let AI carry the mechanical load while the assessed object becomes the student's reasoning about it.*

**Kentucky — the three paradigms** (KDE brief, p.12, citing Ouyang & Jiao 2021). AI-directed (learner as recipient), AI-supported (learner as collaborator), AI-empowered (learner as leader). *University adaptation: a lens for classifying any AI-integrated activity by how much cognitive agency stays with the student. Maps cleanly onto course design and onto rubric language.*

**Colorado — the "If and How Checklist"** (Colorado Roadmap, Aug 2024, p.13). A structured decision aid for whether and how a given AI use enters instruction. *University adaptation: an adoption gate for new AI uses in a course or program.*

**Georgia — the redlight system** (GaDOE guidance, p.12, adapted from Phil Hintz). A three-signal per-assignment convention for showing students how much AI is permitted. *University adaptation: the lightest-weight option in the catalog. Where a five-level scale is too much machinery, three colors on the assignment sheet still beat silence.*

## 3. Lines AI Must Not Cross

**Georgia — the high-stakes versus non-high-stakes table** (GaDOE guidance, p.4). Enumerates uses where AI should not be the basis of decisions, including subjective grading, educator evaluations, and individualized-plan decisions. *University adaptation: the model for a faculty-facing "never delegate" list covering grading, admissions review, advising holds, and accommodation decisions.*

**Oklahoma — the statutory guardrail** (SB 1734, 2026, sec.1.E). AI tools "shall not be used as the primary basis for student grading, discipline, placement, promotion, retention, or other high-stakes educational decisions," with educator-directed deployment and human-in-the-loop review of outputs. *University adaptation: ready statutory language for an institutional policy on consequential decisions. Utah's framework states the compact version, "AI will not be solely responsible for grading."*

**Illinois and Maryland — the support-versus-judgment line.** AI may assist the machinery around evaluation (rubric drafting, feedback organization) while the consequential judgment remains human. Maryland adds the mirror-image warning for staff, citing the documented tendency to over-trust automated recommendations. *University adaptation: pair the student-facing rule with the faculty-facing automation-bias caution. Offloading is not only a student risk.*

## 4. Academic Integrity Without Detectors

**The fourteen-state caution.** Six jurisdictions tell educators to refrain from or eliminate reliance on AI-detection software (Washington, Massachusetts, West Virginia, Vermont, Colorado, Maryland). Eight more bar it as sole evidence (Illinois, Indiana, California, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Arizona, New Mexico, Mississippi). Eight of the fourteen cite research or the tools' bias against multilingual writers. Louisiana stands alone in recommending detectors. *University adaptation: whatever position an institution takes, publish it. Fourteen state agencies wrote theirs down.*

**Washington — conversation anchored to the published scale** (OSPI v3.0, p.40). Instead of detector verdicts, know the student's voice through work gathered over time, and when misuse is suspected, hold a conversation anchored to the posted AI level for the assignment. *University adaptation: the procedural replacement for detection. The published scale converts an accusation into a shared reference point.*

**North Carolina — the teachable moment** (NCDPI living document, p.32). Suspected over-reliance "should be viewed as a teachable moment to reinforce the appropriate partnership with AI tools rather than a 'gotcha' moment." Vermont adds the welfare framing, treating integrity violations as potentially "a cry for help." *University adaptation: tone guidance for integrity offices. The first response is pedagogical, the process is preserved for genuine cases.*

**Disclosure and citation conventions.** Utah, West Virginia, Ohio, and North Carolina direct students to cite AI use in standard formats (APA, MLA, Chicago), with West Virginia linking the style guides directly. Oklahoma and Wyoming add disclosure statements plus chat links. Tennessee's model policy teaches "the difference between cheating and seeking support." *University adaptation: a one-page campus standard for AI attribution ends per-course reinvention.*

**Alabama and Colorado — the appeal right.** Alabama's template requires a process for students to contest AI-generated grades. Colorado recommends an appeal process covering both AI grading and accusations of AI use. *University adaptation: due process is the piece most campus AI policies forget. Two states already drafted it.*

## 5. Protecting Learning from Cognitive Offloading

**Vermont — the dedicated treatment** (AOE Guidance v1.0, Cognitive Offloading section). Cites Mollick's "Against Brain Damage," uses the calculator analogy (you would not hand a calculator to a student first learning multiplication), and frames the educator's task as distinguishing the valuable learning tool from the crutch. *University adaptation: the rationale text for why some coursework stays AI-free. Quotable in any syllabus.*

**Minnesota — the repetition rationale** (MDE guidance, p.3). "Without clear boundaries, students may rely on AI to complete tasks they should be practicing themselves, bypassing the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity." *University adaptation: the plainest-language justification in the corpus for AI-free foundational courses.*

**The AI-free floor.** Every leveled scale in the corpus reserves a bottom level where students rely solely on their own knowledge and skills. *University adaptation: a design principle, not just a rule. Any course using AI should also contain assessed work that certifiably does not.*

**North Carolina — semester sequencing** (NCDPI, Appendix B). Start the term at Level 0 or 1, progress to Levels 3 and 4 as students demonstrate disclosure, verification, and ethical use. Mississippi adds the mirror principle for supports, "gradually reducing students' reliance on these tools." *University adaptation: a semester arc. Early weeks certify unaided skill, later weeks grant earned AI collaboration.*

**Rhode Island — in-person performance checkpoints** (RIDE guidance, grade 9-12 band). Students "should demonstrate knowledge and skills with performance-based and in-person assessments to ensure they can perform without AI assistance." *University adaptation: periodic unaided demonstrations calibrate everything the process artifacts claim.*

**Pennsylvania — offloading as an assessed competency** (PDE AI Endorsement Framework, Domain 6). Teacher candidates must analyze "how cognitive offloading to AI systems influences attention, memory, and problem-solving," plus effects on socialization and belonging. *University adaptation: a ready module specification for faculty development and TA training.*

## 6. AI Literacy and Skill Frameworks

**New Mexico — the three-part literacy definition** (NMPED guidance, p.6, after Digital Promise). Users should "understand how AI systems function, evaluate its potential and risks, and apply it thoughtfully in real-world contexts." *University adaptation: a ready rubric for an entry diagnostic. Three axes, scorable in one short instrument.*

**Nevada — Understand, Apply, Integrate, Grow** (STELLAR Pathway, p.24). A four-stage learning arc for all stakeholders. *University adaptation: doubles as a within-course sequence for introducing any AI-permitted workflow.*

**The EVERY framework — Alaska and North Carolina.** Evaluate, Verify, Engage, Revise, and remember that **You** are responsible for the final product. *University adaptation: the verification habit in a mnemonic. The closing step, personal responsibility for AI-assisted output, is the norm universities most need to preserve.*

**Prompting frameworks, three options.** Missouri's Five S (Set the scene, be Specific, Simplify, Structure the output, Share feedback). North Carolina's CRAFT 2.0 with Power-Ups. Nevada's four elements (task, format, voice, context). *University adaptation: pick one and extend it toward disciplinary verification and sourcing, which is the college-level addition no state built.*

**Wisconsin — the five training pillars** (DPI guidance, June 2024). Understanding AI and Machine Learning Basics, Integrating AI into Subject Areas, Hands-On AI Experiences, Collaboration and Networking, Continuous Learning and Adaptability, each with named tools. *University adaptation: a complete faculty-development curriculum outline. Wisconsin's inclusion of librarians suggests the natural campus partner.*

## 7. Faculty Development Models

**Massachusetts — the Design Divide** (DESE guidance, after the National Educational Technology Plan). Access to AI and use of AI get attention, while the capacity to *design* meaningful AI-integrated learning experiences does not. *University adaptation: the diagnosis that should set the faculty-development budget. Most campus AI training teaches acceptable use. The gap is assessment design.*

**Virginia — exploration first** (EO 30 Guidelines, Strategies). Educators need "hands-on experience to understand these technologies' capabilities and limitations" through structured, guided experimentation before policy debates. Montana and Nevada add low-stakes practice settings and communities of practice. *University adaptation: run faculty AI workshops as labs, not lectures, before asking departments to write policy.*

**Maryland — the automation-bias module** (MSDE framework). Professional learning must counter "the documented tendency to over-trust automated recommendations." *University adaptation: the faculty-side offloading training almost no institution runs.*

## 8. Tool Vetting, Procurement, and Equity of Access

**Georgia — the seven-criterion Evaluation Process Rubric** (GaDOE guidance, p.10). A structured instrument for vetting AI tools before adoption. Vermont's Tool Vetting Framework adds the useful category split between general-purpose AI and education-specific chatbots. Colorado contributes an ISTE-derived evaluation tool, and Rhode Island a procurement checklist. *University adaptation: any of these converts ad hoc tool adoption into a reviewable process.*

**New Mexico — M.A.Z.E.** (NMPED guidance, p.10). Monitor data privacy, Assess for accuracy, Zero-in on bias, Evaluate value. *University adaptation: the pocket version, teachable to students as well as staff.*

**The tool-parity rule — Utah, West Virginia, North Dakota, Ohio.** If an assignment permits AI tools, the tools are made available to all students. North Dakota and Mississippi explicitly flag free-versus-paid tier gaps. *University adaptation: the equity floor. An institution that permits AI on assessed work either provides the tool or accepts that grades partially price a subscription.*

**Vermont — the training-data standard** (AOE guidance, Data Privacy). Removing student names is not enough, because student work itself is intellectual property that trains the vendor's model. Contract against secondary use. Alabama grounds its whole template in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework for institutions that want a formal risk scaffold. *University adaptation: two clauses for every AI procurement contract, no training on student work and no secondary use of student data.*

## 9. Institutional Governance and Self-Assessment

**Michigan — the maturity rubric** (Michigan Virtual AI Integration Framework). Three stages (Investigating, Implementing, Innovating) crossed with eight domains (Leadership and Vision, Policy and Legal, Instructional Framework, Learning Assessments, Professional Learning, Student Use, Operations, Outreach). The project's own analysis notes it works for a university by substituting "institution" for "district." Arkansas's adoption of it proves the rubric travels. *University adaptation: the annual self-assessment. Locate your institution in each row, then fund the lowest-stage domain.*

**Ohio — the fill-in-the-blank model policy** (DEW Model Policy, Dec 2025). A complete board-policy template with bracketed placeholders, covering purpose, definitions, literacy, stakeholders, privacy, procurement, ethics, acceptable use, integrity, and review. Its stakeholder section seats postsecondary partners by name. *University adaptation: the fastest honest starting draft for an institutional policy, and a reminder to put student and faculty representatives in the drafting room.*

**Maryland — the named owner** (SB 720). Every system designates a central-office AI coordinator. Missouri and Ohio recommend the same role. *University adaptation: accountability has a job title. Without one, AI policy is everyone's committee and no one's job.*

**North Carolina — the living document with a public update log** (NCDPI). Version history table, dated changes, continuous revision. Montana commits to a biannual review cycle. *University adaptation: publish the policy with its changelog. The log is what makes revision routine instead of controversial.*

**North Dakota — role-based checklists.** Five implementation checklists (School Leadership, Educators, Business and IT, Policy and Legal, Public Outreach). *University adaptation: the format insight. One institutional policy, five one-page role translations, so the registrar and the department chair each get their own to-do list.*

## 10. Student Rights and Support

**The appeal path — Alabama and Colorado** (see §4). Contest rights for AI-assisted grades and AI-use accusations.

**Rhode Island — accommodations protection** (RIDE guidance). Grading approaches must not penalize students for accommodations or modifications supported by AI, with explicit instruction for differently-abled students. Washington empowers special-education students to use AI for access and personalization. *University adaptation: an explicit carve-out in every course AI policy so a ban never makes assistive technology contraband. Coordinate with disability services before publishing any restriction.*

**Oklahoma — transparency and opt-out architecture** (SB 1734, secs.1.F-G). Annual disclosure of every AI tool in use, its vendor, the data collected, and its purpose, plus an opt-out that cannot academically penalize the student. *University adaptation: an annual student-facing AI tool disclosure would exceed nearly every university's current transparency, and the no-penalty principle matters wherever campus tools quietly embed AI.*

**Ohio — conduct provisions for AI-enabled harm** (DEW Model Policy, Other Considerations). Names non-consensual intimate imagery and AI-enabled impersonation for bullying and harassment, tied to state law. Six states carry deepfake or NCII prohibitions. *University adaptation: student-conduct codes written before generative AI rarely cover synthetic media harms. The K-12 language is a ready patch.*

## Provenance and cautions

- These are voluntary K-12 instruments except where statutes are cited (Oklahoma SB 1734, Maryland SB 720, Tennessee PC 550, Idaho SB 1227, Utah HB 218, Ohio ORC 3301.24). Adoption by districts varies, so treat the instruments as designs to adapt, not evidence of universal student experience.
- Several source documents are living and versioned (North Carolina, Wisconsin, Montana, West Virginia). Verify the current edition before institutional adoption.
- The Perkins et al. (2024) journal citation and DOI should be confirmed against the publisher before formal use.
- Full analytical context sits in `cross-state-ai-policy-comparison.md`. The institutional playbook, readiness verdict, and primary-source URLs sit in `higher-education-ai-readiness-guidelines.md` (Annexes A and B).
