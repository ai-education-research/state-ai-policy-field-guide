# Higher-Education AI Readiness Guidelines

*Prepared for university faculty and administrators. Primary and sole evidence source: `cross-state-ai-policy-comparison.md` (the 52-jurisdiction K-12 AI policy comparison), cited below as "Comparison §Section." State files (Tennessee.md, Oklahoma.md, etc.) are cited exactly as they appear inside the Comparison. No state practice, student competency, or policy requirement is asserted that does not appear there.*

**Labels used throughout:**
- **[K-12 Evidence]** — stated in the Comparison and traceable to a cited state file.
- **[Synthesis]** — an aggregation of Comparison content into a pattern.
- **[Inference]** — a forward-looking reading about students or institutions, not stated by any state document. Where the Comparison itself labels a claim [Inference], that label carries through here.
- **[Recommendation]** — an action for higher education, grounded in the cited evidence.
- **[Author framing]** — a characterization of higher education's own current condition. The corpus is K-12 only and cannot supply this; it is the author's professional observation, stated as the premise of the analysis, and testable at any institution by collecting a sample of current syllabi and counting the distinct AI policies.

## The Collision This Document Addresses

*The evidence base above is entirely K-12. The receiving environment — higher education — is deliberately **not** part of that corpus, so the following characterization is labeled as author framing, not corpus evidence, and no claim below rests on it.*

**[Author framing, not corpus evidence]** The question these guidelines answer is a collision. On one side, a growing minority of entering students were governed in K-12 by an explicit, often per-assignment AI regime: a leveled permission scale, a disclosure convention, a stated detector position. On the other side sits the higher-education reality this project was built to address — that AI policy is typically set **per instructor**, ranging within a single institution from a blanket ban to silent permission, and that a student's five concurrent courses may carry five different, mostly unstated AI rules. A student who in high school knew Level 0 from Level 3 and submitted chat-link disclosures can read a college syllabus that says nothing about AI as *less* structured than the system they left.

This document takes the K-12 side as its evidence and treats the higher-education side as the problem to be solved. Where a claim depends on the higher-education side, it is labeled [Author framing] or [Inference]; the corpus cannot supply it, and the honest answer to "is higher ed ready?" (below, and in the companion Comparison §Conclusion) turns on evidence about higher education's own current practice that neither document contains.

## Executive Summary

K-12 policy has already built the three things most university AI policies lack **[Author framing on the higher-ed side; see The Collision This Document Addresses]**, and some incoming students carry fragments of all three **[Inference]**.

1. **A shared, citable permission vocabulary exists.** Four jurisdictions print attribution to the peer-reviewed AI Assessment Scale of Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh (2024): New Mexico.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md, North Carolina.md. Washington.md runs a structurally compatible five-level scale, and Puerto Rico.md is its verbatim Spanish translation; Oregon.md recommends the AIAS to districts (Comparison §Executive Summary #1). An institution adopting the AIAS joins an existing chain rather than inventing a local convention (Comparison §Conclusion). **[K-12 Evidence + Synthesis]**

2. **Detector reliance is a defensibility problem.** Fourteen jurisdictions' guidance cautions against relying on AI-detection software (six discourage it outright, eight say it must not be the sole basis for a finding); eight of those files carry a named bias or research rationale; Louisiana.md is the sole state recommending it (Comparison §Major Differences #2, taxonomy). Some students can cite their own state's K-12 guidance against detector output (Comparison §Implications #3). **[K-12 Evidence + Inference]**

3. **Cognitive offloading is a named, taught concept.** Arizona.md reframes the question as "not whether students offload thinking, but which thinking they offload and why"; Vermont.md supplies a two-question task test; Minnesota.md states the repetition rationale; Pennsylvania.md makes offloading an assessed teacher competency (Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 1). Higher education can borrow this language instead of drafting its own. **[K-12 Evidence]**

4. **Some institutions are already named in state policy.** Tennessee.md binds public universities to adopt AI policies by July 1, 2025, with a legislative-hearing backstop; Maryland.md tasks a four-year institution with certifying K-12 AI tools; Virginia.md assigns roles to every public college; Texas.md exempts higher education from its AI-governance duties; New Jersey.md would require institutions to offer AI degree programs (pending) (Comparison §Implications #4). **[K-12 Evidence]**

5. **Variance within a cohort exceeds variance between states.** Most guidance is voluntary and district-implemented; mandates govern policy adoption, not content; several statutory baselines have no covered graduates yet (Comparison §Executive Summary #5-6, §Expected Graduate AI Readiness [Inference]). The operational consequence: **diagnose at entry, never assume.** **[Inference]**

**The three highest-leverage institutional moves**, per Comparison §Conclusion: check whether your state already names you; adopt a per-assignment permission scale with a genuine AI-free floor; and invest in faculty capacity to design AI-integrated assessment, the "Design Divide" named by Massachusetts.md and Oregon.md. **[Recommendation]**

### The readiness verdict, stated plainly

**Is higher education ready? No — in three provable senses, with one legal exception, and with one precision the framing must keep.** **[Synthesis + Inference]**

1. **No shared course-level vocabulary.** At least seven K-12 jurisdictions taught a leveled permission scale, four citing a peer-reviewed source an institution could adopt tomorrow; higher education has no counterpart convention (Comparison §Executive Summary #1). *The claim about higher education's side is [Author framing], not corpus evidence.*
2. **No published positions on the documented collision points.** K-12 wrote down detector stances, disclosure norms, and appeal paths (Comparison §Major Differences #2; §Shared Themes #6; §Implications #7); an institution without published positions on those three points will meet its first disputes unprepared. **[Inference]**
3. **No diagnostic capacity.** No one — including K-12, where only Idaho.md even directs an assessment, still pending — measures what entrants actually know about AI (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness). An institution that cannot see its incoming variance cannot manage it. This is the one deficit that is universal and fully corpus-supported. **[K-12 Evidence + Inference]**

**The exception:** Tennessee's public universities were statutorily required to adopt AI policies by July 1, 2025 — before most K-12 district deadlines — with a legislative-hearing backstop; compliance is unrecorded (Comparison §Implications #4; §Evidence Gaps). The corpus's only direct evidence about higher education therefore cuts *against* a blanket "higher ed lagged" story, and any skeptical dean can cite it. **[K-12 Evidence]**

**The precision:** the thesis is two independent claims, and neither may borrow strength from the other. *Claim 1 (merits):* K-12 built instruments higher education should adopt because they are good, regardless of how many students arrive trained. *Claim 2 (tail management):* a growing minority of entrants — not the modal student, whose composite profile probably lacks the leveled vocabulary (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness) — carry taught priors that will collide with improvised course policies; since no one can tell which students those are, the rational response is entry diagnosis plus one published institutional floor. Every scale instrument in the corpus dates from 2024-2025, so scale-socialized cohorts arrive in force roughly 2027-2029: institutions have a **preparation window**, not a crisis already missed. **[Synthesis + Inference]**

## Is Higher Education Ready? The Verdict

**The short answer is no, in three provable senses, with one legal exception and one timing grace period.** This section states the verdict the rest of the document acts on, and it splits the thesis into two independent claims so that neither has to borrow strength from the other.

**The two-claim thesis.**
- **The merits claim.** Higher education should adopt K-12's instruments because they are good on their own terms: a peer-reviewed permission scale, a disclosure-based integrity model, and a defensible rationale for AI-free work (Comparison §Executive Summary #1-3). This claim is true regardless of how many students arrive trained. **[K-12 Evidence + Recommendation]**
- **The tail claim.** A growing minority of entrants carry taught priors — per-assignment permission expectations, disclosure and chat-link habits, detector skepticism — that will collide with silent or improvised course policies. Because no institution can tell which students those are, the only rational response is an entry diagnostic plus one published institutional floor (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness; §Implications #2-3). **[Inference]**

Stated separately, the two claims resolve the corpus's own tension: the finding that within-cohort variance exceeds between-state variance weakens "students arrive trained" as a universal, but leaves it intact as a tail claim — and the tail is what collides first (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness [Inference]). **[Synthesis]**

**The three provable deficits.**
1. **No shared course-level vocabulary.** Seven K-12 jurisdictions taught a leveled permission scale, four citing a peer-reviewed source universities could adopt tomorrow; higher education, per the author's premise, offers none (Comparison §Executive Summary #1). **[K-12 Evidence + Author framing]**
2. **No published positions on the documented collision points.** K-12 wrote down its detector stances, disclosure conventions, and appeal paths; an institution without published positions on those three points meets the first dispute unprepared (Comparison §Major Differences #2; §Common but Inconsistently Implemented). **[K-12 Evidence + Inference]**
3. **No diagnostic capacity.** No one measures what entrants actually know about AI — and K-12 does not either; only Idaho.md even directs an assessment, still pending. An institution that cannot see its incoming variance cannot manage it. This deficit is universal and fully evidence-based (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness). **[K-12 Evidence]**

**The Tennessee nuance.** The corpus's only direct evidence about higher education's own obligations cuts against a simple "higher ed lagged" story: Tennessee law required every public university to adopt an AI policy by July 1, 2025 — *before* most K-12 district deadlines (Ohio's July 1, 2026; Oklahoma's 2027-28), with a legislative-hearing backstop for noncompliance (Comparison §Implications #4). A skeptical dean will raise this, so raise it first — and note what it converts into: compliance is unrecorded anywhere in the corpus, so a Tennessee public institution without an adopted policy today is not merely unready but past-due noncompliant. That is the single most concrete readiness finding available. **[K-12 Evidence + Inference]**

**The timing window.** Every scale instrument in the corpus dates from 2024-2025 (North Carolina January 2024, Washington March 2024, Wyoming June 2024, Puerto Rico August 2024, New Mexico April 2025, Oklahoma v2.0 July 2025). A student entering college in fall 2026 spent at most about two years under the earliest instrument, and only if their district adopted voluntary guidance. Scale-socialized cohorts therefore arrive in force roughly 2027-2029. The accurate urgency claim is not "they are already here" but "institutions have a two-to-four-year preparation window" — with one exception that collapses the window to zero: dual-enrollment students are living the collision now, governed by a K-12 scale and a college syllabus in the same week. **[Inference from instrument dates in the Comparison §Matrix]**

## What K–12 Students Are Learning About AI

Organized into the eight domains requested. Each carries a strength-of-evidence note. Everything below is a *ceiling of possibility*: guidance is largely voluntary and district-implemented (Comparison §Evidence Gaps #1).

### 1. AI literacy and responsible use — evidence: strong
- **Definitions students may have met:** New Mexico.md's three-part literacy (understand how AI works, evaluate risks, apply thoughtfully); Nevada.md's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle; Indiana.md's "I CAN" competency statements (Comparison §Recommended Higher-Education Response — Onboarding; §Matrix). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **A developmental arc** converged on independently by four states: Awareness (elementary) → Literacy (middle) → Fluency (high school), with open-ended chatbots restricted for young children (Vermont.md, North Carolina.md, North Dakota.md, Rhode Island.md; Comparison §Common but Inconsistently Implemented; §Policy Archetypes E). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Statutory floors are arriving, not arrived:** Utah.md requires AI literacy in the required grade 7-8 course; Maryland.md requires AI literacy in standards by June 1, 2027; Idaho.md directs literacy standards plus assessment, content pending; Alabama.md's CS/AI credit starts with the class of 2032 (Comparison §Major Differences #7). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **The universal frame:** AI supports, and must not replace, the student's own thinking and the teacher's judgment, recurring almost verbatim across unrelated states (Comparison §Shared Themes #1). **[Synthesis]**

### 2. Prompting and tool use — evidence: moderate, no convergence
- Named prompting frameworks taught as skills: Missouri.md's "Five S"; North Carolina.md's CRAFT 2.0 with Power-Ups; Nevada.md's task/format/voice/context; the EVERY responsible-use framework in Alaska.md and North Carolina.md (Comparison §Common but Inconsistently Implemented). **No single model dominates**, so no shared prompting vocabulary can be assumed. **[K-12 Evidence + Synthesis]**
- Tool environments differ sharply: Puerto Rico.md mandates one platform (MS Copilot) under supervision; Tennessee.md districts run approved-tools-only models; North Carolina.md and Wisconsin.md students may have used hands-on model-building tools (Teachable Machine, Machine Learning for Kids); South Carolina.md CTE completers may hold real Python/model-building skills their peers lack (Comparison §Matrix; §Expected Graduate AI Readiness). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 3. Privacy, security, ethics, bias, and accessibility — evidence: strong
- **FERPA and COPPA are the universal floor**, frequently joined by CIPA, IDEA, PPRA, Section 504, and the ADA, with state statutes layered on top in Montana.md, Utah.md, Oregon.md, and Louisiana.md (Comparison §Shared Themes #4). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **"Never put PII into consumer AI"** is a near-universal, concretely stated rule; Vermont.md goes furthest, teaching that de-identification is insufficient because student intellectual property still trains the model (Comparison §Shared Themes #5). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Equity of access as a condition of permission:** if an assignment permits AI, the tool must be available to every student (Utah.md, West Virginia.md, North Dakota.md, Ohio.md), with free-versus-paid tier gaps named (North Dakota.md, Mississippi.md; Comparison §Shared Themes #8). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Accessibility commitments:** WCAG/Section 508 procurement standards (Montana.md), accessibility written into procurement (New Mexico.md), and non-penalization of AI-supported accommodations (Rhode Island.md) (Comparison §Recommended Response — Access and equity). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Deepfake and non-consensual-imagery prohibitions** appear in at least six jurisdictions (Ohio.md, Oklahoma.md, Utah.md, West Virginia.md, Montana.md, North Carolina.md; Comparison §Common but Inconsistently Implemented). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 4. Academic integrity and attribution — evidence: strong
- **Disclosure and citation, not surveillance, is the taught integrity mechanism:** cite AI in APA/MLA/Chicago (Utah.md, West Virginia.md, Ohio.md, North Carolina.md); submit disclosure statements (Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md); educators model disclosure themselves (North Carolina.md, West Virginia.md) (Comparison §Shared Themes #6). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **The most granular convention:** Oklahoma.md and Wyoming.md students submit **links to their AI chat transcripts** with work (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness — Disclosure habits). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Detector stances form a taxonomy:** six discourage/refrain, eight caution against sole reliance (fourteen total), Louisiana.md recommends, Oregon.md and Utah.md are tolerant-with-caveats, and roughly half the corpus is silent — silence being a gap, not a position (Comparison §Major Differences #2; §Evidence Gaps #8). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- Tennessee.md districts teach the difference between "cheating and seeking support" (Comparison §Matrix — Tennessee row). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 5. Critical evaluation of AI outputs — evidence: strong
- **Verification is framed as a personal obligation:** the EVERY framework ends with "You are responsible" (Alaska.md, North Carolina.md), and Missouri.md and Montana.md require review of AI output before use (Comparison §Recommended Response — Deeper learning). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Critique is taught from middle school** in the gating states: students were taught to critique AI output for errors and bias starting in grades 6-8 (North Dakota.md, North Carolina.md, Rhode Island.md, Vermont.md; Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- Wisconsin.md delivers the same habit through a librarian-led media-literacy lens (Comparison §Matrix — Wisconsin row). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 6. Subject-matter learning and independent reasoning — evidence: strong
- **Every leveled scale reserves an AI-free floor** whose stated purpose is preserving unaided cognition — worded state by state as students relying solely or entirely on their own knowledge and skills (verbatim variants per state in Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 2: OK, WY, WA, NC, NM, PR), plus Louisiana.md's AI-Prohibited tier and Georgia.md's RED level. **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Assessment redesign over enforcement:** personal context, original arguments, original data (Utah.md, West Virginia.md); reasoning, analysis, reflection, and process over recall with visible student thinking (Maryland.md); performance-based, in-person demonstration at grades 9-12 (Rhode Island.md); process versus product (Oregon.md); real-world, collaborative tasks (Mississippi.md) (Comparison §Shared Themes #7). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 7. Cognitive offloading and appropriate human oversight — evidence: strong where present, absent elsewhere
- **Tier-1 treatments students may have absorbed:** Arizona.md's "which thinking they offload and why" plus a microtask/productive-friction design; Vermont.md's calculator analogy and two-question task test, with teachers "empowered to say when AI usage is inappropriate"; Minnesota.md's warning against bypassing "the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity"; Utah.md naming agentic AI's offloading of "decision making and/or cognitive effort" as a governed risk (Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 1). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Human-oversight guardrails with legal force in one state:** Oklahoma.md bars AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions by statute (Comparison §Common but Inconsistently Implemented — due process). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- Maryland.md also cautions *educators* against over-trusting automated recommendations (Goddard et al., 2012, as cited there; Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 4). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- Thirteen jurisdictions' captured documents do not address offloading at all (Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 5). **[K-12 Evidence]**

### 8. Collaboration, creativity, and problem-solving with AI — evidence: thin
- The corpus records this mainly **structurally**, through the upper tiers of permission scales, which legitimize supervised co-creation (Louisiana.md's AI-Empowered tier "redefining the task"; the top levels of the seven scale jurisdictions), and through hands-on model-building exposure (North Carolina.md, Wisconsin.md) (Comparison §Matrix; §Expected Graduate AI Readiness). **[K-12 Evidence]**
- This is the least-developed domain in the Comparison; claims about incoming students' collaborative-AI skill should be correspondingly modest. **[Synthesis]**

## Incoming Student AI Profile

The Comparison's realistic composite: a student governed by a district acceptable-use policy rather than a state scale; told AI should support rather than replace their thinking; who may or may not have been taught to cite AI; who probably lacks a leveled permission vocabulary; and who, if from one of fourteen states whose guidance cautioned against detector reliance, may bring skepticism of AI detectors (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness [Inference]). Around that composite:

### Capabilities higher education can reasonably expect
*Probable for students from guidance states; still verify.* **[Inference, grounded]**
- Awareness that AI use in coursework is **governed**, and that misuse is an integrity matter (district-AUP composite; Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
- The **support-not-replace** frame for AI's role in their own thinking (Comparison §Shared Themes #1, near-universal).
- Basic awareness that **AI output can be wrong or biased** (verification duties and middle-school critique instruction across many states; Comparison §What students were taught, domains 5 and 7).

### Capabilities that vary significantly by state or district
**[K-12 Evidence for the variance itself]**
- **Leveled permission vocabulary** — real only in the seven scale jurisdictions (NM, OK, NC, WA, WY, PR, LA); explicitly absent in roughly two dozen others (Comparison §Common but Inconsistently Implemented).
- **Disclosure habits** — chat-link submission (OK, WY); process evidence such as drafts and chat links (NC); formal citation formats (UT, WV, OH) (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
- **Detector priors** — guidance-level skepticism (14 states, two strengths), guidance-level acceptance (Louisiana.md), tolerant-with-caveats (Oregon.md, Utah.md), or unknown (silent states); student-level exposure hedged as "may arrive."
- **Prompting frameworks** — four different taught models, or none.
- **Tool familiarity** — single-platform supervised (PR), approved-tools-only (TN districts), hands-on model building (NC, WI), genuine technical AI/ML skill (SC CTE completers), or no state-shaped baseline at all (NE, SD, TX).
- **Formal AI-literacy coursework** — required only in Utah (grades 7-8); everywhere else elective or informal.

### Common misconceptions or weak areas
**[Inference, grounded in Comparison]**
- **Permission-scope confusion.** Scale-state students expect AI rules to attach to the *task*; others expect one global rule. Either group misreads the other's default, and both misread a silent syllabus (Comparison §Implications #2).
- **Detector-legitimacy mismatch.** A Louisiana-taught student and a Washington-taught student hold opposite, *both-taught* beliefs about detection software (Comparison §Major Differences #2).
- **Unverified self-assessment.** Formal evaluation of AI literacy is almost nonexistent; only Idaho.md even directs assessment guidelines, still pending. Students' sense of their own AI competence has rarely been tested (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness — structural facts).
- **Tool-specific generalization.** Puerto Rico.md students' fluency is platform-specific (Copilot, supervised); it will not automatically transfer to an open multi-tool campus (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).

### Skills that should not be assumed
**[Synthesis]**
- A shared permission vocabulary of any kind; prompt-engineering proficiency; consistent citation or disclosure habits; technical AI/ML skills outside the CTE tail; explicit understanding of *when not to use AI* (taught mainly in the Tier-1 offloading and gating states); experience with institutional appeal or due-process mechanics (evidenced only in Alabama.md and Colorado.md).

### Differences in access, exposure, and readiness
**[K-12 Evidence]**
- **Nine no-state-framework jurisdictions** (NE, SD, TX, NY, SC, IA, KS, AR, NH) provide no baseline at all (Comparison §Policy Archetypes H).
- **Access-driven variance is structural**, not attitudinal: rural connectivity and budget limits (Montana.md), free-versus-paid tool tiers (North Dakota.md, Mississippi.md), equity concerns (Nevada.md, Iowa.md) (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
- **The gating states deliberately limited early exposure**, so their *younger* cohorts have less AI history, by design, not deficit (Vermont.md, North Carolina.md; Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
- **Timing gaps:** Alabama's requirement covers the class of 2032; Iowa's proposed requirement 2030-31; California's model policy posted June 2026; Idaho's content is pending; Oklahoma's district policies bind before 2027-28. Near-term entrants predate all of these (Comparison §Executive Summary #6).

## What Higher Education Should and Should Not Assume

### Should not assume — the nine traps
**[Synthesis unless noted]**
1. A uniform baseline of any kind: within-cohort range exceeds between-state range (Comparison §Expected Graduate AI Readiness [Inference]).
2. A shared leveled vocabulary (seven jurisdictions only).
3. Citation or disclosure habits (the composite says "may or may not").
4. Any particular detector prior, in either direction.
5. That a state mandate reached current students: mandates govern *adoption*, not content (Tennessee.md, Ohio.md; Comparison §Executive Summary #5), and several take effect years out.
6. That "the state has guidance" means the student experienced it: voluntariness plus district implementation everywhere (Comparison §Evidence Gaps #1).
7. That CTE-style technical skill generalizes to the cohort (South Carolina.md).
8. That students were ever formally assessed on AI literacy (only Idaho.md directs it, pending).
9. That a state file's silence on a practice means the state rejects it (Comparison §Scope and Method) — this discipline applies to institutional planning too.

## Guidelines for Faculty

### Course-level AI expectations — [Recommendation]
1. **Declare an AI level for every assignment**, not one global course rule. Scale-state students experienced permission as a property of the task, posted and explicitly taught (North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Washington.md; Comparison §Implications #2). A silent or blanket syllabus reads as *less* clear than their high school (Comparison §Implications #2 [Inference]).
2. **Use the AIAS as the level vocabulary** where the institution has not chosen one: it is the peer-reviewed source four states print, and structurally compatible with Washington's and Puerto Rico's scales (Comparison §Executive Summary #1).
3. **Reserve a genuine AI-free floor** for assessments whose goal is core-skill demonstration, the one control every scale state implemented (Comparison §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 2).
4. **Keep AI out of consequential judgment about students.** Follow Georgia.md's high-stakes table, Oklahoma.md's statutory bar, Illinois.md's and Maryland.md's support-versus-high-stakes lines, and Utah.md's rule that AI "will not be solely responsible for grading" (Comparison §Recommended Response — Assessment).

### Syllabus language — [Recommendation]
Every syllabus should answer six questions, each grounded in a taught K-12 practice:
- **Which scale governs, and what is the default level?** (AIAS continuity; §Executive Summary #1)
- **How is AI use disclosed?** Named citation format, disclosure statement, and, where process transparency matters, links to AI chats (UT/WV/OH/NC; OK/WY; §Shared Themes #6).
- **Which tools are permitted, and are they provided?** Tool-parity rule (UT, WV, ND, OH; §Shared Themes #8).
- **Why are some tasks AI-free?** Borrow the taught rationales: the calculator analogy (Vermont.md) and the repetition argument (Minnesota.md) (§Cognitive-Offloading Strategies).
- **What happens on suspicion?** A conversation anchored to the published level, not a detector verdict (Washington.md; North Carolina.md's "teachable moment"; §Recommended Response — Student support).
- **How does a student contest a finding or an AI-assisted grade?** (Alabama.md, Colorado.md; §Implications #7.)

*Model clause, synthesized from the cited practices* **[Recommendation — model language]**: "Each assignment in this course carries a posted AI level, from an AI-free floor (no AI at any point; you rely on your own knowledge and skills) to full, disclosed AI collaboration. Where AI is permitted, you must disclose how you used it, cite it in [format], and retain your chat links. I will never treat detector output as proof; concerns begin with a conversation, and you may appeal any AI-related finding." *(Numbering note: state adaptations differ — Oklahoma and North Carolina number the floor Level 0; New Mexico and Puerto Rico number it Level 1. Pick one numbering, publish it, and keep it; see the Instruments Annex.)*

### Assessment and assignment design — [Recommendation]
- Design **AI-resilient tasks**: personal context, original arguments, original data collection (Utah.md, West Virginia.md); reasoning, analysis, reflection, and process over recall, with visible evidence of student thinking (Maryland.md); real-world and collaborative work (Mississippi.md) (Comparison §Shared Themes #7).
- **Collect process artifacts** — outlines, drafts, chat links — as the assessed object at AI-permitted levels (North Carolina.md, Oklahoma.md, Wyoming.md; §Recommended Response — Assessment).
- **Anchor unaided competence** with performance-based, in-person checkpoints, the practice Rhode Island.md sets for grades 9-12 (§Shared Themes #7).
- **Sequence levels across the term**: begin at Level 0-1 and progress toward 3-4 as students demonstrate disclosure, verification, and ethical use (North Carolina.md's stated progression; Mississippi.md's scaffolding-fade principle; §Recommended Response — Deeper learning).

### Teaching practices that reduce cognitive offloading — [Recommendation]
See §Preventing Cognitive Offloading below for the full playbook; the two course-level essentials:
- Apply Vermont.md's two-question test before permitting AI on any task: what skill should students develop, and will AI help them practice it or perform it for them (§Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 1).
- Use Arizona.md's microtask/productive-friction design: decide "which cognitive muscles to focus on and which ones rest," and require students to explain their reasoning inside AI-infused tasks (§Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 1).

### AI literacy instruction — [Recommendation]
- Teach evaluation before generation: the verification duty (EVERY's "You are responsible," Alaska.md/North Carolina.md) and output critique for accuracy and bias, extending the middle-school habit the gating states built (ND, NC, RI, VT; §Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
- Use New Mexico.md's **teaching-with versus teaching-about** distinction and its interrogation prompts as ready-made classroom moves (§Recommended Response — Faculty development).
- Extend, do not restart, prompting instruction: students may arrive with Five S, CRAFT, or task/format/voice/context; the college-level addition is disciplinary verification and sourcing (§Common but Inconsistently Implemented) **[Inference]**.

### Feedback, attribution, and academic-integrity processes — [Recommendation]
- **Accept and normalize attribution**: APA/MLA/Chicago AI citation plus disclosure statements; invite chat links where they help (UT, WV, OH, NC; OK, WY; §Shared Themes #6).
- **Handle suspicion by conversation, not verdict**: anchored to the posted level (Washington.md), framed as a teachable moment (North Carolina.md), alert to distress (Vermont.md's "cry for help" framing) (§Recommended Response — Student support).
- **Never use a detector as sole or decisive evidence.** Fourteen states' guidance cautioned against relying on the tools, and eight of those files cite unreliability or bias against multilingual writers; expect some students to cite that guidance (§Implications #3). If the institution permits detectors at all, publish that position explicitly (§Recommended Response — Student support).
- **Do not penalize AI-supported accommodations** (Rhode Island.md; Washington.md's special-education empowerment; §Recommended Response — Access and equity).
- **Keep grading human.** AI may support instructor workflow, but consequential evaluation stays with the instructor (Utah.md; IL/MD support-versus-high-stakes lines; §Recommended Response — Assessment).

## Guidelines for Administrators

### Institution-wide AI policy — [Recommendation]
1. **Check your statutory position first.** Tennessee public institutions were required to adopt AI policies by July 1, 2025, with a noncompliance hearing; Maryland names a four-year institution to certify K-12 tools; Virginia assigns roles to every public college; Texas exempts higher education; New Jersey's program-offering mandate is pending (Comparison §Implications #4).
2. **Adopt one institutional permission scale with an AI-free floor**, and require every syllabus to use it. Learn from the mandate states' gap: requiring that a policy exist without minimum content reproduces K-12's variance problem (Tennessee.md, Ohio.md; §Executive Summary #5) **[Synthesis]**.
3. **Integrate rather than proliferate.** Fold AI into existing integrity, conduct, and procurement policy with cross-references, the Kentucky.md normalization and Indiana.md fold-in approach, rather than a standalone parallel regime (§Major Differences #6).
4. **Publish the institution's detector position explicitly** — the corpus guarantees students arrive with contradictory taught priors (§Recommended Response — Student support).
5. **Operate a review cycle, not a document**: scheduled review plus a public update log (Montana.md, Missouri.md, Ohio.md, North Carolina.md; §Recommended Response — Governance).
6. **Build the appeal path** covering AI-assisted grading and AI-use allegations (Alabama.md, Colorado.md), and keep AI out of high-stakes decisions institutionally, mirroring Oklahoma.md's statutory bar (§Common but Inconsistently Implemented).

### Approved tool availability — [Recommendation]
- **Guarantee tool parity**: any tool an assignment permits, the institution provides to every enrolled student (UT, WV, ND, OH; §Shared Themes #8). Audit free-versus-paid tiers annually (ND, MS; §Recommended Response — Access and equity).
- **Adopt a vetting rubric**: Georgia.md's seven-criterion evaluation rubric, Vermont.md's Tool Vetting Framework, Colorado.md's ISTE-derived evaluation tool, and Rhode Island.md's procurement checklist are ready templates (§Common but Inconsistently Implemented).
- **Contract against training on student work.** Vermont.md's position — de-identification is insufficient because student intellectual property still trains the model — is the strongest taught privacy standard; procurement should meet it (§Shared Themes #5).
- **Meet accessibility standards in procurement** (WCAG/508 per Montana.md; accessibility-in-procurement per New Mexico.md; §Recommended Response — Access and equity).

### Student onboarding — [Recommendation]
Fund and mandate the program described in §Student Onboarding Recommendations below: an entry diagnostic, a common-floor module, and targeted bridges. Onboarding is where the corpus's variance problem is either absorbed or passed through to every classroom **[Synthesis]**.

### Faculty development — [Recommendation]
- **Target the Design Divide** — faculty capacity to *design* AI-integrated learning, the gap Massachusetts.md names and Oregon.md echoes — not merely acceptable-use awareness (§Implications #9).
- **Run exploration-first, low-stakes PD**: hands-on structured experimentation before policy debate (Virginia.md), safe practice settings and communities of practice (Montana.md, Nevada.md) (§Recommended Response — Faculty development).
- **Import ready pedagogy** rather than commissioning it: Arizona.md's microtask design, Vermont.md's two-question test, New Mexico.md's distinctions and prompts, Wisconsin.md's five training pillars (§Recommended Response — Faculty development).
- **Train against automation bias**, the educator-side offloading risk Maryland.md names (§Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 4).
- **Align teacher-preparation programs** with the state instruments that now govern them: Pennsylvania.md's six-domain, 12-credit endorsement with a PK-12 capstone; Georgia.md's educator-preparation involvement; Montana.md's university-system mandate; Iowa.md's pending invitation to CS teacher-prep institutions (§Implications #5).

### Privacy, accessibility, equity, and procurement — [Recommendation]
- Hold the FERPA/COPPA floor plus applicable state overlays (§Shared Themes #4); enforce the PII prohibition in every sanctioned workflow (§Shared Themes #5).
- **Govern surveillance-adjacent tools with care.** The same tool category is described approvingly in Utah.md (distress monitoring) and litigated on First and Fourth Amendment grounds in Kansas.md (the Gaggle case). Proctoring, wellness-flagging, and communications monitoring sit in unresolved legal space (§Implications #8).
- Protect AI-supported accommodations from integrity penalties (Rhode Island.md; §Recommended Response — Access and equity).

### Academic support and advising — [Recommendation]
- Equip advisors with the state-variance picture (the Comparison's matrix) so they can read an incoming student's likely priors from jurisdiction and district **[Inference]**.
- Position writing centers and libraries to teach disclosure, citation, and verification — extending the librarian-led media-literacy model Wisconsin.md demonstrates (§Matrix — Wisconsin row).
- Provide a bilingual bridge for Puerto Rico.md students, whose AI vocabulary (SENSE, the five niveles) is Spanish and whose experience was single-platform and supervised (§Recommended Response — Onboarding).
- Train integrity officers in conversation-first process (WA, NC, VT; §Recommended Response — Student support).

### Governance, assessment, and continuous improvement — [Recommendation]
- **Name an accountable owner**, mirroring Maryland.md's required AI coordinator and the coordinator roles Missouri.md and Ohio.md recommend (§Recommended Response — Governance).
- **Take the offered seats**: Ohio.md and Wyoming.md invite postsecondary representatives onto district AI workgroups — a direct channel to feeder-district expectations (§Recommended Response — Governance).
- **Self-assess annually** with an adapted maturity rubric: Michigan.md's three-stage, eight-domain instrument transfers to institutions by substitution, per its own state file (§Implications #6); Rhode Island.md and North Dakota.md supply getting-started and procurement checklists.
- **Track the moving parts**: pending statutes (NJ, IA, SC, NY, WV; Virginia's unverified enacted text) and living documents (NC, WI) can change entry assumptions between admission cycles (§Evidence Gaps #5-7).

## AI-Enabled Teaching and Assessment Strategies

A design library of patterns the K-12 corpus has already field-built. **[Recommendation throughout; each grounded as cited]**

1. **Assignment-type × level planning grid.** Washington.md pairs its five-level scale with an educator matrix keyed to real assignment types; adapt that grid to course design so every assessment type has a considered level (§Unique State Approaches).
2. **Leveled sequencing across the term.** Start at Level 0-1, progress to 3-4 as students demonstrate disclosure and verification (North Carolina.md; §Recommended Response — Deeper learning).
3. **Process-artifact assessment.** Grade the thinking trail — outlines, drafts, chat links — not just the product (NC, OK, WY; §Recommended Response — Assessment).
4. **Personal-context and original-data tasks.** Assignments demanding personal context, original argument, or self-collected data resist wholesale delegation (UT, WV; §Shared Themes #7).
5. **Reasoning-visible design.** Require "visible evidence of student thinking" and weight reasoning, analysis, reflection, and process over recall (Maryland.md; §Shared Themes #7).
6. **In-person performance checkpoints.** Periodic unaided demonstrations calibrate what the process artifacts claim (Rhode Island.md; §Shared Themes #7).
7. **Paradigm-based activity design.** Kentucky.md's three paradigms — AI-directed, AI-supported, AI-empowered — map activity design to how much cognitive agency stays with the learner (§Implications #6).
8. **Supervised co-creation capstones.** Reserve the scale's top tier for capstone work where full AI collaboration is disclosed, cited, and defended — the structure Louisiana.md's AI-Empowered tier and the scale states' top levels legitimize (§Matrix; §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 2).
9. **Delegation triage.** Use Georgia.md's high-stakes versus non-high-stakes table to decide what a course never delegates to AI: subjective grading, evaluations, individualized decisions (§Implications #6).
10. **Adoption decisions by checklist.** Colorado.md's "If and How Checklist" structures whether and how a given AI use enters a course at all (§Implications #6).
11. **Interrogation-prompt routines.** Build New Mexico.md's interrogation prompts into recurring class practice for questioning AI output (§Recommended Response — Faculty development).
12. **Unit-level literacy arc.** Nevada.md's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow cycle doubles as a within-course arc for introducing any AI-permitted workflow (§Recommended Response — Onboarding).

## Preventing Cognitive Offloading

The corpus's most valuable export. Higher education does not need to invent this rationale; it needs to adopt and extend it. **[Synthesis + Recommendation]**

**Adopt the frame.** Arizona.md's formulation — "not all cognitive offloading is harmful"; the governing question is "not whether students offload thinking, but which thinking they offload and why" — is the most defensible in the corpus because it survives contact with legitimate accessibility and efficiency uses (§Cognitive-Offloading Strategies, Tier 1; §Recommended Response — Deeper learning).

**Decide with the two-question test.** Before permitting AI on a task: what skill should students develop here, and will AI help them practice it or perform it for them (Vermont.md; Tier 1).

**Explain the why to students.** Use the taught rationales students may already recognize: the calculator analogy (Vermont.md) and the repetition argument — bypassing "the repetition necessary to build foundational skills and cognitive capacity" (Minnesota.md) (Tier 1).

**Protect struggle structurally, not rhetorically:**
- A genuine AI-free floor on the course scale (every scale state; Tier 2).
- Productive friction inside AI-permitted tasks: microtask design where students explain their reasoning (Arizona.md; Tier 1).
- Deliberate scaffold-fading across the term (Mississippi.md's "gradually reducing students' reliance"; North Carolina.md's level progression; Tier 4 and §Recommended Response).
- In-person, unaided checkpoints (Rhode Island.md; §Shared Themes #7).

**Make verification a standing obligation.** Every AI output is reviewed before use, and the human remains responsible: EVERY's closing step (AK, NC), Missouri.md's and Montana.md's review requirements (§Recommended Response — Deeper learning).

**Empower refusal.** Faculty may prohibit AI on specific tasks and say why; Vermont.md states this authority for teachers explicitly (Tier 1). **[K-12 Evidence + Recommendation]**

**Guard the faculty side too.** Offloading is not only a student risk: Maryland.md warns against educators over-trusting automated recommendations; Utah.md extends the concern to agentic systems that offload "decision making and/or cognitive effort" (Tiers 1, 4).

**Train the trainers.** Pennsylvania.md already assesses teacher candidates on analyzing "how cognitive offloading to AI systems influences attention, memory, and problem-solving" — a ready module spec for faculty development (Tier 1).

## Student Onboarding Recommendations

**[Recommendation throughout]**

1. **Diagnose, never assume.** Administer a short AI-literacy diagnostic at entry, built on instruments students may partially recognize: New Mexico.md's three-part definition (understand, evaluate, apply thoughtfully) or Nevada.md's Understand/Apply/Integrate/Grow (Comparison §Recommended Response — Onboarding).
2. **Screen the two tails.** Identify South Carolina.md-style CTE completers with genuine technical AI/ML skill for advanced placement, and absence-state students (NE, SD, TX and peers) for the full common floor (§Recommended Response — Onboarding).
3. **Deliver one common-floor module for all entrants**, covering: the institution's permission scale and AI-free rationale; disclosure and citation mechanics; the PII rule; the detector position; the suspicion-and-appeal process; and the approved-tool list with parity guarantee. Each element maps to a taught K-12 practice (§Shared Themes #5-8; §Recommended Response) — for many students this is reinforcement; for absence-state students it is first instruction (§Readiness Framework above).
4. **Build targeted bridges:** a bilingual vocabulary bridge for Puerto Rico students (SENSE and the niveles → the institution's scale); a multi-tool orientation for single-platform and approved-tools-only students (PR; TN districts); and no-penalty catch-up for students whose access was limited structurally (Montana.md's rural variance; free-tier gaps in ND/MS) (§Expected Graduate AI Readiness; §Recommended Response — Onboarding).
5. **Respect the gating states' design.** Younger cohorts from VT/NC/ND/RI-style regimes had deliberately limited early exposure; treat thin AI histories as policy compliance, not deficiency (§Expected Graduate AI Readiness).
6. **Recheck assumptions by cohort year.** As statutory baselines mature (UT grade 7-8 course now; OK district policies 2027-28; MD standards 2027; AL class of 2032), later cohorts arrive with firmer floors; onboarding content should shrink accordingly (§Major Differences #7) **[Inference]**.

## Instruments Annex: The Scales, Written Out

*This annex closes the gap between "adopt a per-assignment scale" and having one to adopt. Levels are reproduced from the primary-verified state adaptations recorded in the project's per-state analyses. The canonical peer-reviewed source is:*

> **Perkins, M., Furze, L., Roe, J., & MacVaugh, J. (2024). The Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale (AIAS).** *[Complete with journal, volume, and DOI before external release; the author-year-title record is what the state documents themselves print.]*

**New Mexico's AIAS adaptation (verbatim AIAS level names; NM guidance p.19):**
1. **NO AI** — assessment completed entirely without AI, in a controlled environment.
2. **AI PLANNING** — AI for pre-task work (brainstorming, outlining, initial research); final submission shows how the student developed the ideas.
3. **AI COLLABORATION** — AI may help complete the task (drafting, feedback, refinement); the student critically evaluates and modifies AI output.
4. **FULL AI** — AI may complete any element, with the student directing AI and demonstrating critical thinking.
5. **AI EXPLORATION** — AI used creatively; student and instructor co-design the assessment.

**Oklahoma's AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale (0-4; OSDE guidance p.18), with per-level disclosure:**
0. **NO AI Use** — no AI at any point; may require an academic-honesty pledge.
1. **AI-Assisted Idea Generation and Structuring** — brainstorming and structure only; no AI content in the final submission; disclosure statement plus links to AI chats.
2. **AI-Assisted Editing** — AI improves clarity of student-created work; no new AI content; disclosure plus chat links.
3. **AI for Specified Task Completion** — AI completes teacher-specified elements; all AI content cited; chat links submitted.
4. **Full AI Use with Human Oversight** — AI as "co-pilot" throughout, as the educator allows; cited, with chat links.

**Washington's 5-Step Scaffolding Scale (OSPI, with a companion educator matrix by assignment type):**
1. **No AI Assistance** · 2. **AI-Assisted Brainstorming** · 3. **AI-Supported Drafting** · 4. **AI-Collaborative Creation** · 5. **AI as Co-Creator** — each level pairing use rights with citation and transparency duties. *(Puerto Rico teaches this scale verbatim in Spanish, Nivel 1-5.)*

**Wyoming's Student AI Usage Continuum (four named tiers, AIAS-attributed):**
**AI FREE** (any AI use is an integrity violation) → **AI ASSISTED** (specified tasks only; no AI content in final product; disclosure + chat links) → **AI ENHANCED** (interactive use with required human oversight of all AI content) → **AI EMPOWERED** (full integration; student responsible for accuracy, fairness, originality; detailed disclosure).

**Adoption guidance [Recommendation]:** pick one instrument, keep its numbering, and publish it institution-wide; the AIAS-lineage versions (New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, North Carolina) let you cite the peer-reviewed source, and Oklahoma's disclosure column is the most complete if you want chat-link transparency.

## Evidence Gaps and Limitations

**Inherited from the Comparison (apply to every claim above):**
1. **Voluntariness bounds all student-level claims.** Most K-12 guidance is non-binding and district-implemented; no per-district adoption data exists anywhere in the corpus (Comparison §Evidence Gaps #1).
2. **Coverage holes:** five jurisdictions' guidance substance was uncaptured (DE, FL, HI, ME, NY's district guidance); two rest on ledger records rather than read text (NY, TX); four third-party substitute documents are non-retrievable (NH coalition, SD ASBSD, KS KU framework, MT MTSBA); Virginia's two enacted statutes are text-unverified (§Evidence Gaps #2-5). Students from these jurisdictions cannot be profiled beyond the structural facts.
3. **Absence claims are search-bounded**, not certified; **living documents** (NC, WI, MT, WV) may have changed since capture; **detector silence is systematically ambiguous** (§Evidence Gaps #6-8).
4. DC and Puerto Rico are **not states**, and their governance differs in ways that change how "guidance" binds (§Evidence Gaps #10).

**Specific to this translation document:**
5. **Policy ≠ practice ≠ learning.** This document reasons from what policies *say* to what students *may know*. The corpus contains no measured student outcomes; only Idaho.md even directs the creation of AI-literacy assessment, still pending (§Expected Graduate AI Readiness). Every profile claim is therefore an inference from policy exposure, which is why the diagnostic, not the profile, should drive placement. **[Synthesis]**
6. **The cohort-variance thesis is itself an inference** — well-grounded in the corpus's structure, but not an empirical finding (§Expected Graduate AI Readiness [Inference]).
7. **Recommendations are synthesis, not validated interventions.** The faculty and administrator guidelines extend documented K-12 practices into a higher-education setting; none has been evaluated in that setting within this evidence base. **[Synthesis]**
8. **Discipline-specific guidance is absent from the corpus**, so all discipline-level advice here is inference from general-purpose instruments. **[Inference]**
9. Corrections identified during the state pass (Washington's scale reversal, Puerto Rico's level names, Louisiana's single SAMR model, Utah SB 322's failure, and the consumer-protection statutes mis-scoped as school law) are folded into the Comparison but still pending in the project's `VERIFICATION.md` and `data.js` (§Evidence Gaps #11).

## Conclusion

The K-12 record hands higher education a working kit: a peer-reviewed permission vocabulary already taught in at least four states and compatible with three more; an integrity model built on disclosure and process rather than detection, advised by guidance in fourteen states; and a defensible, student-recognizable rationale for keeping some work AI-free, articulated best by Arizona, Vermont, and Minnesota (Comparison §Executive Summary; §Cognitive-Offloading Strategies). **[Synthesis]**

What K-12 could not deliver, and what these guidelines therefore center, is uniformity. Guidance was voluntary, mandates governed adoption rather than content, several statutory floors have no graduates yet, and nine jurisdictions built nothing at all (Comparison §Executive Summary #5-6; §Policy Archetypes H). The institution's job is not to assume a baseline but to *create* one: diagnose at entry, publish one scale with an AI-free floor, guarantee tool parity, run integrity by conversation and appeal, and build faculty capacity where the corpus says the real gap sits — in designing AI-integrated learning, not in policing it (Comparison §Implications #9; §Conclusion). **[Recommendation]**

Faculty and students remain the active agents in this design; AI is the instrument. The K-12 corpus does not tell a university what to decide, but it has already done much of the hard drafting, and it removes the last excuse: the claim that no one has worked out how to govern AI in a classroom. Someone has. Fifty-two someones, unevenly, and their work is citable. **[Synthesis]**
