IL Guidance, not a mandate

They spent four years under a written AI rule. Yours is the first one they cannot find.

Illinois published guidance under Public Act 104-0399 and named no statewide scale. Nothing in that law obliges a university. But your incoming class also sat in classrooms across the other 51 jurisdictions, and seven of them publish a leveled AI permission scale that four attribute to peer-reviewed university research. The case here rests entirely on merits. It does not rest on whether anyone is making you.

How to read this

This is a diagnosis, not a checklist. Each point below names one thing your incoming students were taught to expect, one thing your syllabus or handbook probably does not say, and who the mismatch fails.

Nothing here assumes every student arrived trained. Adoption varies by district and most K-12 guidance is voluntary. The point is that you cannot tell which students those are, and one silence fails the trained and the untrained student in opposite directions.

01

Permission is a property of the assignment, not the course.

What they were taught

Each task carries a posted level, from an AI-free floor up to full disclosed collaboration. The level was taught, not inferred.

What your syllabus probably says

One rule for the whole course, or nothing at all.

Who this fails

The trained student, who reads a blanket rule as less rigorous than their high school.

02

Disclosure has a named format.

What they were taught

A citation format, a disclosure statement, and in two states a retained link to the AI chat itself.

What your syllabus probably says

Cite your sources.

Who this fails

The honest student, who wants to disclose and cannot find out how.

03

If a tool is required, it is provided.

What they were taught

Tool parity. A school that requires a tool supplies it, so the paying student holds no advantage.

What your syllabus probably says

Nothing about who pays for the tool it assumes you have.

Who this fails

The student on the free tier, graded against classmates on the paid one.

04

Some work is AI-free, and the reason is taught.

What they were taught

An explicit rationale for protecting unaided work, including the calculator analogy and the argument from repetition.

What your syllabus probably says

AI is prohibited on this assignment, with no reason given.

Who this fails

Every student, who reads an unexplained ban as distrust rather than as pedagogy.

05

A detector result is not proof, and they were told so.

What they were taught

Seven jurisdictions publish detector skepticism in writing. Maryland calls detectors unreliable and inequitable. Illinois calls a result one data point.

What your syllabus probably says

Nothing, while the detector runs inside your LMS by default.

Who this fails

The multilingual student, who carries the highest false-positive exposure and was never taught to expect it here.

The position to take

Publish your detector stance before a case arises. State that detector output never stands alone as evidence, that concerns begin with a conversation anchored to the posted level of that assignment, and that a student may contest any AI-related finding.

What the evidence supports

Seven jurisdictions publish caution about detectors. Twenty-two are silent, which this project records as a gap rather than as endorsement. No state guidance in the corpus instructs a teacher to treat a detector result as sufficient evidence of misconduct.

Washington and North Carolina go further and describe the response as a conversation. North Carolina names it a teachable moment. That is the practice a scale-state student may arrive expecting, and it may not be the practice your integrity office follows.

Where these come from
Each chip links to that jurisdiction's profile and its recorded primary sources. Detector silence is recorded as a gap rather than a position, because the corpus cannot distinguish a state that considered detectors and declined to endorse them from one that never addressed the question at all.

The objection you will hear first

Newly drafted — needs your approval

"We already have an academic integrity office and the detector is built into the LMS. This is solved."

The detector being available is not a position on what it proves. Your integrity office has a process for adjudicating a charge. It does not have a published standard of evidence for this particular charge, and the difference surfaces the first time a multilingual student is flagged and asks what the threshold was.

Seven state education agencies wrote that standard down for teenagers. A faculty member enforcing an unpublished one is exposed personally, and so is the institution on appeal. Publishing the stance costs a paragraph and removes the improvisation.

06

There is a way to contest the finding.

What they were taught

An appeal path, including for a grade that AI helped produce.

What your syllabus probably says

Appeals exist for grades, and no one has said whether this counts.

Who this fails

The wrongly accused student, who cannot tell which office to go to.
