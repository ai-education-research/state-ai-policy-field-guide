# Is Higher Ed Ready?

*K-12 wrote the rules for AI in the classroom. Universities are still clearing their throats.*

**By Sidaard Gunasekaran**

Picture two students in the same first-year section this fall. One graduated from an Oklahoma district that adopted the state's AI Acceptable Use Rating Scale. Every assignment she submitted in high school carried a posted level, from Level 0, no AI at any point, to Level 4, full use with human oversight. When a level permitted AI, she disclosed how she used it and attached links to her chat transcripts. Her teachers taught her, citing the state's own guidance, that AI detectors produce false positives.

The student beside her graduated in Texas, which has issued no classroom AI guidance at all. Whatever rules he followed came from his district, if his district wrote any.

Their college syllabus says nothing about AI. She reads the silence as a step down in rigor from her high school. He reads it as permission. The same silence fails both of them, and it fails them in opposite directions.

I teach aerospace engineering, and I have spent the past months reading the K-12 artificial intelligence policies of all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico, down to the primary documents. I began with the assumption most of us in higher education carry without examining it. We assume the schools are behind us. On this subject they are ahead, and the distance is uncomfortable.

Seven jurisdictions publish leveled scales that tell a student exactly how much AI a given assignment permits. Four of them print an academic citation for their source, a 2024 peer-reviewed instrument called the AI Assessment Scale. Vermont hands its teachers two questions to ask before permitting AI on any task. What skill should students practice here, and will AI help them practice it or perform it for them? A university could ask those same two questions of any assignment. Most of us never have.

Fourteen states caution educators against relying on AI-detection software, and eight of them cite research or name the tools' bias against multilingual writers. A dean who suspends a student on a detector's verdict should expect that student to quote her own state's guidance back to the hearing board. Would your institution's case survive that moment? What exists in writing, anywhere on your campus, about the tool your integrity office trusts?

Even legislatures moved before most faculty senates. Tennessee required every one of its public universities to adopt an AI policy by July 1, 2025, and boards that failed owed an appearance before a legislative committee. The deadline has passed. I have found no record of anyone checking the compliance. Has your provost?

Now walk to our side of the campus gate. In most universities, AI policy is whatever each instructor decides, and many decide nothing. That sentence is my observation as a professor, not a finding from my dataset, so test it against your own institution. Collect ten current syllabi. Count the distinct AI policies. Count the silences. You already suspect what the numbers will be, and that suspicion is itself worth sitting with. A first-year student taking five courses can face five contradictory regimes in a single semester, most of them unstated. We would never accept that from a school district. We call it academic freedom when we do it ourselves.

So, is higher education ready? Try three smaller questions about your own institution, because the large question is only these three wearing a gown.

When a student in your class uses AI tonight, which document tells her what was permitted? If the answer is that it depends on the instructor, that is not a policy. It is a lottery.

When an integrity case turns on a detector's verdict, where does the accused student appeal, and what has your institution written down about the tool's error rate? Fourteen state education agencies wrote their answer. Most universities have written nothing.

What does an entering student at your institution actually know about AI? Not what we assume from her age. What do we know that she knows? No office on your campus can answer, because no one measures it. The schools cannot answer either. Only Idaho has even directed its education department to build such an assessment. Every institution in this country, at every level, is teaching a population it has never diagnosed. In my field we would call that flying without instruments.

Candor requires two admissions. Most K-12 guidance is voluntary, adopted district by district, so the typical graduate today never met a leveled scale. And the instruments are young, dating from 2024 and 2025, which means students raised on them arrive in force between 2027 and 2029. Read those dates carefully before taking comfort in them. The collision is not behind us, where a postmortem could sort it out. It is ahead of us, on a schedule we did not set. Dual-enrollment students are already living it, sitting in a scaled high-school classroom and an ungoverned college classroom in the same week.

A piece like this is supposed to end with a numbered list of solutions. This one will not. The recommendations exist, mine among many, and any committee that wants them can gather them in an afternoon. Advice is not what we are short of. We are short of the shared conviction that the silence in our syllabi is a decision, one we are making by default, on behalf of students who were taught to expect better.

So I will end where I began, in that first-year section. The student from Oklahoma already knows what Level 0 means. She knows how to disclose her AI use, and she knows why a detector might wrongly accuse her. The question worth carrying into your next faculty meeting is whether her professors know as much.

*Sidaard Gunasekaran is the Hans von Ohain Endowed Chair at the University of Dayton.*

## Notes for editors and fact-checkers

- **Oklahoma scale and chat-link disclosure:** OSDE, *Guidance and Considerations for Using Artificial Intelligence in Oklahoma K-12 Schools*, v2.0 (July 2025), p.18. The scale runs Level 0 through Level 4 and requires links to AI chats at Levels 1-4.
- **Texas absence:** No TEA classroom AI guidance located as of the project's source pass (2026-07-29); corroborated by contemporary reporting.
- **Seven scale jurisdictions / four with printed attribution:** New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, North Carolina (attributed to Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh, 2024, *The Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale*); Washington and Puerto Rico run compatible scales; Louisiana runs a SAMR-aligned tier model. Full taxonomy in `cross-state-ai-policy-comparison.md`.
- **Vermont two-question test:** Vermont AOE, *AI Guidance for Education*, v1.0 (Jan 23, 2026), Cognitive Offloading section.
- **Fourteen-state detector taxonomy (6 discourage, 8 not-sole-evidence, 8 with bias/research rationale, Louisiana the sole recommender):** `cross-state-ai-policy-comparison.md`, §Major Differences #2.
- **Tennessee statute:** Public Chapter No. 550 (2024), Section 1. University policies due July 1, 2025; noncompliant boards appear before the joint government operations committee. The claim that compliance is unrecorded reflects the project corpus, which contains no compliance record.
- **Idaho assessment directive:** SB 1227, §33-7005 (assessment guidelines for student GenAI understanding; content pending).
- **Instrument dates and the 2027-2029 window:** scale instruments date Jan 2024 (NC) through Jul 2025 (OK v2.0); cohort-arrival math is the author's inference, flagged as such in the companion documents.
- **The ten-syllabi test and the per-instructor characterization of higher ed** are the author's professional observation, not findings from the K-12 dataset, and are presented as such in the companion documents.
